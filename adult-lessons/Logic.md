Introduction
─────────────────────────────────

Mathematical logic is the branch of mathematics that takes mathematical reasoning itself as its object of study. Instead of proving theorems about numbers, groups or manifolds, it proves theorems about theorems: what it means for a sentence to be true in a structure, what it means for a sentence to be provable from axioms, how these two notions are related, and where the power of formal reasoning runs out. The subject was created between roughly 1879 and 1936 by Frege, Peano, Russell and Whitehead, Hilbert, Skolem, Gödel, Tarski, Church and Turing, and the results of that period — the completeness theorem, the compactness theorem, the incompleteness theorems, the undecidability of the halting problem — remain the backbone of every graduate course in the field.

The elementary course on logic dealt with statements, the connectives NOT, AND, OR and IF–THEN, quantifiers, the converse and contrapositive, and valid argument forms. Here we treat the same material the way a graduate text does. Formulas become precisely defined strings generated by a grammar; truth becomes a mathematically defined relation between structures and formulas; proof becomes a finite combinatorial object that can itself be studied, counted and encoded as a number. Once both sides are defined rigorously, the central questions of the subject can be stated and answered: is every valid formula provable (yes, by Gödel's completeness theorem), is every true statement of arithmetic provable from a reasonable set of axioms (no, by Gödel's incompleteness theorem), and can validity be decided mechanically (yes for propositional logic, no for first-order logic, by the theorem of Church and Turing).

We assume familiarity with proofs by induction, with countable and uncountable sets at the level of Cantor's diagonal argument, and with basic algebraic structures such as groups and ordered fields. No prior course in logic beyond the elementary one is needed. Proofs are given in full where they fit on a page; for the longer arguments (the incompleteness theorems, Cook–Levin) we give the complete architecture of the proof and prove every step that contains a genuine idea.

In this course, you will:

  1. Define the syntax and semantics of propositional logic precisely and prove unique readability
  2. Prove functional completeness of standard connective sets, classify the incomplete ones, and work with CNF and DNF
  3. Build a natural deduction system and prove that it is sound
  4. Prove the completeness and compactness theorems for propositional logic
  5. Study resolution, Horn formulas, 2-SAT and the satisfiability problem
  6. Define first-order languages, structures and Tarskian satisfaction, and study definability
  7. Prove Gödel's completeness theorem by the Henkin method
  8. Derive the compactness and Löwenheim–Skolem theorems and apply them to nonstandard models
  9. Develop Turing machines, the halting problem and the undecidability of first-order validity
  10. Understand Gödel numbering, the diagonal lemma and both incompleteness theorems, and Tarski's undefinability theorem
  11. State and understand the Cook–Levin theorem and the theory of NP-completeness
  12. Meet Russell's paradox, the axioms of ZFC, ordinals, cardinals and the independence of the continuum hypothesis
  13. Work through 60 practice problems

Notation. We write ¬, ∧, ∨, →, ↔ for the connectives, ⊤ and ⊥ for the constants true and false, ⊨ for semantic consequence and ⊢ for derivability. Sets of formulas are denoted Γ, Δ, Σ, T; formulas are denoted φ, ψ, χ, θ. We write ℕ = {0, 1, 2, …} and identify truth values with 1 (true) and 0 (false).

Lesson 1: Propositional Logic — Syntax and Semantics
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        Formulas are generated from atoms by the connectives; every formula is read in exactly one way.
        A valuation v : At → {0, 1} extends uniquely to all formulas.
        Γ ⊨ φ means: every valuation satisfying all of Γ satisfies φ.

The language

Fix a set At of propositional atoms (or variables), usually At = {p₀, p₁, p₂, …}. The set Form of propositional formulas is the smallest set of finite strings over the alphabet At ∪ {¬, ∧, ∨, →, ↔, (, )} ∪ {⊤, ⊥} such that every atom, ⊤ and ⊥ belong to Form, and whenever φ, ψ ∈ Form, the strings ¬φ, (φ ∧ ψ), (φ ∨ ψ), (φ → ψ) and (φ ↔ ψ) belong to Form. Equivalently, Form is the union of the sets Form₀ ⊆ Form₁ ⊆ … where Form₀ consists of the atoms and constants and Formₙ₊₁ adds all strings obtained from members of Formₙ by one application of a connective. The second description shows that every formula has a finite construction sequence, and it justifies proof by induction on formulas: if a property holds of the atoms and constants and is preserved by each connective, it holds of every formula.

In practice we drop outermost brackets and adopt the conventions that ¬ binds most tightly, then ∧ and ∨, then → and ↔, and that → associates to the right. These are abbreviations only; the official objects are fully bracketed strings.

Unique readability

To define anything by recursion on formulas we need to know that a formula cannot be built in two different ways. This is the content of the unique readability theorem.

For a string s let b(s) be the number of left brackets in s minus the number of right brackets.

Lemma 1.1. (i) Every formula φ has b(φ) = 0 and every initial segment of φ has b ≥ 0. (ii) Every proper nonempty initial segment of a formula that begins with a left bracket has b ≥ 1. (iii) No formula is a proper initial segment of another formula.

Proof. (i) and (ii) are proved together by induction on φ. Atoms and constants contain no brackets. The initial segments of ¬φ are the empty string and ¬t with t an initial segment of φ, so the claims pass from φ to ¬φ. The proper nonempty initial segments of (φ ∗ ψ) are "(" t with t an initial segment of φ, and "(" φ ∗ t′ with t′ an initial segment of ψ (t′ may be all of ψ). By induction these have b = 1 + b(t) ≥ 1 and b = 1 + 0 + b(t′) ≥ 1, and the whole formula has b = 1 + 0 + 0 − 1 = 0. For (iii), suppose the formula ψ is a proper initial segment of the formula φ, and induct on φ. An atom or constant has no proper nonempty initial segment. If φ begins with a left bracket, so does ψ, and then b(ψ) = 0 by (i) but b(ψ) ≥ 1 by (ii), a contradiction. If φ = ¬φ′, then ψ = ¬ψ′ with ψ′ a formula that is a proper initial segment of φ′, contradicting the induction hypothesis. ∎

Theorem 1.2 (Unique readability). Every formula φ is exactly one of: an atom, a constant, ¬ψ for a unique ψ, or (ψ ∗ χ) for a unique binary connective ∗ and unique formulas ψ, χ.

Proof. The first symbol of φ determines which case occurs. If ¬ψ = ¬ψ′, then ψ = ψ′ trivially. Suppose (ψ ∗ χ) = (ψ′ ∗′ χ′). Then ψ and ψ′ are both initial segments of the same string, so one is an initial segment of the other, and by Lemma 1.1(iii) they are equal. Deleting them, the next symbol gives ∗ = ∗′, and what remains, minus the final bracket, gives χ = χ′. ∎

Semantics

A valuation is a function v : At → {0, 1}. By unique readability v extends to a unique function v̄ : Form → {0, 1} satisfying v̄(⊤) = 1, v̄(⊥) = 0, v̄(¬φ) = 1 − v̄(φ), and

        v̄(φ ∧ ψ) = min(v̄(φ), v̄(ψ))          v̄(φ ∨ ψ) = max(v̄(φ), v̄(ψ))
        v̄(φ → ψ) = max(1 − v̄(φ), v̄(ψ))      v̄(φ ↔ ψ) = 1 exactly when v̄(φ) = v̄(ψ)

We write v ⊨ φ when v̄(φ) = 1. A formula is a tautology (⊨ φ) if every valuation satisfies it, satisfiable if some valuation does, and a contradiction if none does. For a set Γ of formulas, Γ ⊨ φ (φ is a semantic consequence of Γ) means that every valuation satisfying every member of Γ also satisfies φ. Formulas φ and ψ are logically equivalent, φ ≡ ψ, if φ ⊨ ψ and ψ ⊨ φ, that is, if φ ↔ ψ is a tautology.

Lemma 1.3 (Coincidence). If v and w agree on the atoms occurring in φ, then v̄(φ) = w̄(φ).

Proof. Induction on φ; the inductive steps use only the values on immediate subformulas. ∎

The coincidence lemma means that a formula in n atoms has only 2ⁿ relevant valuations, which is why truth tables decide tautologyhood. Two elementary but constantly used facts follow directly from the definitions.

Proposition 1.4. (i) Γ ⊨ φ → ψ if and only if Γ ∪ {φ} ⊨ ψ (the semantic deduction theorem). (ii) Γ ⊨ φ if and only if Γ ∪ {¬φ} is unsatisfiable.

Proof. (i) If Γ ⊨ φ → ψ and v satisfies Γ ∪ {φ}, then v̄(φ → ψ) = 1 and v̄(φ) = 1 force v̄(ψ) = 1. Conversely, if Γ ∪ {φ} ⊨ ψ and v satisfies Γ, either v̄(φ) = 0, making φ → ψ true, or v̄(φ) = 1, so v̄(ψ) = 1. (ii) A valuation satisfying Γ ∪ {¬φ} is exactly a valuation satisfying Γ but not φ. ∎

Substitution

If φ is a formula in the atoms p₁, …, pₙ and ψ₁, …, ψₙ are formulas, write φ[ψ₁/p₁, …, ψₙ/pₙ] for the result of simultaneously replacing each pᵢ by ψᵢ. A straightforward induction proves the substitution lemma: v̄(φ[ψ⃗/p⃗]) = w̄(φ), where w is the valuation with w(pᵢ) = v̄(ψᵢ). Consequently every substitution instance of a tautology is a tautology, and replacing a subformula by a logically equivalent one preserves logical equivalence. These two facts license all the algebraic manipulation of formulas that follows.

Lesson 2: Truth Functions, Functional Completeness and Normal Forms
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        There are 2^(2ⁿ) Boolean functions of n variables.
        {¬, ∧, ∨} is functionally complete; so is {↑} (NAND) alone.
        Post: a set is complete iff it escapes each of five maximal clones.

Boolean functions

An n-ary Boolean function is a map F : {0, 1}ⁿ → {0, 1}. Since {0, 1}ⁿ has 2ⁿ elements and each can be sent to 0 or 1, there are exactly 2^(2ⁿ) such functions: 4 unary, 16 binary and 256 ternary ones. Every formula φ(p₁, …, pₙ) defines a Boolean function F_φ by F_φ(a₁, …, aₙ) = v̄(φ) where v(pᵢ) = aᵢ, and φ ≡ ψ exactly when F_φ = F_ψ. Hence there are, up to logical equivalence, exactly 2^(2ⁿ) formulas in n given atoms. A set S of connectives is functionally complete if every Boolean function of every arity n ≥ 1 is F_φ for some formula φ built from atoms using only connectives from S.

Theorem 2.1. {¬, ∧, ∨} is functionally complete.

Proof. Let F be n-ary. If F is identically 0, it is defined by p₁ ∧ ¬p₁. Otherwise, for each a = (a₁, …, aₙ) with F(a) = 1 form the minterm μ_a = ℓ₁ ∧ … ∧ ℓₙ where ℓᵢ = pᵢ if aᵢ = 1 and ℓᵢ = ¬pᵢ if aᵢ = 0. Then μ_a is true at exactly the single point a. The disjunction of all such minterms is true exactly at the points where F is 1. ∎

The formula constructed is the full disjunctive normal form of F. A formula is in disjunctive normal form (DNF) if it is a disjunction of conjunctions of literals (atoms or negated atoms), and in conjunctive normal form (CNF) if it is a conjunction of disjunctions of literals, called clauses. Applying the theorem to ¬F and using De Morgan's laws, ¬(α ∨ β) ≡ ¬α ∧ ¬β and ¬(α ∧ β) ≡ ¬α ∨ ¬β, shows that every formula is also equivalent to a CNF: one clause for each falsifying assignment, the clause being false exactly at that assignment.

Corollary 2.2. {¬, ∧}, {¬, ∨}, {¬, →} and {→, ⊥} are functionally complete, and so are {↑} and {↓}, where p ↑ q = ¬(p ∧ q) (NAND, the Sheffer stroke) and p ↓ q = ¬(p ∨ q) (NOR).

Proof. It suffices to define the members of a known complete set. α ∨ β ≡ ¬(¬α ∧ ¬β); α ∧ β ≡ ¬(¬α ∨ ¬β); α ∨ β ≡ ¬α → β; ¬α ≡ α → ⊥. For NAND, ¬α ≡ α ↑ α and α ∧ β ≡ (α ↑ β) ↑ (α ↑ β). For NOR, ¬α ≡ α ↓ α and α ∨ β ≡ (α ↓ β) ↓ (α ↓ β). ∎

Sheffer showed in 1913 that ↑ alone suffices, though Peirce had observed it around 1880; it is a classical theorem that ↑ and ↓ are the only binary connectives that are functionally complete by themselves. This is why NAND gates are the universal building block of digital circuits.

Proving incompleteness: invariants

To show a set is not complete, find a property shared by every function definable from it but not by all Boolean functions. A function F preserves 0 if F(0, …, 0) = 0. Every formula built from atoms using only ∧ and ∨ defines a function preserving 0 and preserving 1, and moreover a monotone function (a ≤ b coordinatewise implies F(a) ≤ F(b)). Since ¬ is not monotone, {∧, ∨} is not complete. Similarly, every formula using only → defines a function preserving 1, since 1 → 1 = 1; hence ¬ is not definable from → alone and {→} is incomplete. Every formula built from ¬ and ↔ defines an affine function, one of the form a₀ ⊕ a₁x₁ ⊕ … ⊕ aₙxₙ over the field 𝔽₂ (here ⊕ is addition mod 2, and α ↔ β corresponds to α ⊕ β ⊕ 1); since ∧ is not affine, {¬, ↔} is incomplete.

Theorem 2.3 (Post, 1941). A set S of Boolean functions is functionally complete if and only if, for each of the following five classes, S contains a function not in that class: the 0-preserving functions, the 1-preserving functions, the monotone functions, the affine functions, and the self-dual functions (those with F(¬a₁, …, ¬aₙ) = ¬F(a₁, …, aₙ)).

Each of the five classes is closed under composition and contains the projections, so the "only if" direction follows from the invariance argument above. The "if" direction is a pleasant exercise (Problem 49): from a non-0-preserving, a non-1-preserving and a non-self-dual function one obtains ¬ and the constants; then a non-monotone function and a non-affine function yield ∧. The five classes are exactly the maximal clones in Post's lattice of all clones on {0, 1}, which Post classified completely — a countably infinite lattice.

The cost of normal forms

Normal forms can be exponentially long. The parity function x₁ ⊕ … ⊕ xₙ is 1 at exactly 2ⁿ⁻¹ points, and no conjunction of fewer than n literals can be true only at points of odd parity (fixing fewer than n coordinates leaves a subcube containing both parities). So every DNF of parity has at least 2ⁿ⁻¹ terms, and by symmetry every CNF has at least 2ⁿ⁻¹ clauses. For satisfiability purposes this blow-up can be avoided by the Tseitin transformation: introduce a new atom for each subformula and assert, by a few clauses, that it is equivalent to its definition. The result is a CNF of size linear in the original formula that is equisatisfiable with it (satisfiable if and only if the original is), though not logically equivalent to it.

Lesson 3: Natural Deduction and Soundness
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        A derivation is a finite tree of formulas built by introduction and elimination rules.
        Γ ⊢ φ: there is a derivation of φ all of whose open assumptions lie in Γ.
        Soundness: Γ ⊢ φ implies Γ ⊨ φ.

Why a proof calculus

Semantic consequence quantifies over all valuations. For propositional logic that is a finite check, but for first-order logic it quantifies over all structures of every cardinality, a genuinely infinitary notion. A proof calculus replaces it by a syntactic notion: finite objects, checkable by a mechanical procedure. The calculus we use is Gentzen's natural deduction (1934), which models the way mathematicians actually argue: we make assumptions, reason from them, and discharge them.

The rules

A derivation is a finite tree whose nodes are labelled with formulas, whose leaves are assumptions, and whose inner nodes are justified by rules. Some rules discharge assumptions, written [φ]; a discharged assumption is no longer an open hypothesis of the derivation. Writing the premises before the slash and the conclusion after it, the rules are:

        ∧I:  φ, ψ / φ ∧ ψ                    ∧E:  φ ∧ ψ / φ      and   φ ∧ ψ / ψ
        ∨I:  φ / φ ∨ ψ    and   ψ / φ ∨ ψ    ∨E:  φ ∨ ψ,  [φ]…χ,  [ψ]…χ / χ
        →I:  [φ]…ψ / φ → ψ                   →E:  φ, φ → ψ / ψ   (modus ponens)
        ¬I:  [φ]…⊥ / ¬φ                      ¬E:  φ, ¬φ / ⊥
        ⊥E:  ⊥ / φ   (ex falso)              RAA: [¬φ]…⊥ / φ   (reductio ad absurdum)

Here [φ]…ψ means a subderivation of ψ in which φ may be used as an assumption, and the rule discharges φ. We write Γ ⊢ φ if there is a derivation with conclusion φ whose open (undischarged) assumptions all belong to Γ. The biconditional is treated as an abbreviation of (φ → ψ) ∧ (ψ → φ).

Dropping RAA gives intuitionistic logic, in which the law of excluded middle φ ∨ ¬φ and double negation elimination ¬¬φ → φ are not derivable. Dropping ⊥E as well gives minimal logic. Classical logic is our default.

A worked derivation

We derive the excluded middle ⊢ φ ∨ ¬φ. Assume ¬(φ ∨ ¬φ) (assumption 1). Assume φ (assumption 2). By ∨I, φ ∨ ¬φ; with assumption 1 and ¬E, ⊥. By ¬I discharging assumption 2, ¬φ. By ∨I, φ ∨ ¬φ; with assumption 1 and ¬E, ⊥. By RAA discharging assumption 1, φ ∨ ¬φ. All assumptions are discharged, so the conclusion is derivable from the empty set.

Basic properties of ⊢

The following are immediate from the definition: monotonicity (Γ ⊢ φ and Γ ⊆ Δ imply Δ ⊢ φ); finiteness (if Γ ⊢ φ, then Γ₀ ⊢ φ for some finite Γ₀ ⊆ Γ, because a derivation is finite); cut or transitivity (if Γ ⊢ φ and Γ ∪ {φ} ⊢ ψ, then Γ ⊢ ψ, by grafting); and the syntactic deduction theorem (Γ ⊢ φ → ψ if and only if Γ ∪ {φ} ⊢ ψ, by →I and →E). A set Γ is consistent if Γ ⊬ ⊥. By RAA and ¬I, Γ ⊢ φ if and only if Γ ∪ {¬φ} is inconsistent, and Γ ⊬ φ if and only if Γ ∪ {¬φ} is consistent.

Theorem 3.1 (Soundness). If Γ ⊢ φ, then Γ ⊨ φ.

Proof. We prove by induction on derivations D the statement: if D has conclusion φ and its open assumptions form the set Δ, then Δ ⊨ φ. Since Δ ⊆ Γ, this gives Γ ⊨ φ. A one-node derivation is an assumption φ with Δ = {φ}, and {φ} ⊨ φ. For the inductive step we check each rule; we do the three that discharge.

→I: D ends with φ → ψ, obtained from a derivation of ψ with open assumptions Δ′ ⊆ Δ ∪ {φ}. By induction Δ ∪ {φ} ⊨ ψ, and Proposition 1.4 gives Δ ⊨ φ → ψ.

∨E: from derivations of φ ∨ ψ (assumptions in Δ), of χ (in Δ ∪ {φ}) and of χ (in Δ ∪ {ψ}). If v satisfies Δ, then v satisfies φ or ψ; in either case the corresponding induction hypothesis gives v ⊨ χ.

RAA: from a derivation of ⊥ with assumptions in Δ ∪ {¬φ}. By induction Δ ∪ {¬φ} ⊨ ⊥, so Δ ∪ {¬φ} is unsatisfiable, so Δ ⊨ φ by Proposition 1.4.

The non-discharging rules are immediate; for example →E is sound because v ⊨ φ and v ⊨ φ → ψ imply v ⊨ ψ. ∎

Corollary 3.2. Every satisfiable set is consistent. In particular ⊬ ⊥, and the calculus does not derive p ∨ q → p, since that formula is not a tautology.

Hilbert systems and sequent calculus

Natural deduction is one of three standard formats. A Hilbert system has many axiom schemes and a single rule, modus ponens; for instance the schemes φ → (ψ → φ), (φ → (ψ → χ)) → ((φ → ψ) → (φ → χ)) and (¬ψ → ¬φ) → (φ → ψ) suffice for the {¬, →} fragment. Gentzen's sequent calculus manipulates sequents Γ ⇒ Δ and enjoys the cut-elimination theorem (Gentzen's Hauptsatz): every derivable sequent has a derivation in which every formula is a subformula of the end sequent. All three formats derive exactly the same consequences, and the soundness and completeness theorems hold for each.

Lesson 4: Completeness and Compactness for Propositional Logic
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        Completeness: Γ ⊨ φ implies Γ ⊢ φ.
        Every consistent set extends to a maximal consistent set, which defines a valuation.
        Compactness: Γ is satisfiable iff every finite subset of Γ is satisfiable.

Statement and strategy

Theorem 4.1 (Completeness; for Γ = ∅ due to Post, 1921). If Γ ⊨ φ, then Γ ⊢ φ.

By the remarks in Lesson 3, Γ ⊬ φ implies that Γ ∪ {¬φ} is consistent, and Γ ⊭ φ is equivalent to Γ ∪ {¬φ} being satisfiable. So completeness is equivalent to the model existence lemma: every consistent set of formulas is satisfiable. The proof extends the consistent set to a set so large that it decides every formula, and then reads a valuation off it. This is the propositional skeleton of Henkin's proof of Gödel's theorem in Lesson 8.

Maximal consistent sets

A set Γ is maximal consistent if it is consistent and no proper superset is consistent.

Lemma 4.2. Let Γ be maximal consistent. Then (i) Γ is deductively closed: Γ ⊢ φ implies φ ∈ Γ; (ii) for every φ, exactly one of φ and ¬φ is in Γ; (iii) φ ∧ ψ ∈ Γ iff φ ∈ Γ and ψ ∈ Γ; φ ∨ ψ ∈ Γ iff φ ∈ Γ or ψ ∈ Γ; φ → ψ ∈ Γ iff φ ∉ Γ or ψ ∈ Γ.

Proof. (i) If Γ ⊢ φ, then Γ ∪ {φ} is consistent (a derivation of ⊥ from it could be combined with the derivation of φ by cut), so by maximality φ ∈ Γ. (ii) Not both, since φ, ¬φ ⊢ ⊥. If φ ∉ Γ, then Γ ∪ {φ} is inconsistent, so Γ ⊢ ¬φ by ¬I, and ¬φ ∈ Γ by (i). (iii) We do ∨. If φ ∈ Γ then Γ ⊢ φ ∨ ψ by ∨I, so φ ∨ ψ ∈ Γ. Conversely, if φ ∨ ψ ∈ Γ but φ, ψ ∉ Γ, then ¬φ, ¬ψ ∈ Γ by (ii), and from φ ∨ ψ, ¬φ, ¬ψ one derives ⊥ by ∨E, contradicting consistency. The other cases are similar. ∎

Lemma 4.3 (Lindenbaum). Every consistent set Γ is contained in a maximal consistent set.

Proof. If At is countable, Form is countable; enumerate it as φ₀, φ₁, φ₂, …. Put Γ₀ = Γ and let Γₙ₊₁ = Γₙ ∪ {φₙ} if this is consistent and Γₙ₊₁ = Γₙ ∪ {¬φₙ} otherwise. Each Γₙ is consistent: if both Γₙ ∪ {φₙ} and Γₙ ∪ {¬φₙ} were inconsistent, then Γₙ ⊢ ¬φₙ and Γₙ ⊢ ¬¬φₙ, so Γₙ would be inconsistent. Let Γ* = ⋃ₙ Γₙ. A derivation of ⊥ from Γ* uses finitely many assumptions, all lying in some Γₙ, so Γ* is consistent; and Γ* contains φ or ¬φ for every φ, so it is maximal. For uncountable At apply Zorn's lemma to the family of consistent supersets of Γ ordered by inclusion: the union of a chain is consistent by the same finiteness argument. ∎

Proof of Theorem 4.1. Let Γ be consistent and extend it to a maximal consistent Γ*. Define v(p) = 1 iff p ∈ Γ*. By induction on formulas, using Lemma 4.2, v̄(φ) = 1 iff φ ∈ Γ*. The atomic case is the definition; for example, v̄(φ ∨ ψ) = 1 iff v̄(φ) = 1 or v̄(ψ) = 1 iff φ ∈ Γ* or ψ ∈ Γ* iff φ ∨ ψ ∈ Γ*. Hence v satisfies Γ* ⊇ Γ. ∎

Compactness

Theorem 4.4 (Compactness). A set Γ of propositional formulas is satisfiable if and only if every finite subset is satisfiable. Equivalently, if Γ ⊨ φ then Γ₀ ⊨ φ for some finite Γ₀ ⊆ Γ.

Proof. If every finite subset of Γ is satisfiable, then by soundness every finite subset is consistent. Since derivations are finite, Γ is consistent, and by completeness Γ is satisfiable. ∎

There is also a purely semantic proof: the space {0, 1}^At of valuations is a compact Hausdorff space by Tychonoff's theorem, each set Mod(φ) of valuations satisfying φ is closed (indeed clopen, since φ involves finitely many atoms), and the finite intersection property gives a point in the intersection of the sets Mod(φ) for φ ∈ Γ. This explains the name.

Applications

Compactness turns finite combinatorics into infinite combinatorics. Three classical examples:

  1. De Bruijn–Erdős (1951). An infinite graph G is k-colourable iff every finite subgraph is. Take atoms c(v, i) (vertex v gets colour i) for v ∈ V(G) and 1 ≤ i ≤ k, with formulas saying that each vertex gets at least one colour, at most one colour, and adjacent vertices get different colours. A finite subset mentions finitely many vertices and is satisfied by a colouring of the induced finite subgraph.
  2. König's lemma. Every finitely branching infinite tree has an infinite branch.
  3. Every partial order extends to a linear order. Take atoms r(a, b) for "a precedes b" and the axioms of linear order together with r(a, b) whenever a < b; every finite subset is satisfiable by the finite case (topological sorting).

In each case the finite version is an elementary combinatorial fact and compactness delivers the infinite version, at the cost of a weak choice principle: for uncountable At, the compactness theorem is equivalent over ZF to the Boolean prime ideal theorem, which is strictly weaker than the axiom of choice.

Lesson 5: Resolution, Horn Formulas and the Satisfiability Problem
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        Resolution: from clauses C ∪ {p} and D ∪ {¬p}, infer C ∪ D.
        A CNF is unsatisfiable iff the empty clause □ is derivable by resolution.
        Horn-SAT and 2-SAT are solvable in linear time; 3-SAT is NP-complete.

Clauses as sets

A literal is an atom p or its negation ¬p; the complement of a literal ℓ is written ℓ̄. A clause is a finite set of literals, read as their disjunction; the empty clause □ is unsatisfiable. A CNF formula is a finite set of clauses, read as their conjunction. The satisfiability problem SAT asks, given a CNF, whether some valuation satisfies it. By the Tseitin transformation of Lesson 2 this is no less general than satisfiability of arbitrary formulas.

The resolution rule

Given clauses C₁ containing a literal ℓ and C₂ containing ℓ̄, their resolvent on ℓ is (C₁ − {ℓ}) ∪ (C₂ − {ℓ̄}). A resolution refutation of a clause set S is a finite sequence of clauses, each in S or a resolvent of two earlier ones, ending in □. Resolution was introduced by Davis and Putnam (1960) and, for first-order logic, by J. A. Robinson (1965), and it underlies modern automated theorem proving.

Theorem 5.1 (Soundness and refutation completeness of resolution). A finite clause set S is unsatisfiable if and only if □ can be derived from S by resolution.

Proof. Soundness: if v satisfies C₁ and C₂, then either v ⊨ ℓ, in which case v must satisfy some literal of C₂ other than ℓ̄, or v ⊨ ℓ̄, in which case v satisfies some literal of C₁ other than ℓ. Either way v satisfies the resolvent, so □ is never derived from a satisfiable set.

Completeness, by induction on the number n of atoms occurring in S. If n = 0, S is either empty (satisfiable) or {□}. Suppose S is unsatisfiable and p occurs in S. Let S_p be obtained by deleting every clause containing p and deleting ¬p from the remaining clauses; this is S simplified under p = 1. Similarly S_¬p is S simplified under p = 0. Both are unsatisfiable and mention fewer atoms, so each has a resolution refutation. Restore ¬p to the clauses of S_p from which it was deleted, and replay the refutation: every step remains a valid resolution step, and it now ends either in □ or in {¬p}. Similarly the refutation of S_¬p becomes a derivation of □ or of {p}. If either ends in □ we are done; otherwise one more resolution of {p} with {¬p} gives □. ∎

Example. S = {{p, q}, {¬p, q}, {p, ¬q}, {¬p, ¬q}}. Resolving the first two on p gives {q}; the last two give {¬q}; resolving these gives □. So S is unsatisfiable, as is obvious from its four clauses excluding all four assignments of p, q.

Resolution is complete but can be very slow: Haken proved in 1985 that the pigeonhole principle PHPⁿ⁺¹ₙ, which says that n + 1 pigeons cannot sit in n holes injectively, has only resolution refutations of exponential size, 2^Ω(n). Proof complexity studies such lower bounds for stronger systems; a superpolynomial lower bound for every proof system would imply NP ≠ coNP and hence P ≠ NP (Cook and Reckhow, 1979).

Horn formulas

A Horn clause has at most one positive literal. Horn clauses are definite clauses {¬q₁, …, ¬qₖ, p}, read as rules q₁ ∧ … ∧ qₖ → p (with k = 0 a fact p), and goal clauses {¬q₁, …, ¬qₖ}.

Theorem 5.2. Satisfiability of a set of Horn clauses is decidable in polynomial time (indeed in linear time, by Dowling and Gallier 1984). Moreover a satisfiable Horn set has a least model.

Proof. Marking algorithm: start with nothing marked. Repeatedly, if some definite clause q₁ ∧ … ∧ qₖ → p has all qᵢ marked and p unmarked, mark p. When no rule applies, let v make exactly the marked atoms true. Every definite clause is then satisfied. Every atom that gets marked is true in every model, by induction on the stage at which it is marked. If some goal clause has all its atoms marked, the set is unsatisfiable, because those atoms are true in every model; otherwise v satisfies all goal clauses too, and v is the least model. Each step adds a mark, so there are at most n rounds. ∎

The least-model property is the semantic foundation of logic programming (Prolog) and of Datalog.

The 2-SAT problem

Theorem 5.3 (Aspvall, Plass and Tarjan 1979). Satisfiability of CNFs with at most two literals per clause is decidable in linear time.

Proof sketch. Form the implication graph with a vertex for each literal and, for each clause {ℓ₁, ℓ₂}, the edges ℓ̄₁ → ℓ₂ and ℓ̄₂ → ℓ₁. A valuation satisfies the CNF iff no edge goes from a true literal to a false one. The CNF is unsatisfiable iff some atom p and ¬p lie in the same strongly connected component: in that case p forces ¬p and ¬p forces p. Conversely, if no such component exists, process the components in reverse topological order, and whenever a component whose literals are still unassigned is reached, make all of its literals true and their complements false; one checks that no edge then leads from a true literal to a false one. Strongly connected components can be found in linear time by Tarjan's algorithm. ∎

By contrast, 3-SAT is NP-complete (Lesson 12). The dividing line between 2 and 3 literals per clause is the first and most famous instance of the phenomenon that tiny changes in a problem can move it from easy to apparently intractable. Schaefer's dichotomy theorem (1978) shows that every Boolean constraint satisfaction problem is either in P (it falls into one of six tractable classes, which include Horn, dual-Horn, 2-SAT and affine) or is NP-complete.

Lesson 6: First-Order Languages, Structures and Satisfaction
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        A signature supplies constant, function and relation symbols, each with an arity.
        An L-structure 𝔄 interprets them on a nonempty domain A.
        Tarski's definition: 𝔄 ⊨ φ[s] by recursion on φ, with s an assignment of variables.

Languages

Propositional logic cannot express "every element has an inverse" or "between any two rationals there is another". First-order logic adds individual variables x, y, z, … ranging over the elements of a structure, the quantifiers ∀ and ∃, the equality symbol =, and a signature L (also called a language or vocabulary): a set of constant symbols c, function symbols F each with an arity n ≥ 1, and relation symbols R each with an arity n ≥ 1.

Terms are generated from variables and constants by applying function symbols: if t₁, …, tₙ are terms and F is n-ary, F(t₁, …, tₙ) is a term. Atomic formulas are t₁ = t₂ and R(t₁, …, tₙ). Formulas are generated from atomic formulas by the connectives and by ∀x φ and ∃x φ. Unique readability holds as before.

Examples of signatures: the language of groups L = {·, ⁻¹, e}; the language of ordered rings {+, ·, −, 0, 1, <}; the language of arithmetic L_A = {0, S, +, ·, <}; the language of set theory {∈}; the language of graphs {E}.

Free and bound variables

An occurrence of x in φ is bound if it lies within a subformula ∀x ψ or ∃x ψ, and free otherwise. FV(φ) is the set of variables with a free occurrence. A sentence is a formula with no free variables. In ∀x (x < y) ∧ x = 0 the first two occurrences of x are bound, the last is free, and FV = {x, y}. Writing φ(x₁, …, xₙ) signals that FV(φ) ⊆ {x₁, …, xₙ}.

Structures

An L-structure 𝔄 consists of a nonempty set A (the domain or universe) together with an element c^𝔄 ∈ A for each constant symbol, a function F^𝔄 : Aⁿ → A for each n-ary function symbol and a relation R^𝔄 ⊆ Aⁿ for each n-ary relation symbol. The standard model of arithmetic is 𝔑 = (ℕ, 0, S, +, ·, <). The rational ordered field, every group, every graph and the universe of sets (if it were a set) are structures in their signatures.

Satisfaction

An assignment in 𝔄 is a function s from the variables to A. It extends to terms by s(c) = c^𝔄 and s(F(t₁, …, tₙ)) = F^𝔄(s(t₁), …, s(tₙ)). Write s[x ↦ a] for the assignment that agrees with s except that it sends x to a. Tarski's satisfaction relation 𝔄 ⊨ φ[s] is defined by recursion on φ:

        𝔄 ⊨ (t₁ = t₂)[s]        iff  s(t₁) = s(t₂)
        𝔄 ⊨ R(t₁, …, tₙ)[s]     iff  (s(t₁), …, s(tₙ)) ∈ R^𝔄
        𝔄 ⊨ ¬φ[s]               iff  not 𝔄 ⊨ φ[s]
        𝔄 ⊨ (φ ∧ ψ)[s]          iff  𝔄 ⊨ φ[s] and 𝔄 ⊨ ψ[s]       (similarly ∨, →, ↔)
        𝔄 ⊨ ∀x φ[s]             iff  𝔄 ⊨ φ[s[x ↦ a]] for every a ∈ A
        𝔄 ⊨ ∃x φ[s]             iff  𝔄 ⊨ φ[s[x ↦ a]] for some a ∈ A

By the coincidence lemma, whether 𝔄 ⊨ φ[s] depends only on s restricted to FV(φ). For a sentence σ we therefore simply write 𝔄 ⊨ σ, read "𝔄 is a model of σ". For a set Σ of sentences, Mod(Σ) is the class of its models, and Σ ⊨ σ means that every model of Σ is a model of σ. A theory is a set of sentences closed under ⊨; Th(𝔄) = {σ : 𝔄 ⊨ σ} is the complete theory of 𝔄.

The definition is deliberately metamathematical: the clause for ∀ uses "for every" in the metalanguage. The satisfaction relation is not definable inside the structure itself, a fact made precise by Tarski's undefinability theorem in Lesson 11.

Substitution

The term t is substitutable for x in φ if no free occurrence of x in φ lies within the scope of a quantifier ∀y or ∃y with y occurring in t. Without this proviso substitution changes meaning: in φ = ∃y (y ≠ x), which is true in any structure with at least two elements, substituting y for x gives ∃y (y ≠ y), which is false. When t is substitutable, the substitution lemma holds: 𝔄 ⊨ φ[t/x][s] iff 𝔄 ⊨ φ[s[x ↦ s(t)]]. Bound variables can always be renamed to make a term substitutable.

Homomorphisms and isomorphisms

An isomorphism h : 𝔄 → 𝔅 is a bijection that preserves the interpretations of all symbols: h(c^𝔄) = c^𝔅, h(F^𝔄(a⃗)) = F^𝔅(h(a⃗)), and a⃗ ∈ R^𝔄 iff h(a⃗) ∈ R^𝔅. A routine induction proves the isomorphism lemma: 𝔄 ⊨ φ[s] iff 𝔅 ⊨ φ[h ∘ s] for all φ and s. Hence isomorphic structures satisfy the same sentences; they are elementarily equivalent, 𝔄 ≡ 𝔅. The converse fails badly, as compactness will show: (ℚ, <) ≡ (ℝ, <), and 𝔑 is elementarily equivalent to uncountable structures.

Lesson 7: Definability, Normal Forms and the Proof Calculus for First-Order Logic
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        A set X ⊆ Aⁿ is definable if X = {a⃗ : 𝔄 ⊨ φ[a⃗]} for some formula φ.
        Definable sets are invariant under automorphisms.
        Every formula has a prenex form; every sentence has an equisatisfiable universal Skolem form.

Definable sets

Given an L-structure 𝔄 and a formula φ(x₁, …, xₙ), the set φ(𝔄) = {a⃗ ∈ Aⁿ : 𝔄 ⊨ φ[a⃗]} is the set defined by φ. Definable sets are closed under Boolean operations (by the connectives) and projections (by ∃). Examples: in (ℝ, +, ·, 0, 1) the order is definable by x ≤ y ↔ ∃z (x + z · z = y), because the nonnegative reals are exactly the squares. In 𝔑 the set of primes is defined by 1 < x ∧ ∀y ∀z (y · z = x → y = 1 ∨ z = 1), where 1 abbreviates S(0).

Theorem 7.1. If σ is an automorphism of 𝔄 and X is definable in 𝔄 without parameters, then σ(X) = X.

Proof. By the isomorphism lemma applied to σ : 𝔄 → 𝔄, 𝔄 ⊨ φ[a⃗] iff 𝔄 ⊨ φ[σ(a⃗)]. ∎

This is the main tool for proving non-definability. In (ℤ, +), the map x ↦ −x is an automorphism, so the set ℕ is not definable without parameters, nor is the order <. In (ℝ, <) every translation x ↦ x + c is an automorphism; since translations can move any point to any other, the only parameter-free definable subsets of ℝ are ∅ and ℝ.

Prenex normal form

A formula is in prenex form if it is Q₁x₁ … Qₙxₙ ψ with each Qᵢ a quantifier and ψ quantifier-free. Using the equivalences ¬∀x φ ≡ ∃x ¬φ, ¬∃x φ ≡ ∀x ¬φ, and, when x ∉ FV(ψ), (∀x φ) ∧ ψ ≡ ∀x (φ ∧ ψ), (∃x φ) ∧ ψ ≡ ∃x (φ ∧ ψ) and their analogues for ∨, together with (∀x φ) → ψ ≡ ∃x (φ → ψ) and (∃x φ) → ψ ≡ ∀x (φ → ψ), every formula can be converted to an equivalent prenex formula after renaming bound variables. Note the reversal of the quantifier in the antecedent of an implication; also note that (∀x φ) → ψ ≡ ∃x (φ → ψ) uses that domains are nonempty. Prenex formulas are classified by quantifier alternations: Σₙ formulas begin with ∃ and have n alternating blocks, Πₙ formulas begin with ∀. This hierarchy will reappear in computability theory as the arithmetical hierarchy.

Skolem normal form

Given a prenex sentence ∀x₁ … ∀xₖ ∃y ψ(x⃗, y), add a new k-ary function symbol f and pass to ∀x₁ … ∀xₖ ψ(x⃗, f(x⃗)). Repeating removes all existential quantifiers and yields a universal sentence σ^S in an expanded language.

Theorem 7.2 (Skolem). σ is satisfiable if and only if its Skolem form σ^S is satisfiable. Indeed σ^S ⊨ σ, and every model of σ expands to a model of σ^S.

Proof. If 𝔄 ⊨ ∀x⃗ ψ(x⃗, f(x⃗)), then for each a⃗ the element f^𝔄(a⃗) witnesses ∃y ψ(a⃗, y). Conversely, if 𝔄 ⊨ ∀x⃗ ∃y ψ, use the axiom of choice to pick, for each a⃗, some b with 𝔄 ⊨ ψ[a⃗, b], and interpret f by a⃗ ↦ b. ∎

The proof calculus

Natural deduction for first-order logic adds to the propositional rules the quantifier rules and the equality rules:

        ∀E:  ∀x φ / φ[t/x]                 ∀I:  φ[y/x] / ∀x φ     (y not free in any open assumption, nor in ∀x φ)
        ∃I:  φ[t/x] / ∃x φ                 ∃E:  ∃x φ,  [φ[y/x]]…χ / χ   (y not free in χ, ∃x φ or other open assumptions)
        =I:  t = t                         =E:  s = t,  φ[s/x] / φ[t/x]

with t substitutable. The eigenvariable conditions on ∀I and ∃E are essential: without them one could derive ∃x P(x) ⊢ ∀x P(x). The soundness theorem extends routinely: if Σ ⊢ φ then Σ ⊨ φ. The real content of first-order logic is the converse, Gödel's completeness theorem.

Lesson 8: Gödel's Completeness Theorem
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        Σ ⊨ φ implies Σ ⊢ φ: truth in all models is captured by finite derivations.
        Henkin: add witnesses c_φ with ∃x φ → φ[c_φ/x], extend to a maximal consistent set.
        The model is built from the closed terms, modulo provable equality.

Statement

Theorem 8.1 (Gödel 1929). Let Σ be a set of sentences and φ a sentence of a first-order language L. If Σ ⊨ φ, then Σ ⊢ φ. Equivalently (model existence): every consistent set of sentences has a model.

The equivalence is as in the propositional case: Σ ⊬ φ implies that Σ ∪ {¬φ} is consistent, and a model of Σ ∪ {¬φ} witnesses Σ ⊭ φ. Gödel's original proof (his doctoral dissertation) reduced to prenex sentences and used propositional compactness on a sequence of quantifier-free instances. The proof below is Leon Henkin's (1949), which generalises to uncountable languages and to many other logics. We give it for countable L; for arbitrary L replace the enumerations by transfinite ones or by Zorn's lemma, and the model obtained has cardinality at most |L| + ℵ₀.

Step 1: Henkin witnesses

A set Σ of L-sentences has the witness property if for every L-formula φ(x) with one free variable there is a constant c with ∃x φ(x) → φ(c) in Σ.

Lemma 8.2. Let Σ be consistent and let c be a constant symbol not occurring in Σ or in φ. Then Σ ∪ {∃x φ(x) → φ(c)} is consistent.

Proof. Suppose Σ, ∃x φ(x) → φ(c) ⊢ ⊥. Then Σ ⊢ ¬(∃x φ(x) → φ(c)), hence Σ ⊢ ∃x φ(x) and Σ ⊢ ¬φ(c). Since c does not occur in Σ, a derivation of ¬φ(c) can be turned into a derivation of ¬φ(y) for a fresh variable y by replacing c throughout (constants not in the assumptions behave like eigenvariables), and then ∀I gives Σ ⊢ ∀y ¬φ(y), which with Σ ⊢ ∃x φ(x) yields Σ ⊢ ⊥, a contradiction. ∎

Let L₀ = L, and let Lₙ₊₁ be Lₙ together with a new constant c_φ for each Lₙ-formula φ(x) with one free variable. Put L* = ⋃ Lₙ, and let Σ* be Σ together with all the Henkin axioms ∃x φ(x) → φ(c_φ). Each finite subset of Σ* adds finitely many Henkin axioms to Σ; adding them one at a time, in order of the level n of their constants, preserves consistency by Lemma 8.2. So Σ* is consistent and has the witness property for L*.

Step 2: Lindenbaum

Exactly as in Lemma 4.3, extend Σ* to a maximal consistent set Δ of L*-sentences. Since Δ ⊇ Σ*, it still has the witness property. Maximal consistent sets have the properties of Lemma 4.2, and in addition: ∃x φ(x) ∈ Δ iff φ(c) ∈ Δ for some closed term c (by the witness axioms and ∃I), and ∀x φ(x) ∈ Δ iff φ(t) ∈ Δ for every closed term t (apply the previous clause to ¬φ).

Step 3: the term model

Let T be the set of closed L*-terms. Define t ∼ u iff (t = u) ∈ Δ. By the equality rules and deductive closure, ∼ is an equivalence relation and a congruence: if tᵢ ∼ uᵢ then F(t⃗) ∼ F(u⃗), and R(t⃗) ∈ Δ iff R(u⃗) ∈ Δ. Let A = T/∼, and interpret c^𝔄 = [c], F^𝔄([t₁], …, [tₙ]) = [F(t₁, …, tₙ)] and R^𝔄 = {([t₁], …, [tₙ]) : R(t₁, …, tₙ) ∈ Δ}. These are well defined because ∼ is a congruence.

Lemma 8.3 (Truth lemma). For every L*-sentence ψ, 𝔄 ⊨ ψ iff ψ ∈ Δ.

Proof. By induction on the number of logical symbols in ψ. For atomic sentences this is the definition of 𝔄 (each closed term t denotes [t], by induction on t). The connective steps use Lemma 4.2. For ∃x φ(x): 𝔄 ⊨ ∃x φ(x) iff 𝔄 ⊨ φ[a] for some a ∈ A; every a is [t] for a closed term t, and 𝔄 ⊨ φ[[t]] iff 𝔄 ⊨ φ(t) by the substitution lemma, iff φ(t) ∈ Δ by induction (φ(t) has fewer logical symbols than ∃x φ(x)), iff ∃x φ(x) ∈ Δ by the witness property. The ∀ case is dual. ∎

Finally, by the truth lemma 𝔄 satisfies every member of Δ ⊇ Σ, so the reduct of 𝔄 to the original language L is a model of Σ. This proves Theorem 8.1. ∎

Consequences

The completeness theorem has three immediate consequences of great importance. First, compactness: Σ has a model iff every finite subset does, since consistency is a finitary property. Second, the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem: a consistent set in a countable language has a countable model, since A is a quotient of the countable set T. Third, the set of valid sentences of a computable language is computably enumerable: one can list all derivations mechanically, and a sentence is valid iff it eventually appears as a conclusion. Church and Turing showed in 1936 that this set is not computable (Lesson 10), so completeness is the best possible result: validity is semi-decidable and no more.

The completeness theorem is also exactly where the expressive power of first-order logic is limited. Second-order logic, which allows quantification over all subsets of the domain, can characterise (ℕ, S, 0) up to isomorphism by the induction axiom, but in consequence it has no sound, complete and effective proof system.

Lesson 9: Compactness and the Löwenheim–Skolem Theorems
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        Compactness: a set of first-order sentences with arbitrarily large finite models has an infinite model.
        Downward LS: a countable theory with an infinite model has a countable model.
        Upward LS: a theory with an infinite model has models of every infinite cardinality κ ≥ |L|.

Compactness and its first consequences

Theorem 9.1 (Compactness). A set Σ of first-order sentences has a model iff every finite subset of Σ has a model.

The first application shows that finiteness is not first-order. Let λₙ be the sentence ∃x₁ … ∃xₙ ⋀ xᵢ ≠ xⱼ (the conjunction over all i < j) saying that there are at least n elements.

Corollary 9.2. If Σ has arbitrarily large finite models, it has an infinite model. Consequently the class of finite structures, and the class of finite groups, are not axiomatisable by any set of first-order sentences.

Proof. Every finite subset of Σ ∪ {λₙ : n ≥ 1} mentions only finitely many λₙ and is satisfied by a sufficiently large finite model of Σ. A model of the whole set is infinite. If a set Φ axiomatised the finite structures, Φ would have arbitrarily large finite models and hence an infinite one. ∎

Similarly, connectedness of graphs, being a torsion group, and being a well-order are not first-order axiomatisable. For well-orders: if Φ axiomatised them, add constants c₀, c₁, … and the sentences cₙ₊₁ < cₙ; every finite subset is satisfied in ℕ by interpreting finitely many cₙ as a decreasing finite sequence, and a model of everything is a linear order with an infinite descending sequence.

Nonstandard models of arithmetic

Let Th(𝔑) be the set of sentences true in the standard model 𝔑 = (ℕ, 0, S, +, ·, <). Add a constant c and consider Th(𝔑) ∪ {c > S(S(…S(0)…)) : n ∈ ℕ}, where the n-th sentence has n copies of S. Every finite subset is satisfied in 𝔑 by interpreting c as a large number. So there is a model 𝔐 of Th(𝔑) containing an element greater than every numeral: a nonstandard model of true arithmetic. Skolem constructed one explicitly in 1934.

Such a model looks like this: an initial copy of ℕ, followed by densely ordered copies of ℤ with neither a first nor a last copy. Indeed, if a is nonstandard then so is ⌊a/2⌋ (smaller copy) and 2a (larger copy), and between a and b in different copies lies ⌊(a + b)/2⌋, in a third copy. Since the order type of the copies is a dense linear order without endpoints, a countable nonstandard model has order type ω + ζ·η, where ω is ℕ, ζ is ℤ and η is ℚ. Tennenbaum's theorem (1959) adds that in no countable nonstandard model of Peano arithmetic is either + or · computable.

Nonstandard analysis

The same method applied to Th(ℝ, +, ·, <, every function and relation on ℝ) produces an elementary extension *ℝ containing a positive element ε smaller than every 1/n. Abraham Robinson (1961) showed that the infinitesimal calculus of Leibniz can be developed rigorously inside *ℝ: f is continuous at a iff f(x) − f(a) is infinitesimal whenever x − a is.

The Löwenheim–Skolem theorems

Let 𝔄 ⊆ 𝔅 be L-structures with 𝔄 a substructure of 𝔅. 𝔄 is an elementary substructure, 𝔄 ≼ 𝔅, if for every formula φ(x⃗) and tuple a⃗ from A, 𝔄 ⊨ φ[a⃗] iff 𝔅 ⊨ φ[a⃗].

Lemma 9.3 (Tarski–Vaught test). A substructure 𝔄 ⊆ 𝔅 is elementary iff for every formula φ(x⃗, y) and every a⃗ from A: if 𝔅 ⊨ ∃y φ[a⃗, y], then 𝔅 ⊨ φ[a⃗, b] for some b ∈ A.

Proof. Necessity is clear. For sufficiency, prove 𝔄 ⊨ φ[a⃗] iff 𝔅 ⊨ φ[a⃗] by induction on φ; only the ∃ step needs the hypothesis, which supplies a witness inside A. ∎

Theorem 9.4 (Downward Löwenheim–Skolem; Löwenheim 1915, Skolem 1920). Let 𝔅 be an L-structure and X ⊆ B. Then there is 𝔄 ≼ 𝔅 with X ⊆ A and |A| ≤ |X| + |L| + ℵ₀.

Proof. Choose Skolem functions: for each formula φ(x⃗, y), a function f_φ on B that picks a witness b with 𝔅 ⊨ φ[a⃗, b] whenever one exists. Let A be the closure of X under all f_φ (and the interpretations of the function symbols). A is a union of countably many stages, each of size at most |X| + |L| + ℵ₀, it is a substructure, and it passes the Tarski–Vaught test by construction. ∎

Theorem 9.5 (Upward Löwenheim–Skolem, Tarski–Vaught). If Σ has an infinite model, then for every cardinal κ ≥ |L| + ℵ₀, Σ has a model of cardinality exactly κ.

Proof. Add κ new constants c_α and the sentences c_α ≠ c_β for α ≠ β. Every finite subset is satisfied in the given infinite model, so by compactness (in the form for languages of size κ) there is a model of size at least κ. Apply Theorem 9.4 with X the set of interpretations of the c_α to obtain a model of size exactly κ. ∎

Skolem's paradox

ZFC proves that there are uncountable sets. If ZFC is consistent, then by Theorem 9.4 it has a countable model M. Inside M there is an element r that M believes is the set of real numbers, and M ⊨ "r is uncountable"; yet externally r has only countably many members in M. There is no contradiction: "uncountable" means that no bijection with ω exists, and the bijections that exist externally are simply not elements of M. Uncountability is relative to the model.

Categoricity

A theory T is κ-categorical if it has, up to isomorphism, exactly one model of cardinality κ. By the Löwenheim–Skolem theorems no first-order theory with an infinite model is categorical outright. Examples: the theory DLO of dense linear orders without endpoints is ℵ₀-categorical by Cantor's back-and-forth argument but not 2^ℵ₀-categorical; the theory of algebraically closed fields of a fixed characteristic is κ-categorical for every uncountable κ, since such a field is determined by its transcendence degree, but not ℵ₀-categorical.

Theorem 9.6 (Łoś–Vaught test). If T has no finite models and is κ-categorical for some κ ≥ |L| + ℵ₀, then T is complete.

Proof. If T ⊬ σ and T ⊬ ¬σ, then T ∪ {¬σ} and T ∪ {σ} both have models, necessarily infinite, and by Löwenheim–Skolem both have models of size κ. These are isomorphic by categoricity, yet one satisfies σ and the other ¬σ. ∎

So DLO is complete, which gives (ℚ, <) ≡ (ℝ, <); and ACF₀ is complete, which yields the Lefschetz principle: a first-order sentence in the language of rings is true in ℂ iff it is true in all algebraically closed fields of characteristic 0, iff it is true in algebraically closed fields of characteristic p for all sufficiently large primes p. A celebrated application is Ax's theorem (1968) that every injective polynomial map ℂⁿ → ℂⁿ is surjective, proved by checking it over the algebraic closures of the finite fields 𝔽ₚ, which are unions of finite fields, where injective maps of finite sets are surjective. Morley's categoricity theorem (1965) states that a countable theory categorical in one uncountable cardinal is categorical in all of them; it founded modern model theory.

Lindström's theorem (1969) characterises first-order logic as the strongest logic satisfying both compactness and the downward Löwenheim–Skolem property for countable sentences, which explains the central position of first-order logic.

Lesson 10: Computability — Turing Machines and the Halting Problem
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        A Turing machine is a finite control acting on an unbounded tape; it formalises "algorithm".
        The halting problem K = {e : φₑ(e) halts} is computably enumerable but not computable.
        Church–Turing: validity of first-order sentences is undecidable.

Turing machines

A Turing machine M consists of a finite set Q of states with a start state q₀ and a halting state q_h, a finite tape alphabet Γ containing a blank symbol ␣ and the input alphabet, and a transition function δ : (Q − {q_h}) × Γ → Q × Γ × {L, R}. A configuration records the state, the tape contents (finitely many nonblank cells) and the head position; δ determines a unique successor configuration. M halts on input w if, started in q₀ with w on the tape and the head on its first symbol, it eventually enters q_h. A partial function f : ℕ ⇀ ℕ is computable (Turing computable, partial recursive) if some machine, on input the binary representation of n, halts with f(n) on the tape when f(n) is defined and runs forever otherwise. A set is computable (decidable, recursive) if its characteristic function is computable, and computably enumerable (c.e., recursively enumerable) if it is the domain of a computable partial function, equivalently the range of a computable function or empty.

Turing introduced the machines in 1936 by analysing what a human computer does with pencil and paper. Independently, Church defined the λ-definable functions and Gödel and Kleene the general recursive functions; all three classes were shown to coincide. The Church–Turing thesis asserts that this class is exactly the class of functions computable by any effective procedure. It is not a theorem, since "effective" is informal, but it is universally accepted.

Universality and enumeration

Machines are finite objects and can be coded by natural numbers. Fix such a coding and let φₑ be the partial function computed by the machine with code e (φₑ undefined everywhere if e codes nothing). When the index is a compound expression we use Kleene's notation {e}(n) for φₑ(n).

Theorem 10.1 (Universal machine). There is a single machine U such that U(e, n) ≃ φₑ(n) for all e and n, meaning both sides are undefined or both are defined and equal.

U simply decodes e and simulates the coded machine step by step. This is the theoretical origin of the stored-program computer. A second fundamental fact is Kleene's s-m-n theorem: there is a computable total function s such that {s(e, m)}(n) ≃ φₑ(m, n), which lets one hard-wire parameters into programs.

The halting problem

Theorem 10.2 (Turing 1936). The set K = {e : φₑ(e) is defined} is c.e. but not computable. Hence the halting set H = {(e, n) : φₑ(n) is defined} is not computable.

Proof. K is c.e.: run U(e, e) and accept if it halts. Suppose K were computable. Then the function g(e) = 0 if e ∉ K, undefined if e ∈ K, would be computable: decide whether e ∈ K and, if so, enter an infinite loop. Let g = φ_d. Then d ∈ K iff φ_d(d) is defined iff g(d) is defined iff d ∉ K, a contradiction. ∎

The argument is Cantor's diagonal argument applied to the list φ₀, φ₁, φ₂, … of computable functions. A set A is computable iff both A and its complement are c.e. (Post): run the two enumerations in parallel. So the complement of K is not c.e.

Reductions and Rice's theorem

A many-one reduction from A to B is a total computable f with x ∈ A iff f(x) ∈ B. If A reduces to B and B is computable, so is A. Using the s-m-n theorem one reduces K to many natural problems.

Theorem 10.3 (Rice 1953). Let 𝒞 be a set of partial computable functions that is neither empty nor all of them. Then {e : φₑ ∈ 𝒞} is not computable.

Proof. We may assume the nowhere-defined function ∅ is not in 𝒞 (otherwise use the complement). Fix ψ ∈ 𝒞. By s-m-n there is a computable f with {f(e)}(n) ≃ ψ(n) if φₑ(e) halts and undefined otherwise (first run φₑ(e), then ψ(n)). Then e ∈ K iff the function computed by f(e) is ψ iff f(e) ∈ {e : φₑ ∈ 𝒞}. ∎

So no algorithm decides, for an arbitrary program, whether it computes a total function, whether it ever outputs 7, or whether it is equivalent to a given program. This is the theoretical reason why perfect static analysis of software is impossible.

Undecidability of first-order logic

Theorem 10.4 (Church 1936, Turing 1936). For a suitable finite language L, the set of valid L-sentences is not computable.

Proof idea. Given a machine M and input w, effectively write a sentence σ(M, w) that describes the computation of M: a binary relation symbol for the successor of time steps, predicate symbols Tape_a(t, x) (at time t, cell x holds a), State_q(t) and Head(t, x), and axioms stating that the initial configuration is correct and that each configuration follows from the previous one by δ. Let σ(M, w) be the conjunction of these axioms implies ∃t State_h(t), with h the halting state. If M halts, the axioms force the actual finite computation to be embedded in every model and σ(M, w) is valid; if M does not halt, the standard model of the computation falsifies it. So halting reduces to validity. ∎

Thus Hilbert's Entscheidungsproblem has a negative answer. Validity is c.e. (by completeness) but not computable; satisfiability in the finite (Trakhtenbrot 1950) is c.e. but its complement is not, so validity in finite structures is not even c.e. — the completeness theorem fails for finite model theory. By contrast, many fragments are decidable: monadic first-order logic, Presburger arithmetic Th(ℕ, +), and the theory of real closed fields Th(ℝ, +, ·, <) (Tarski).

Other undecidable problems

The same method yields undecidability in ordinary mathematics: the word problem for finitely presented groups (Novikov, Boone, 1950s); Hilbert's tenth problem, whether a polynomial equation with integer coefficients has an integer solution (Matiyasevich 1970, building on Davis, Putnam and Robinson: every c.e. set is Diophantine); the tiling problem for Wang tiles (Berger 1966); and the mortality problem for 3 × 3 integer matrices (Paterson 1970).

Lesson 11: Gödel's Incompleteness Theorems
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        Syntax can be coded by numbers, and provability becomes a Σ₁ property of numbers.
        Diagonal lemma: for every φ(x) there is σ with T ⊢ σ ↔ φ(⌜σ⌝).
        G1: a consistent, c.e. theory containing Q is incomplete. G2: it does not prove Con(T).

Arithmetic and its axioms

Work in the language L_A = {0, S, +, ·, <}. Write n̄ for the numeral S(S(…S(0)…)) with n copies of S. Robinson arithmetic Q (1950) consists of the seven axioms

        S(x) ≠ 0                        S(x) = S(y) → x = y
        x ≠ 0 → ∃y (x = S(y))           x + 0 = x            x + S(y) = S(x + y)
        x · 0 = 0                       x · S(y) = x · y + x

(universally closed), with x < y defined as ∃z (S(z) + x = y) or taken as primitive with a suitable axiom. Peano arithmetic PA adds the induction scheme: for every formula φ(x, y⃗), the universal closure of φ(0) ∧ ∀x (φ(x) → φ(S(x))) → ∀x φ(x). Q is very weak — it does not prove x + y = y + x — but it is strong enough for incompleteness.

A formula is Δ₀ (bounded) if all its quantifiers are bounded, ∀x < t or ∃x < t; Σ₁ if it is ∃x⃗ ψ with ψ bounded.

Theorem 11.1 (Σ₁-completeness of Q). Every Σ₁ sentence true in 𝔑 is provable in Q.

Proof sketch. Q proves every true atomic and negated atomic sentence about numerals (by computing with the recursion axioms), and Q ⊢ ∀x (x < n̄ ↔ x = 0̄ ∨ … ∨ x = k̄), where k = n − 1, so bounded quantifiers reduce to finite conjunctions and disjunctions. A true ∃x ψ(x) has a true instance ψ(m̄), which Q proves, and ∃I finishes. ∎

Gödel numbering

Assign each symbol of L_A a code number, and code a finite sequence (a₁, …, aₖ) of numbers by 2^(a₁+1) · 3^(a₂+1) · … · pₖ^(aₖ+1), where pₖ is the k-th prime. Then formulas, and finite sequences of formulas such as derivations, receive Gödel numbers ⌜φ⌝ and ⌜D⌝. Inside formulas we write ⌜φ⌝ also for the numeral of the number ⌜φ⌝. The key point is that all the syntactic operations — "x codes a formula", "x codes the negation of the formula coded by y", "the substitution of the numeral for z into the formula coded by y has code x", "d codes a derivation from T of the formula coded by x" — are computable relations on numbers, indeed primitive recursive when T has a primitive recursive set of axioms.

Theorem 11.2 (Representability). Every computable relation R ⊆ ℕᵏ is representable in Q by a Σ₁ formula ρ(x⃗): if R(n⃗) then Q ⊢ ρ(n̄⃗), and if not R(n⃗) then Q ⊢ ¬ρ(n̄⃗). Every computable function f is represented by a formula ψ_f(x⃗, y) with Q ⊢ ∀y (ψ_f(n̄⃗, y) ↔ y = m̄), where m = f(n⃗).

The proof codes Turing machine computations (or, following Gödel, the recursive definitions of primitive recursive functions) using Gödel's β-function lemma: for every finite sequence a₀, …, aₙ there are c, d with aᵢ = c mod (1 + (i + 1)d) for all i ≤ n, by the Chinese remainder theorem. This lets a single pair of numbers code a sequence, using only + and ·.

Let T be a c.e. theory extending Q. Then "d codes a T-derivation of x" is a computable relation Prf_T(d, x), and Prov_T(x) := ∃d Prf_T(d, x) is a Σ₁ formula with T ⊢ σ ⟹ 𝔑 ⊨ Prov_T(⌜σ⌝) ⟹ Q ⊢ Prov_T(⌜σ⌝).

The diagonal lemma

Lemma 11.3 (Diagonal or fixed-point lemma; Gödel, Carnap). For every formula φ(x) with one free variable there is a sentence σ such that Q ⊢ σ ↔ φ(⌜σ⌝).

Proof. Let diag be the computable function sending the code of a formula χ(x) to the code of χ(⌜χ⌝), and let δ(x, y) represent it. Put ψ(x) := ∃y (δ(x, y) ∧ φ(y)), let m = ⌜ψ⌝, and let σ := ψ(m̄). Then diag(m) = ⌜σ⌝, so Q ⊢ ∀y (δ(m̄, y) ↔ y = ⌜σ⌝), and therefore Q ⊢ σ ↔ ∃y (y = ⌜σ⌝ ∧ φ(y)) ↔ φ(⌜σ⌝). ∎

The sentence σ says, in a precise arithmetical sense, "I have property φ". It is a formalised version of the liar sentence, but with provability in place of truth there is no paradox, only incompleteness.

The first incompleteness theorem

Theorem 11.4 (Gödel 1931, Rosser 1936). Let T be a consistent, computably axiomatisable theory extending Q. Then there is a sentence G_T such that T ⊬ G_T and T ⊬ ¬G_T.

Proof (Gödel's version, under the assumption that T is ω-consistent, or simply sound). Apply the diagonal lemma to ¬Prov_T(x) to get G with T ⊢ G ↔ ¬Prov_T(⌜G⌝). If T ⊢ G, then T ⊢ Prov_T(⌜G⌝) by Σ₁-completeness and T ⊢ ¬Prov_T(⌜G⌝) by the fixed point, so T is inconsistent. Hence T ⊬ G. Therefore no n is the code of a derivation of G, so T ⊢ ¬Prf_T(n̄, ⌜G⌝) for every n by representability. If T ⊢ ¬G, then T ⊢ ∃d Prf_T(d, ⌜G⌝), so T would prove an existential statement while refuting each instance: T would be ω-inconsistent, contradicting the assumption. ∎

Rosser's trick removes ω-consistency: diagonalise instead on the formula "for every derivation of x there is a shorter derivation of the negation of x". Then for consistent T neither the Rosser sentence nor its negation is provable. Note also that G is true in 𝔑 whenever T is consistent, since it asserts its own unprovability, which holds.

A computability-theoretic proof

A proof of a weaker form via Lesson 10 is instructive. Suppose T is c.e., sound (all its theorems true in 𝔑) and contains Q. The set K is Σ₁-definable: e ∈ K iff 𝔑 ⊨ κ(ē) for a Σ₁ formula κ. If T decided every sentence κ(ē), then, being sound, T ⊢ ¬κ(ē) iff e ∉ K, so the complement of K would be c.e. (enumerate the theorems of T), contradicting Theorem 10.2. Hence there is some e ∉ K with T ⊬ ¬κ(ē): a true Π₁ sentence that T does not prove. Moreover the set of true sentences of arithmetic is not c.e., and not even arithmetically definable.

Tarski's undefinability theorem

Theorem 11.5 (Tarski 1933/36). There is no formula True(x) of L_A such that 𝔑 ⊨ True(⌜σ⌝) ↔ σ for every sentence σ.

Proof. Apply the diagonal lemma to ¬True(x): we obtain λ with 𝔑 ⊨ λ ↔ ¬True(⌜λ⌝), and with True this gives 𝔑 ⊨ λ ↔ ¬λ. ∎

Truth is not arithmetically definable, whereas provability is (it is Σ₁). Since the two notions have different definability properties, they cannot coincide: this is the shortest route to the incompleteness of every arithmetically definable sound theory.

The second incompleteness theorem

Let Con(T) be the sentence ¬Prov_T(⌜0 = 1⌝). The Hilbert–Bernays–Löb derivability conditions, valid for PA and for every c.e. theory containing it (with the natural formalisation of Prov_T), are:

        D1:  if T ⊢ φ, then T ⊢ Prov(⌜φ⌝)
        D2:  T ⊢ Prov(⌜φ → ψ⌝) → (Prov(⌜φ⌝) → Prov(⌜ψ⌝))
        D3:  T ⊢ Prov(⌜φ⌝) → Prov(⌜Prov(⌜φ⌝)⌝)

D3 is formalised Σ₁-completeness, and its verification is the technical heart of the theorem.

Theorem 11.6 (Gödel 1931). If T is a consistent c.e. extension of PA, then T ⊬ Con(T).

Proof. Let G satisfy T ⊢ G ↔ ¬Prov(⌜G⌝). From T ⊢ G → ¬Prov(⌜G⌝) and D1, D2 one gets T ⊢ Prov(⌜G⌝) → Prov(⌜¬Prov(⌜G⌝)⌝). By D3, T ⊢ Prov(⌜G⌝) → Prov(⌜Prov(⌜G⌝)⌝). Since T proves that a formula and its negation together yield 0 = 1, D1 and D2 give T ⊢ Prov(⌜G⌝) → Prov(⌜0 = 1⌝), that is, T ⊢ Con(T) → ¬Prov(⌜G⌝), hence T ⊢ Con(T) → G. If T ⊢ Con(T), then T ⊢ G, contradicting the first theorem. ∎

The theorem ended Hilbert's programme in its original form: a consistency proof for arithmetic or analysis cannot be carried out in a weaker, finitary system that is itself formalisable in PA. Gentzen (1936) proved Con(PA) in primitive recursive arithmetic augmented by transfinite induction up to the ordinal ε₀ = sup{ω, ω^ω, ω^ω^ω, …}, which locates exactly the strength of PA.

Löb's theorem (1955), proved by the same method, says that if T ⊢ Prov(⌜φ⌝) → φ, then T ⊢ φ. It answers Henkin's question about the sentence asserting its own provability: such a sentence is provable.

Natural independent statements

For two decades the only known independent statements of arithmetic were metamathematical. Paris and Harrington (1977) found a strengthened finite Ramsey theorem that is true but unprovable in PA, and Kirby and Paris (1982) showed the same for Goodstein's theorem, that every Goodstein sequence (write n in hereditary base b, replace b by b + 1, subtract 1, repeat) eventually reaches 0. Both are proved by transfinite induction up to ε₀.

Lesson 12: SAT, NP and the Cook–Levin Theorem
─────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        P: decidable in polynomial time. NP: membership has polynomial-size certificates checkable in polynomial time.
        Cook–Levin: SAT is NP-complete; every NP problem reduces to SAT in polynomial time.
        Fagin: NP is exactly the class of properties definable in existential second-order logic.

Time complexity

Computability asks what can be computed at all; complexity theory asks what can be computed efficiently. A deterministic Turing machine runs in time t(n) if it halts within t(n) steps on every input of length n. The class P consists of languages (sets of binary strings) decided in time O(nᵏ) for some k. The class NP consists of languages L for which there is a polynomial p and a polynomial-time relation V with x ∈ L iff ∃y (|y| ≤ p(|x|) ∧ V(x, y)); equivalently, languages decided by nondeterministic machines in polynomial time. The certificate y is a "witness": a satisfying assignment for SAT, a Hamiltonian cycle for HAM, a factor for COMPOSITE. Clearly P ⊆ NP. Whether P = NP is the central open problem of theoretical computer science and one of the seven Clay Millennium Prize problems.

Reductions and completeness

A polynomial-time reduction from A to B is a polynomial-time computable f with x ∈ A iff f(x) ∈ B; write A ≤ₚ B. B is NP-hard if every A ∈ NP satisfies A ≤ₚ B, and NP-complete if moreover B ∈ NP. If any NP-complete problem is in P, then P = NP.

Theorem 12.1 (Cook 1971, Levin 1973). SAT is NP-complete.

Proof sketch. SAT ∈ NP, with the satisfying assignment as certificate. Let L ∈ NP be decided by a nondeterministic machine M in time p(n). For an input x of length n, the computation is a tableau of (p(n) + 1) rows (time steps) and p(n) + 1 columns (tape cells), each cell holding a tape symbol and possibly the state of the head. Introduce Boolean variables X(i, j, s), true iff cell j at time i holds symbol s (s ranges over the finite set Γ ∪ (Q × Γ)). Write clauses expressing: (1) each cell holds exactly one symbol; (2) row 0 is the start configuration on x; (3) every 2 × 3 window of consecutive cells in consecutive rows is consistent with a transition of M — a local condition, because a Turing machine changes the tape only next to the head; (4) some cell at some time holds the accepting state. The number of variables and clauses is O(p(n)²), with constants depending only on M, and the formula can be produced in polynomial time. It is satisfiable iff some computation of M accepts x. ∎

Theorem 12.2. 3-SAT, the restriction of SAT to clauses with exactly three literals, is NP-complete.

Proof. Reduce SAT (in CNF) to 3-SAT. Replace a clause (ℓ₁ ∨ … ∨ ℓₖ) with k > 3 by (ℓ₁ ∨ ℓ₂ ∨ y₁) ∧ (¬y₁ ∨ ℓ₃ ∨ y₂) ∧ … ∧ (¬y(k−3) ∨ ℓ(k−1) ∨ ℓₖ), with k − 3 new variables and k − 2 clauses; short clauses are padded using new variables (for example (ℓ) becomes four clauses (ℓ ∨ a ∨ b), (ℓ ∨ a ∨ ¬b), (ℓ ∨ ¬a ∨ b), (ℓ ∨ ¬a ∨ ¬b)). Each replacement is satisfiable together with the rest exactly when the original clause is. ∎

From 3-SAT, Karp (1972) derived NP-completeness of 21 problems, including CLIQUE, VERTEX COVER, HAMILTONIAN CYCLE, SUBSET SUM, and CHROMATIC NUMBER. For CLIQUE: given a 3-CNF with m clauses, build a graph with a vertex for each occurrence of a literal (3m vertices) and join two vertices when they lie in different clauses and are not complementary; the graph has an m-clique iff the formula is satisfiable.

Beyond NP

coNP is the class of complements of NP languages; TAUT, the set of propositional tautologies, is coNP-complete, since φ is a tautology iff ¬φ is unsatisfiable. NP = coNP would mean that every tautology has a short proof in some proof system. PSPACE, the class of languages decidable in polynomial space, contains NP and coNP; its canonical complete problem is TQBF, the truth of fully quantified Boolean formulas ∀x₁ ∃x₂ … φ (Stockmeyer and Meyer 1973). The time hierarchy theorem gives P ≠ EXP, but none of the inclusions P ⊆ NP ⊆ PSPACE ⊆ EXP is known to be strict individually.

Descriptive complexity

Theorem 12.3 (Fagin 1974). A class of finite structures is in NP iff it is definable by an existential second-order sentence ∃R₁ … ∃Rₖ φ, with φ first-order.

For example 3-colourability of graphs is defined by ∃R ∃G ∃B (∀x (R(x) ∨ G(x) ∨ B(x)) ∧ ∀x ∀y (E(x, y) → ¬(R(x) ∧ R(y)) ∧ ¬(G(x) ∧ G(y)) ∧ ¬(B(x) ∧ B(y)))). Immerman and Vardi (1982) showed that on ordered finite structures P is captured by first-order logic with a least fixed-point operator. Thus P = NP becomes a question about the expressive power of logics on finite structures.

Russell's paradox

Frege's Grundgesetze (1893, 1903) contained a principle equivalent to unrestricted comprehension: for every property φ(x) there is a set {x : φ(x)}. Russell observed in 1901, and wrote to Frege in 1902, that the property x ∉ x then yields R = {x : x ∉ x}, with R ∈ R iff R ∉ R. The theory is inconsistent. The paradox is a diagonal argument of exactly the same shape as Cantor's theorem, the halting problem and the Gödel sentence.

The axioms of ZFC

Zermelo (1908) proposed an axiomatisation, completed by Fraenkel and Skolem (1922) with Replacement and by von Neumann with Foundation. In the language {∈}:

  1. Extensionality: sets with the same elements are equal.
  2. Empty set: there is a set with no elements.
  3. Pairing: for all a, b there is {a, b}.
  4. Union: for every set a there is ⋃a, the set of members of members of a.
  5. Power Set: for every a there is P(a) = {x : x ⊆ a}.
  6. Separation (a scheme): for every set a and formula φ, {x ∈ a : φ(x)} exists.
  7. Replacement (a scheme): if φ(x, y) defines a function on a set a, its image {y : ∃x ∈ a φ(x, y)} is a set.
  8. Infinity: there is a set containing ∅ and closed under x ↦ x ∪ {x}.
  9. Foundation: every nonempty set has an ∈-minimal element.
  10. Choice: every set of nonempty sets has a choice function.

Separation only carves subsets out of existing sets, which blocks Russell's argument: applied to a set a it shows that {x ∈ a : x ∉ x} is not a member of a. Consequently there is no set of all sets. The intended picture is the cumulative hierarchy V(0) = ∅, V(α + 1) = P(V(α)), and V(λ) = the union of the V(α) for α < λ when λ is a limit; Foundation says that every set appears at some stage.

Ordinals

A set is transitive if every element of it is a subset of it. An ordinal is a transitive set well-ordered by ∈. Von Neumann's ordinals are 0 = ∅, 1 = {0}, 2 = {0, 1}, …, ω = {0, 1, 2, …}, ω + 1 = ω ∪ {ω}, and so on. Every well-ordered set is isomorphic to a unique ordinal, and the ordinals are themselves well-ordered by ∈; the collection of all ordinals is not a set (the Burali-Forti paradox, 1897). Transfinite induction and recursion over ordinals generalise induction and recursion over ℕ. Ordinal arithmetic is not commutative: 1 + ω = ω ≠ ω + 1, and 2 · ω = ω ≠ ω · 2 = ω + ω.

Cardinals

|X| = |Y| means there is a bijection, |X| ≤ |Y| an injection. The Cantor–Schröder–Bernstein theorem (provable without Choice) says |X| ≤ |Y| and |Y| ≤ |X| imply |X| = |Y|. With Choice every set is well-orderable (Zermelo 1904), so every set has a cardinality |X|, the least ordinal in bijection with X. The infinite cardinals are ℵ₀ = ω, ℵ₁, ℵ₂, …, ℵ_ω, ….

Theorem 13.1 (Cantor 1891). For every set X, |X| < |P(X)|.

Proof. x ↦ {x} is an injection X → P(X). Suppose f : X → P(X) were surjective, and let D = {x ∈ X : x ∉ f(x)}, a set by Separation. Then D = f(d) for some d, and d ∈ D iff d ∉ f(d) = D. ∎

In particular |ℝ| = |P(ℕ)| = 2^ℵ₀ > ℵ₀. Cardinal arithmetic of infinite cardinals is simple for sums and products — κ + λ = κ · λ = max(κ, λ) if one of them is infinite and neither is 0 — but exponentiation is deep.

The continuum hypothesis

CH asserts 2^ℵ₀ = ℵ₁: every infinite set of reals is either countable or in bijection with ℝ. It was the first of Hilbert's 23 problems (1900). Gödel (1938) showed that if ZF is consistent, then so is ZFC + CH, by constructing the inner model L of constructible sets, in which both Choice and the generalised continuum hypothesis hold. Cohen (1963) invented forcing to show that if ZF is consistent, so is ZFC + ¬CH; indeed 2^ℵ₀ can consistently be ℵ₂, or any ℵ_α with cofinality greater than ω (König's theorem forbids cofinality ω, so 2^ℵ₀ ≠ ℵ_ω). Cohen also showed that Choice is independent of ZF. By the second incompleteness theorem, ZFC cannot prove its own consistency, so these are relative consistency results: Con(ZF) → Con(ZFC + CH), and similarly for ¬CH.

Foundations

ZFC serves as the standard foundation of mathematics: essentially every theorem of ordinary mathematics can be formalised and proved in it, and the formal proofs can in principle be checked mechanically — a programme now realised in proof assistants such as Lean, Coq, Isabelle and Mizar. Gödel's theorems show that no such foundation can be complete or prove its own consistency; large cardinal axioms, such as the existence of inaccessible or measurable cardinals, form a hierarchy of ever stronger extensions of ZFC, linearly ordered by consistency strength in all known cases.

  46. ∧ and ∨ are monotone and preserve 0 and 1, and these properties are closed under composition, so every definable function is monotone and nonconstant (it is 0 at all-zeros and 1 at all-ones). Conversely, let F be monotone with F(0⃗) = 0 and F(1⃗) = 1.
