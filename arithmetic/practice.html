Distribute over every term; combine only like terms

Grab a pencil and paper. Start with the easy ones — they use the exact patterns from the lessons. A few problems are open-ended: more than one right answer exists.

Hint for every problem: ask yourself, "What was done to x — and in what order do I undo it?"

🔴 CHALLENGE (Problems 85–100)

  • 85.
    The meeting point. f(x) = 3x − 2 and g(x) = 2x + 5. For what x do
    the two rules give the SAME output — and what is that output?
    (Set the rules equal to each other.)
  • 86.
    Two roads, one answer. Solve 5 − 2x > 1 TWO ways: once by flipping
    (divide by −2) and once by dodging the flip (add 2x to both sides).
    Show both give the same answer.
  • 87.
    Design it! Write an equation whose solution is x = −3, then check
    that your equation really works. (One recipe: build moves around −3,
    like 2x + 10 = ?, then compute what the right side must be.)
  • 88.
    The exponent detective. Find n in each case:
    (a) 2ⁿ × 2³ = 2⁹ (b) (3ⁿ)² = 3⁸ (c) 5ⁿ = 1
  • 89.
    The ratio split. Red and blue marbles are in the ratio 3:5, with
    56 marbles total. How many of each color? (Call them 3x and 5x.)
  • 90.
    The difference of squares. Solve x² − 9 = 0 by factoring.
    (Hint: 9 = 3², and x² − 3² factors into (x − 3)(x + 3) —
    check that guess by expanding it!)
  • 91.
    Three consecutive integers (like 4, 5, 6) add to 48. Call the
    smallest x — then the others are x + 1 and x + 2. Find all three.
  • 92.
    Catch the mistake. Sam solved −2x + 3 = 11 and got x = 4.
    Check his answer by substitution — is he right? If not, find the
    correct solution and pinpoint where he went wrong.
  • 93.
    Order matters. f(x) = 2x + 1 and g(x) = x². Compute f(g(3)) and
    g(f(3)). Are they the same? What does that tell you about the
    order of composition?
  • 94.
    The notebook budget, upgraded. You have 30 dollars; notebooks cost
    4 dollars each. Write an inequality for n, the number of notebooks
    you can afford, solve it — and explain why the real answer must be
    a whole number. How many notebooks, and how much change?
  • 95.
    Design a system. Two numbers add to 20, and one is 2 more than
    twice the other. Write two equations and solve. Check BOTH
    conditions.
  • 96.
    The equal powers. Which is bigger, 2⁸ or 4⁴ — or are they equal?
    (Hint: write 4 as 2² and use a power-of-a-power law.)
  • 97.
    Factor and verify. Solve x² + 8x + 15 = 0 by factoring, then
    check BOTH answers by substitution into the original equation.
  • 98.
    Design an inequality — open-ended! Write a two-step inequality
    that uses a negative multiplier and whose solution is x > 5.
    Solve your own inequality to prove it works.
  • 99.
    The doubling bacteria. A colony starts with 3 bacteria and doubles
    every hour, so after n hours there are 3 · 2ⁿ bacteria.
    (a) How many after 4 hours? (b) After which hour does the colony
    first pass 100?
  • 100.
    The grand finale — design it!
    (a) Invent a word problem that turns into the system 
    x + y = 12  and  y = x + 4,  then solve it to make sure it works.

    (b) Invent an "undo me" puzzle with at least THREE moves (like
    "I multiply by 2, add 4, divide by 2, subtract 7, and get 5 —
    what was my number?") whose answer is 10. Then undo it step by
    step to prove the answer really is 10.
