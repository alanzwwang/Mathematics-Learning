Lesson 1: Right Triangles — Meet the Mighty Hypotenuse
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        A right triangle has one 90° corner.
        The side across from the right angle — the hypotenuse — is always the longest side.

You already know right triangles: they're triangles with one perfect square corner — a 90° angle, like the corner of a book. Trigonometry lives inside right triangles, so let's name every part.

  · The two sides that FORM the right angle are called the LEGS. They're like the floor and the wall meeting in the corner of a room.

  · The side ACROSS from the right angle is called the HYPOTENUSE (say "hy-POT-en-oos"). It's always slanted, like a ramp — and it's always the LONGEST side of the triangle. Always. No exceptions, ever.

Why is the hypotenuse always longest? The biggest side of any triangle always sits across from the biggest angle — and in a right triangle, the 90° angle IS the biggest angle. (The other two must squeeze into the leftover 90°, so each one is smaller than 90°.)

One more useful fact: a right triangle can never have TWO right angles. Two 90° angles would already eat up all 180° — leaving nothing for the third corner!

✏️ Your Turn

(a) A right triangle has sides 5, 12, and 13. Which is the hypotenuse? (b) A right triangle has sides 7, 24, and 25. Which sides are the legs?

Answers: (a) 13 — the longest side, across from the right angle. (b) 7 and 24 — the two shorter sides that form the right angle. The 25 is the hypotenuse.

Lesson 2: Opposite and Adjacent — It's All About Where You Stand
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        Pick an angle. The side ACROSS from it is the OPPOSITE.
        The side NEXT TO it (that isn't the hypotenuse) is the ADJACENT.
        Switch angles — the labels switch too!

Here's a game. Take any right triangle and pick one of its two pointy corners (not the right angle — one of the other two). Stand at that corner and look around:

  · The side directly ACROSS from you — the one your corner doesn't touch at all — is the OPPOSITE side. Think: you're at home plate, and the opposite side is the outfield wall, way across the field.

  · The side right NEXT TO you — one of the two sides touching your corner — that ISN'T the hypotenuse is the ADJACENT side. ("Adjacent" means "next to," like adjacent seats in a movie theater.)

  · The hypotenuse is just the hypotenuse. It touches your corner too, but it never gets a new name. It's above this labeling game — it's the hypotenuse forever.

Now for the twist that makes this a real skill: switch to the OTHER pointy corner, and the opposite and adjacent sides SWAP. The side that was across from corner A is next to corner B!

Example: a right triangle has legs 3 and 4, and hypotenuse 5. Angle A faces the side of length 3. So for angle A: opposite = 3, adjacent = 4. But angle B faces the side of length 4 — for angle B: opposite = 4, adjacent = 3. Same triangle, different angle, different labels!

✏️ Your Turn

A right triangle has legs 6 and 8, and hypotenuse 10. Angle C faces the side of length 8. (a) For angle C, name the opposite and adjacent sides. (b) Now switch to angle D, which faces the side of length 6. Name the opposite and adjacent sides.

Answers: (a) Opposite = 8 (across from C), adjacent = 6 (next to C, not the hypotenuse). (b) Opposite = 6, adjacent = 8 — they swapped! The hypotenuse stayed 10, as always.

Lesson 3: The Big Idea — Same Angle, Same Ratio
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        A ratio is a fraction made from two sides.
        Same angle → same ratio, no matter how big the triangle is.

        3/5 = 6/10 = 9/15

Here comes the idea that ALL of trigonometry is built on. Don't blink!

A RATIO is just a fraction comparing two numbers — like opposite side ÷ hypotenuse. Now watch what happens when we compute that ratio in three different triangles. Each one has an angle that faces the first side listed:

        Triangle        opposite ÷ hypotenuse
        3-4-5           3/5
        6-8-10          6/10 = 3/5
        9-12-15         9/15 = 3/5

Three different-sized triangles — one identical ratio! That's no accident. The 6-8-10 triangle is just the 3-4-5 with every side doubled, and the 9-12-15 is the 3-4-5 tripled. Doubling the top AND bottom of a fraction never changes its value.

So here's the big idea: the ratio depends ONLY on the angle — never on the size of the triangle. A tiny triangle and a giant triangle with the same angles give the exact same ratios. The ratio is the angle's FINGERPRINT. Two triangles might be different sizes, but if they share an angle, that angle's ratios are identical.

This is why trig works at all! A mathematician can measure a tiny paper triangle, and you can use those same ratios on a triangle the size of a mountain. Same angle, same ratio — forever and ever.

✏️ Your Turn

A right triangle has legs 9 and 12, and hypotenuse 15. (a) Compute opposite ÷ hypotenuse for the angle facing side 9, and simplify. (b) Which famous triangle has the same angles as this one?

Answers: (a) 9/15 = 3/5. (b) The 3-4-5 triangle! The 9-12-15 is the 3-4-5 tripled — so its angles (and its ratios) match exactly.

Lesson 4: Sine, Cosine, Tangent — SOH CAH TOA
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        sin = Opposite ÷ Hypotenuse   (SOH)
        cos = Adjacent ÷ Hypotenuse   (CAH)
        tan = Opposite ÷ Adjacent     (TOA)

Three ratios showed up in Lesson 3 — and they turn out to be so useful that mathematicians gave them names. They're just nicknames for fractions of sides:

  · SINE (say "sign"), written sin:    opposite ÷ hypotenuse
  · COSINE (say "CO-sign"), written cos:  adjacent ÷ hypotenuse
  · TANGENT (say "TAN-jent"), written tan:  opposite ÷ adjacent

How do you keep them straight? Chant the most famous made-up word in math class:

        SOH · CAH · TOA     (say "so-kuh-TOE-uh")

S-OH: Sine is Opposite over Hypotenuse. C-AH: Cosine is Adjacent over Hypotenuse. T-OA: Tangent is Opposite over Adjacent. Chant it five times right now. By problem 17, you'll say it in your sleep.

Let's compute all three for a 3-4-5 triangle. Angle A faces side 3, so for angle A: opposite = 3, adjacent = 4, hypotenuse = 5.

        sin A = 3/5       cos A = 4/5       tan A = 3/4

Now switch to angle B, which faces side 4. The labels swap: opposite = 4, adjacent = 3.

        sin B = 4/5       cos B = 3/5       tan B = 4/3

Bonus discovery — look closely at those two rows: sin A = 3/5 and cos B = 3/5. They're EQUAL! That's not luck. The side opposite angle A is adjacent to angle B, so sine of one pointy angle always equals cosine of the other. And since every right triangle's two pointy angles add to 90° (they share the 90° left over from the right angle), the rule is: sin of an angle = cos of its partner, where partners add to 90°.

One last pattern worth noticing: sin and cos both have the hypotenuse — the LONGEST side — on the bottom. So sin and cos are always smaller than 1. But tan divides leg by leg, and either leg can be longer — so tan can be bigger than 1 (like 4/3 up there!). Remember that; the answer key will test it.

✏️ Your Turn

A right triangle has legs 5 and 12, and hypotenuse 13. Angle C faces side 5. Compute sin C, cos C, and tan C.

Answers: opposite = 5, adjacent = 12, hypotenuse = 13. So sin C = 5/13, cos C = 12/13, tan C = 5/12.

Lesson 5: The Pythagorean Theorem — Find the Missing Side
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        leg² + leg² = hypotenuse²

        3² + 4² = 5²   —   that's 9 + 16 = 25 ✅

Problem: sometimes a problem hands you only TWO sides of a right triangle — but trig needs all three! Luckily, the ancient Greeks (and Babylonians before them) discovered a beautiful rule hiding in every right triangle. Square each leg, add the squares, and you get the square of the hypotenuse:

        leg² + leg² = hypotenuse²

This is the PYTHAGOREAN THEOREM (say "puh-thag-uh-REE-un"). Check it on the 3-4-5: 3² + 4² = 9 + 16 = 25 = 5². It works!

Missing hypotenuse? Add the leg squares, then find what number squared gives that total:

        Legs 5 and 12:  25 + 144 = 169. What squared is 169? 13! (13² = 169)

Missing leg? Subtract instead:

        Leg 8, hypotenuse 10:  100 − 64 = 36. What squared is 36? 6! (6² = 36)

The hall-of-fame triples (whole-number side sets that work perfectly):

        3-4-5 · 6-8-10 · 5-12-13 · 9-12-15 · 8-15-17

Now the full two-step move that the practice problems love: find the missing side FIRST, then compute the trig ratios. Legs 5 and 12? Hypotenuse is 13 (from above) — and now sin, cos, and tan of either angle are yours for the taking.

✏️ Your Turn

A right triangle has legs 8 and 15. (a) Find the hypotenuse. (b) Then give sin of the angle facing side 8.

Answers: (a) 64 + 225 = 289, and 17² = 289 — the hypotenuse is 17. (b) sin = opposite ÷ hypotenuse = 8/17.

Lesson 6: The Special Angles — 30°, 45°, 60°
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section — your permanent cheat sheet:

        sin 30° = 0.5       cos 30° ≈ 0.87      tan 30° ≈ 0.58
        sin 45° ≈ 0.71      cos 45° ≈ 0.71      tan 45° = 1
        sin 60° ≈ 0.87      cos 60° = 0.5       tan 60° ≈ 1.73

Every angle has its own sin, cos, and tan — its fingerprint, remember. Calculators know them all. But three angles are so famous and so useful that their values are worth memorizing: 30°, 45°, and 60°. That's the table above.

First, a symbol: ≈ means "about equal to." The true values of cos 30° and friends are decimals that go on forever, but 0.87 is so close that your ruler will never know the difference. Two values in the table are EXACT — sin 30° = 0.5 and cos 60° = 0.5 and tan 45° = 1 — which is why problems adore them.

Now hunt for the patterns hiding in the table (test-makers love these):

  · Read down the sin column: 0.5, 0.71, 0.87. As the angle GROWS, sine GROWS.
  · Read down the cos column: 0.87, 0.71, 0.5. As the angle grows, cosine SHRINKS.
  · Spot the twins: sin 30° = cos 60°, and sin 60° = cos 30°. Partners add to 90° — and sine of one equals cosine of the other, just like Lesson 4 promised!
  · tan 45° = 1 exactly. Why? A right triangle with a 45° angle has two EQUAL legs (its angles are 45°-45°-90°) — and opposite ÷ adjacent with equal legs is 1. Always.
  · tan 60° ≈ 1.73 — bigger than 1! Tangent is allowed to do that. Sine and cosine never can.

✏️ Your Turn

(a) Which is bigger, tan 30° or tan 45°? (b) sin 30° = 0.5. Without the table, what is cos 60°?

Answers: (a) tan 45° = 1 beats tan 30° ≈ 0.58. (b) 0.5 — 30° and 60° are partners (they add to 90°), and sin of one partner equals cos of the other.

Lesson 7: Measuring the World — Kites, Ladders, Ramps, Shadows
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        height in the air     = sin(angle) × slanted length
        reach across ground   = cos(angle) × slanted length
        height from shadow    = tan(angle) × ground distance

Time for the payoff — using trig on the actual world. Every measuring problem has the same cast of characters: something SLANTED (a kite string, a ladder, a ramp — the hypotenuse), an ANGLE it makes with the ground, a HEIGHT (opposite the angle), and a GROUND distance (adjacent to the angle).

The recipes come straight from SOH CAH TOA. If sin = opposite ÷ hypotenuse, then flipping it around (just like "if 12 ÷ 3 = 4, then 12 = 4 × 3") gives: opposite = sin × hypotenuse. Same trick for cos and tan. Three recipes:

The kite. A kite string is 10 m long and makes a 30° angle with the ground. How high is the kite? Height is OPPOSITE the angle, string is the hypotenuse:

        height = sin 30° × 10 = 0.5 × 10 = 5 m

The ladder. A 10 m ladder leans against a wall at a 60° angle with the ground. How far is its foot from the wall? That distance is ADJACENT to the angle:

        distance = cos 60° × 10 = 0.5 × 10 = 5 m

The flagpole. A flagpole casts an 8 m shadow, and the sun's angle is 45°. How tall is it? Height is opposite, shadow is adjacent — that's tangent's job:

        height = tan 45° × 8 = 1 × 8 = 8 m

Notice the strategy every single time: PICK the angle, NAME the sides you know and want, then choose the ratio that mentions exactly those two sides. That's the whole game.

✏️ Your Turn

(a) A ramp's slanted surface is 20 m long and rises at a 30° angle. How high does it reach? (b) A tree casts a 10 m shadow when the sun's angle has tan ≈ 0.58. How tall is the tree?

Answers: (a) height = sin 30° × 20 = 0.5 × 20 = 10 m. (b) height = 0.58 × 10 = 5.8 m.

Lesson 8: Watch Out! Common Mistakes
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Keep the big ideas in sight:

        Pick your angle FIRST. The labels move when the angle moves.
        sin and cos are always less than 1. tan can be anything.

These five mistakes catch students every single year. Learn them now, and they won't catch you!

Mistake 1: Staring at the wrong angle

        In a 3-4-5 triangle, sin = 3/5.   ❌

For WHICH angle? Sin of the angle facing side 3 is 3/5 — but sin of the angle facing side 4 is 4/5! "Opposite" and "adjacent" are meaningless until you pick your angle. Circle the angle in your sketch first, every time. ✅

Mistake 2: Mixing up sine and cosine

        Angle A faces side 3 in a 3-4-5 triangle, so cos A = 3/5?   ❌

That's SINE. SOH versus CAH: sine takes the Opposite, cosine takes the Adjacent. cos A = 4/5. When in doubt, chant it out: SOH — CAH — TOA. ✅

Mistake 3: Giving tangent a hypotenuse

        In a 5-12-13 triangle, tan of the angle facing side 5 is 5/13?   ❌

TOA: tangent is Opposite ÷ Adjacent — the two LEGS. The hypotenuse never appears in a tangent. The correct answer is 5/12. If you see a 13 under a tan, an alarm should ring. ✅

Mistake 4: Thinking a bigger triangle has bigger trig values

        The 6-8-10 triangle is double the 3-4-5, so its sine is double?   ❌

6/10 simplifies to 3/5! Same angles, same ratios — the size of the triangle is irrelevant. That was the whole point of Lesson 3, and it's what makes trig powerful. ✅

Mistake 5: Swapping the special-angle twins

        sin 60° = 0.5?   ❌

That's sin 30° (and cos 60°). Sine GROWS as the angle grows, so sin 60° must be the bigger value: ≈ 0.87. Before you answer, ask yourself: should this number be the small one (0.5) or the big one (0.87)? ✅

Lesson 9: Review — The Big Picture
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Everything, one last time:

        Opposite = across · Adjacent = next to (not the hypotenuse) · Hypotenuse = longest side
        SOH CAH TOA:  sin = O/H · cos = A/H · tan = O/A
        Pythagorean theorem: leg² + leg² = hypotenuse²
        Special angles: sin 30° = cos 60° = 0.5 · tan 45° = 1 · sin 60° = cos 30° ≈ 0.87

The recap list

  · A right triangle has one 90° angle; the hypotenuse sits across from it and is always the longest side.
  · Opposite and adjacent depend on which angle you pick — switch angles and they swap. The hypotenuse never changes.
  · Same angle, same ratio — no matter the triangle's size. 3/5 = 6/10 = 9/15.
  · sin = opposite ÷ hypotenuse, cos = adjacent ÷ hypotenuse, tan = opposite ÷ adjacent. SOH CAH TOA!
  · sin of an angle equals cos of its partner — partners add to 90°.
  · sin and cos are always less than 1 (the hypotenuse is on the bottom). tan can be bigger than 1.
  · Pythagorean theorem: leg² + leg² = hypotenuse². Add to find a hypotenuse; subtract to find a leg.
  · The special angles: 30° (sin = 0.5), 45° (tan = 1), 60° (cos = 0.5).
  · Measuring the world: height = sin × slant, reach = cos × slant, height = tan × shadow.

The magic sentence

        Pick your angle.
        Name your sides.
        SOH CAH TOA.
        Same angle, same ratio — forever.

Say it out loud three times. Seriously! That's the whole lesson in four lines.

Trigonometry is the math of measuring the unreachable. Every ramp you roll down, every roof over your head, every GPS satellite overhead, every animated character jumping across a screen — they all lean on the three fractions you just learned. And notice how one humble idea from Lesson 3 — same angle, same ratio — quietly unlocked everything else. Math ideas are never alone — they hold hands.

Problems 75–78 — Trig backwards! Find the missing side from the ratio. (Lesson 4)

  75. sin A = 3/5, and the side opposite angle A is 6. How long is the
      hypotenuse? (Hint: 6 is double 3, so the hypotenuse is double 5.)
  76. tan A = 3/4, and the side opposite angle A is 9. How long is the
      adjacent side?
  77. cos B = 12/13, and the hypotenuse is 26. How long is the adjacent side?
  78. sin A = 8/17, and the hypotenuse is 34. How long is the side opposite
      angle A?

Problems 79–82 — Compare and connect! (Lessons 3–4)

  79. Triangle 1 has legs 3 and 4 with hypotenuse 5. Triangle 2 has legs 6
      and 8 with hypotenuse 10. In each triangle, compute sin of the angle
      that faces the smaller leg (simplify both!). What do you notice?
  80. Order these three from least to greatest: tan of the angle facing
      side 3 in a 3-4-5 triangle, tan 45°, and tan of the angle facing
      side 4 in a 3-4-5 triangle.
  81. In any right triangle, the two pointy angles add to 90°. If
      sin A = 5/13, what is cos B (the OTHER pointy angle)? Explain why
      in one sentence.
  82. sin 20° ≈ 0.34. Without any table or calculator, what is cos 70°?
      How do you know?

Problems 83–86 — Word problems with two steps! (Lesson 7)

  83. The kite. A kite string is 20 m long. (a) At a 30° angle, how high is
      the kite? (sin 30° = 0.5) (b) The wind lifts the string to a 60° angle
      with the same 20 m of string. About how high now? (sin 60° ≈ 0.87)
  84. The ladder. A 10 m ladder leans at a 60° angle against a wall.
      (a) How far is its foot from the wall? (cos 60° = 0.5)
      (b) About how high up the wall does it reach? (sin 60° ≈ 0.87)
  85. The ramp law. A ramp may rise at most 0.58 m per meter across
      (that's tan 30° ≈ 0.58). (a) The ramp covers 10 m across — what's the
      greatest rise allowed? (b) Is that ramp steeper or gentler than a
      45° ramp? How do you know?
  86. The two poles. Pole 1 casts a 6 m shadow at 45° (tan 45° = 1).
      Pole 2 casts a 10 m shadow at 30° (tan 30° ≈ 0.58). Find both heights.
      Which pole is taller?

Problems 87–88 — Explain yourself!

  87. Why can sin and cos never be bigger than 1, but tan can? Answer in
      one or two sentences. (Hint: think about what's on the bottom of each
      fraction.)
  88. In a 3-4-5 triangle, sin A = 3/5 for the angle A facing side 3. Without
      computing anything new, what is cos of the OTHER pointy angle?
      Explain why in one sentence.

Problems 89–90 — Spot the impossible!

  89. A student wrote: "In this triangle, sin A = 5/4." Explain why that's
      impossible — without even seeing the triangle.
  90. A triangle has legs 5 and 12 and hypotenuse 13. A student computed
      tan A = 5/13 for the angle A facing side 5. What did they do wrong,
      and what is the correct tan A?


🔴 CHALLENGE (Problems 91–100)

  91. The isosceles split. An isosceles triangle has two sides of 5 and a
      base of 6. Split it straight down the middle into two identical right
      triangles. (a) Each right triangle has one leg of 3 (half the base) and
      hypotenuse 5 — find the other leg (the triangle's height!). (b) Find
      sin, cos, and tan of a BASE angle (the angle where the base meets a
      side of 5 — it faces the height you just found).
  92. Steepest ramp — open-ended! A ramp rises 3 m over 4 m across, so its
      tan is 3/4. Invent a second ramp that is STEEPER and a third ramp that
      is GENTLER. Give the tan of each as a fraction, and order all three
      ramps by steepness.
  93. The twin kites. Kite 1 has a 10 m string at a 30° angle. Kite 2 has a
      20 m string at the same 30° angle. (a) Find both heights.
      (sin 30° = 0.5) (b) Kite 2's string is double, and its height is
      double — so why is its sin NOT double? Explain!
  94. The 45-45 triangle. A right triangle has two EQUAL legs. (a) What are
      its two pointy angles? (Hint: they share the leftover 90° equally.)
      (b) Explain why tan 45° must be EXACTLY 1. (c) Explain why
      sin 45° = cos 45°.
  95. Build-a-triangle — open-ended! Describe TWO different right triangles
      in which tan A = 3/4 (give all three sides of each triangle).
      Check each one.
  96. The shrinking shadow. The sun climbs from 30° in the morning to 60°
      at noon. (a) Does tan get bigger or smaller as the sun climbs? Use the
      cheat sheet. (b) Shadows work like this: shadow = height ÷ tan. If tan
      gets bigger, does a shadow get longer or shorter? (c) A 6 m pole at
      45° casts a shadow of 6 ÷ 1 = 6 m. Is its morning shadow (at 30°)
      longer or shorter than 6 m? What about its noon shadow (at 60°)?
  97. Which hill is steeper? Hill A rises 5 m over 12 m across. Hill B rises
      8 m over 15 m across. Compare their tans — you'll need a common
      denominator! Which hill is steeper?
  98. Partner angles. sin 25° ≈ 0.42 and cos 25° ≈ 0.91. (a) What is
      cos 65°? (b) What is sin 65°? (c) Explain the trick in one sentence.
      (Hint: 25° + 65° = 90°.)
  99. Design-a-problem — open-ended! Invent one word problem whose answer
      uses sin 30° = 0.5, and another whose answer uses tan 45° = 1.
      Solve both to make sure they work. (Use problems 51–58 as models.)
  100. The grand finale — the surveyor's day. A surveyor measures three
       things in one afternoon: (a) A 20 m zip-line cable makes a 30° angle
       with the ground. How high is the start above the end? (sin 30° = 0.5)
       (b) A 20 m crane arm points up at a 60° angle. How far OUT (across the
       ground) does it reach? (cos 60° = 0.5) (c) A building casts a 20 m
       shadow while the sun's angle has tan = 3/4. How tall is the building?
       (d) Which ratio — sin, cos, or tan — did each part use?
