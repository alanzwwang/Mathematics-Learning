Lesson 1: What Is an Equation?

📌 KEY IDEA
An equation says two things are EXACTLY equal: left side = right side. It is a perfect balance.

Before we solve anything, we need to understand what we're looking at — and learn the names of its parts. Honors algebra uses precise words, so let's collect them now.

The Equal Sign Is a Balance

Imagine an old-fashioned balance scale. When both sides weigh exactly the same, the beam is perfectly level. That is what = means: whatever is on the left weighs the same as whatever is on the right.

      3 × (−4) = −12

The left side is 3 × (−4), which is −12. The right side is −12. Same? Yes — the scale balances. This is a true equation.

      5 − 9 = 6

The left side is −4. The right side is 6. The scale tips over with a crash! This is a false equation.

Notice something new: negative numbers showed up and nobody panicked. From here on, negatives and fractions live inside our math as full citizens.

Expression or Equation?

An expression is a math phrase with NO equal sign, like 3x − 7. It just sits there being a recipe: "multiply x by 3, then subtract 7." You can't solve an expression — there's nothing to balance.

An equation is two expressions joined by an equal sign, like 3x − 7 = 11. An equation is a claim: "these two recipes give the same number." Your job is to find the x that makes the claim true.

The Parts Have Names

Look at this expression:

      5x − 3y + 8

The pieces separated by + and − signs are called terms. This one has three terms: 5x, −3y, and 8.

In the term 5x, the number 5 glued to the letter is called the coefficient. It tells you how many x's you have: 5x means 5 × x, or x + x + x + x + x.

In the term −3y, the coefficient is −3. Yes, the minus sign belongs to the term! Always grab the sign with the number.

The term 8 has no letter at all. It is called a constant — a number that never changes, no matter what x and y do.

One more word you already know: a letter like x or y that stands for a number is called a variable.

(b) What is the coefficient of y?

Lesson 2: The Mystery Letter — Solutions and Verifying

📌 KEY IDEA
A solution is a number you can plug in for the variable that makes the equation true. Verifying means plugging it back in to check — and professionals always check.

From Boxes to Letters

Long ago, students wrote problems with empty boxes:

      □ + 5 = 12

"What number goes in the box?" Eventually mathematicians got tired of drawing boxes and started using letters. There is nothing special or scary about x: it is a number wearing a mask. Our job is to unmask it.

What Is a Solution?

A solution is a number you can plug in for x that makes the equation true — that makes the scale balance. Let's verify one, honors style, with a negative answer:

Is x = −3 a solution of 2x + 11 = 5?

      Left side:  2 × (−3) + 11 = −6 + 11 = 5
      Right side: 5
      5 = 5  ✅

Balanced! So x = −3 IS the solution. Notice the negative number plugged in calmly and correctly. That is normal from now on.

Solutions can also be fractions. Is x = 3/2 a solution of 4x − 2 = 4?

      Left side:  4 × (3/2) − 2 = 6 − 2 = 4
      Right side: 4
      4 = 4  ✅

Yes! If you were guessing whole numbers only, you would have searched forever. This is exactly why we need a method instead of guessing.

Verifying with x on Both Sides

When x appears on both sides, plug your answer into EACH side separately and compare:

Verify x = 9 for 5x − 7 = 3x + 11:

      Left side:  5 × 9 − 7 = 45 − 7 = 38
      Right side: 3 × 9 + 11 = 27 + 11 = 38
      38 = 38  ✅

Both sides give the same number, so x = 9 checks out. That check is called verifying the solution, and you'll do it for every equation you ever solve. It is free and takes fifteen seconds!

The Problem with Guessing

For x + 5 = 12, guessing works fine. But what about these?

      (3/4)x − 5 = 7

      2(x − 3) = 5x + 9

      x² = 5x

Guessing could take all afternoon — and some of these answers are negative or fractions, or there is more than one answer! We need a machine that finds x every time, with zero luck involved. That machine has one rule, and it is in the next lesson.

✏️ YOUR TURN

Is x = −5 a solution of 20 − 2x = 30? Plug it in and check.

Answer: Yes! Left side: 20 − 2 × (−5) = 20 − (−10) = 20 + 10 = 30. Right side: 30. ✅

Lesson 3: The Golden Rule — With Negatives and Fractions

📌 THE GOLDEN RULE OF ALGEBRA
Whatever you do to one side, do exactly the same to the other side.

This is the most important lesson in the whole file. Everything else is just practice using it.

The Seesaw Test

Start with something true:

      8 = 8

The seesaw is level. Now add 3 to both sides:

      8 + 3 = 8 + 3   →   11 = 11   ✅

Still true! The same goes for subtracting, multiplying, or dividing — as long as you do it to BOTH sides, the equation stays true. But touch only one side and the seesaw crashes:

      8 + 3 ≠ 8   ❌

Breaking the balance is the one unforgivable sin of algebra.

A Word from History

Over 1,200 years ago, the Persian mathematician al-Khwarizmi wrote a book about exactly these moves. Its title contained the word al-jabr — "restoring" — because moving a subtracted term to the other side as an added one "restores" it. That book's title gave us the word algebra itself. When you subtract 5 from both sides, you are doing the same move al-Khwarizmi wrote about in Baghdad twelve centuries ago.

The Four Opposite Moves — Upgraded

Every operation has an opposite that undoes it:

      adding 5        ↔  subtracting 5
      subtracting 3   ↔  adding 3
      multiplying by 4  ↔  dividing by 4
      dividing by 2   ↔  multiplying by 2

Honors level means the numbers in these moves can be negative or fractions. The rule never changes. Watch.

Worked Example 1: x + (−9) = 4

Something was ADDED to x (a −9), so we SUBTRACT that same thing — which means adding 9 — to both sides:

      x + (−9) + 9 = 4 + 9      add 9 to both sides (Golden Rule)
      x = 13                    −9 + 9 cancels to 0; 4 + 9 = 13

Check: 13 + (−9) = 4 ✅

Worked Example 2: x − (−6) = 10

Careful! Subtracting a negative is the same as adding: x − (−6) means x + 6. Rewrite first, then solve:

      x + 6 = 10                subtracting −6 is the same as adding 6
      x + 6 − 6 = 10 − 6        subtract 6 from both sides
      x = 4                     6 − 6 cancels; 10 − 6 = 4

Check: 4 − (−6) = 4 + 6 = 10 ✅

Worked Example 3: −5x = 45

x was multiplied by −5, so divide both sides by −5:

      −5x ÷ (−5) = 45 ÷ (−5)    divide both sides by −5
      x = −9                    −5 ÷ −5 = 1, leaving x; 45 ÷ −5 = −9

Check: −5 × (−9) = 45 ✅ (negative times negative is positive!)

Worked Example 4: (3/4)x = 12

x was multiplied by the fraction 3/4. The opposite move: multiply by its reciprocal. The reciprocal of 3/4 is 4/3 — you just flip the fraction. Any number times its reciprocal is 1:

      (4/3) × (3/4)x = 12 × (4/3)   multiply both sides by the reciprocal 4/3
      x = 16                        (4/3)(3/4) = 1; 12 × 4/3 = 48/3 = 16

Check: (3/4) × 16 = 48/4 = 12 ✅

Worked Example 5: −x = 7

The sneakiest one-step equation of all. −x means −1 × x. So multiply both sides by −1 (or divide by −1 — same thing):

      −1 × (−x) = −1 × 7        multiply both sides by −1
      x = −7                    −1 × −1 = 1, leaving x

Check: −(−7) = 7 ✅

The Checking Habit

Did you notice the last line of every example? Plug your answer back into the ORIGINAL equation. If the scale balances, you're done. If not, you caught your own mistake before anyone else did. Champions always check.

✏️ YOUR TURN

Solve (−2/3)x = 10. (What is the reciprocal of −2/3?)

Answer: The reciprocal is −3/2. Multiply both sides by −3/2: x = 10 × (−3/2) = −15. Check: (−2/3) × (−15) = 30/3 = 10 ✅

Lesson 4: Multi-Step Equations — Unwrap in Reverse Order

📌 KEY IDEA
An equation like 3x − 7 = 11 dresses x in layers. Peel the layers off in REVERSE order: undo the adding or subtracting first, then the multiplying or dividing.

Socks and Shoes

Here is the trick, and it is pure common sense. In the morning you put on your socks first, then your shoes. At night, which comes off first? The shoes — the last thing that went on. You undo everything in reverse order.

Same with equations. In 3x − 7, x got dressed like this:

      1. First layer:  × 3
      2. Second layer (on top):  − 7

So to undress x, go backwards: undo the −7 first, then the ×3.

Worked Example 1: 3x − 7 = 11

      3x − 7 + 7 = 11 + 7       add 7 to both sides (peel off the top layer)
      3x = 18                   −7 + 7 cancels; 11 + 7 = 18
      3x ÷ 3 = 18 ÷ 3           divide both sides by 3 (peel off the bottom layer)
      x = 6                     3 ÷ 3 = 1; 18 ÷ 3 = 6

Check: 3 × 6 − 7 = 18 − 7 = 11 ✅

Worked Example 2: (2/3)x + 5 = 11

Fractions follow the same socks-and-shoes rule. The +5 is on top, so it comes off first:

      (2/3)x + 5 − 5 = 11 − 5   subtract 5 from both sides
      (2/3)x = 6                +5 − 5 cancels; 11 − 5 = 6
      (3/2) × (2/3)x = 6 × (3/2)   multiply both sides by the reciprocal 3/2
      x = 9                     (3/2)(2/3) = 1; 6 × 3/2 = 18/2 = 9

Check: (2/3) × 9 + 5 = 6 + 5 = 11 ✅

Worked Example 3: (x − 4)/3 = 2

The whole package (x − 4) was divided by 3. Undo that outer layer first:

      3 × (x − 4)/3 = 3 × 2     multiply both sides by 3
      x − 4 = 6                 the 3's cancel; 3 × 2 = 6
      x − 4 + 4 = 6 + 4         add 4 to both sides
      x = 10                    −4 + 4 cancels; 6 + 4 = 10

Check: (10 − 4)/3 = 6/3 = 2 ✅

Worked Example 4: 8 − 2x = 2  (two methods!)

This one has a trap: x is being multiplied by −2. Here are TWO correct ways through.

Method A — subtract 8 first:

      8 − 2x − 8 = 2 − 8        subtract 8 from both sides
      −2x = −6                  8 − 8 cancels; 2 − 8 = −6
      −2x ÷ (−2) = −6 ÷ (−2)    divide both sides by −2
      x = 3                     negative ÷ negative = positive

Method B — add 2x first (moves x to the other side with a happy positive sign):

      8 − 2x + 2x = 2 + 2x      add 2x to both sides
      8 = 2 + 2x                −2x + 2x cancels
      8 − 2 = 2 + 2x − 2        subtract 2 from both sides
      6 = 2x                    8 − 2 = 6; 2 − 2 cancels
      3 = x                     divide both sides by 2

Same answer, x = 3, both times! When two legal paths lead to the same place, pick whichever feels safer to you.

Check: 8 − 2 × 3 = 8 − 6 = 2 ✅

Why the Order Matters

What if you divided by 3 FIRST in 3x − 7 = 11? You would get x − 7/3 = 11/3 — fractions everywhere, yuck! The socks-and-shoes rule keeps the numbers friendly: remove the added or subtracted part first, then deal with the multiplication or division.

✏️ YOUR TURN

Solve (x + 6)/5 = 2. (What is the outer layer — what was done LAST to x?)

Answer: Multiply both sides by 5: x + 6 = 10. Then subtract 6: x = 4. Check: (4 + 6)/5 = 10/5 = 2 ✅

Lesson 5: The Distributive Property, Like Terms, and Factoring

📌 KEY IDEA
a(b + c) = ab + ac. Multiplication distributes over addition — and it works backwards too, which is called factoring.

What "Distribute" Means

To distribute means to hand something out to everyone inside the parentheses. Watch with plain numbers first:

      3(4 + 5) = 3 × 9 = 27           one way: add inside first
      3(4 + 5) = 3×4 + 3×5 = 12 + 15 = 27   other way: distribute the 3

Same answer — that is the distributive property: a(b + c) = ab + ac. It also works with subtraction: a(b − c) = ab − ac. And it works with negatives, which is where honors students earn their stripes:

      −2(x − 4) = −2 × x + (−2) × (−4) = −2x + 8

Did you see it? −2 times −4 is +8. The minus sign gets distributed too! This is the single most common place students lose points, so stare at it until it feels obvious.

Worked Example 1: 4(x − 3) = 20  (two methods again!)

Method A — divide first (works because 20 divides evenly by 4):

      4(x − 3) ÷ 4 = 20 ÷ 4     divide both sides by 4
      x − 3 = 5                 the 4's cancel; 20 ÷ 4 = 5
      x = 8                     add 3 to both sides

Method B — distribute first:

      4x − 12 = 20              distribute: 4 × x = 4x and 4 × (−3) = −12
      4x = 32                   add 12 to both sides
      x = 8                     divide both sides by 4

Same answer! Method A is faster when the division comes out even; Method B always works, even when it doesn't.

Check: 4 × (8 − 3) = 4 × 5 = 20 ✅

Worked Example 2: 3(x + 2) − 5 = 16

      3x + 6 − 5 = 16           distribute the 3: 3 × x and 3 × 2
      3x + 1 = 16               combine the plain numbers: 6 − 5 = 1
      3x = 15                   subtract 1 from both sides
      x = 5                     divide both sides by 3

Check: 3 × (5 + 2) − 5 = 21 − 5 = 16 ✅

Worked Example 3: 12 − 2(x − 3) = 6  (the sign trap!)

      12 − 2x + 6 = 6           distribute −2: −2 × x = −2x, and −2 × (−3) = +6
      18 − 2x = 6               combine the plain numbers: 12 + 6 = 18
      −2x = −12                 subtract 18 from both sides
      x = 6                     divide both sides by −2

Check: 12 − 2 × (6 − 3) = 12 − 6 = 6 ✅

Combining Like Terms

Like terms are terms with the exact same letter part: 7x and −2x are like terms; 7x and 7y are not. You combine like terms by adding their coefficients:

      7x − 2x = (7 − 2)x = 5x

Why does that work? It is the distributive property running backwards! Seven x's minus two x's leaves five x's, the same way seven apples minus two apples leaves five apples.

Worked Example 4: 7x − 3 − 2x = 17

      5x − 3 = 17               combine like terms: 7x − 2x = 5x
      5x = 20                   add 3 to both sides
      x = 4                     divide both sides by 5

Check: 7 × 4 − 3 − 2 × 4 = 28 − 3 − 8 = 17 ✅

Factoring — Distribution in Reverse

Factoring means pulling a common piece OUT of every term and writing it in front of parentheses:

      6x + 15 = 3(2x + 5)

Why is that legal? Because 3 × 2x = 6x and 3 × 5 = 15 — distribute the 3 and you get right back to where you started. Factoring and distributing are the same road in opposite directions. Always check factoring by re-distributing!

Verify with x = 3:  left side: 6 × 3 + 15 = 33.  right side: 3 × (2 × 3 + 5) = 3 × 11 = 33. ✅

Factoring feels like a trick now, but it becomes a superpower in Lesson 7, when it cracks open quadratic equations.

✏️ YOUR TURN

Solve 20 − 4(x + 1) = 8. (Distribute the −4 very carefully!)

Answer: 20 − 4x − 4 = 8 → 16 − 4x = 8 → −4x = −8 → x = 2. Check: 20 − 4 × 3 = 20 − 12 = 8 ✅

Lesson 6: x on Both Sides — and Two Sneaky Special Cases

📌 KEY IDEA
Collect all the x's on one side first (Golden Rule moves only!), then unwrap as usual. And if the x's ever vanish completely, what remains tells you the truth.

The Main Move

Look at this:

      7x − 4 = 3x + 12

There are x's at both ends of the seesaw. Before unwrapping, we gather them together. The move: subtract the smaller x-group from both sides.

Worked Example 1: 7x − 4 = 3x + 12

      7x − 4 − 3x = 3x + 12 − 3x   subtract 3x from both sides (Golden Rule)
      4x − 4 = 12               7x − 3x = 4x; 3x − 3x cancels
      4x = 16                   add 4 to both sides
      x = 4                     divide both sides by 4

Check: left: 7 × 4 − 4 = 24.  right: 3 × 4 + 12 = 24. ✅

Worked Example 2: 5(x − 2) = 2x + 8

      5x − 10 = 2x + 8          distribute the 5
      3x − 10 = 8               subtract 2x from both sides
      3x = 18                   add 10 to both sides
      x = 6                     divide both sides by 3

Check: left: 5 × (6 − 2) = 20.  right: 2 × 6 + 8 = 20. ✅

Worked Example 3: (1/2)x + 3 = (3/4)x − 1  (clear the fractions!)

Honors trick: when fractions annoy you, multiply EVERY term on BOTH sides by a number that wipes the denominators out. Here, 4 does the job:

      4 × (1/2)x + 4 × 3 = 4 × (3/4)x − 4 × 1   multiply every term by 4
      2x + 12 = 3x − 4          4 × 1/2 = 2; 4 × 3/4 = 3 — fractions gone!
      12 = x − 4                subtract 2x from both sides
      16 = x                    add 4 to both sides

It is perfectly fine for x to end up on the right — the equal sign doesn't care. So x = 16.

Check: left: (1/2)(16) + 3 = 11.  right: (3/4)(16) − 1 = 12 − 1 = 11. ✅

Special Case 1: Infinitely Many Solutions

Watch what happens here:

      2(x + 3) = 2x + 6
      2x + 6 = 2x + 6           distribute the 2
      6 = 6                     subtract 2x from both sides — the x's VANISH!

Is 6 = 6 true? Always! It doesn't matter what x is — both sides are really the same expression wearing two costumes. An equation that is true for EVERY value of x is called an identity, and it has infinitely many solutions. Try x = 100: left: 2 × 103 = 206; right: 200 + 6 = 206. ✅

Special Case 2: No Solution

      2(x + 3) = 2x + 5
      2x + 6 = 2x + 5           distribute the 2
      6 = 5                     subtract 2x from both sides — the x's vanish again!

But 6 = 5 is false — and no value of x can fix it, because x is gone! This equation has no solution. The two sides are parallel expressions that can never be equal.

How to Tell Them Apart

When your x's cancel out completely, look at the leftover number sentence:

      Leftover is TRUE  (like 6 = 6)   →  infinitely many solutions (identity)
      Leftover is FALSE (like 6 = 5)   →  no solution

This is not a weird exception — it is the math telling you the truth about the problem you started with.

✏️ YOUR TURN

Solve 6x + 1 = 2x + 17.

Answer: Subtract 2x: 4x + 1 = 17. Subtract 1: 4x = 16. Divide by 4: x = 4. Check: 6 × 4 + 1 = 25 and 2 × 4 + 17 = 25 ✅

Lesson 7: Exponent Laws — and Your First Quadratics

📌 KEY IDEA
An exponent counts repeated multiplication: x³ = x · x · x. Exponents follow a small set of laws — and equations with x² finally get two answers.

The Language of Exponents

In x³, the x is the base and the little 3 is the exponent. The exponent tells you how many copies of the base get multiplied:

      x³ = x · x · x
      2⁴ = 2 · 2 · 2 · 2 = 16

Exponent Law 1: Multiplying Same Bases — Add the Exponents

      x² · x³ = (x · x) · (x · x · x) = x⁵

Count the x's: two, then three more — five total. So the law is:

      xᵃ · xᵇ = xᵃ⁺ᵇ

Worked mini-example: 2x² · 3x³ = (2 · 3) · (x² · x³) = 6x⁵. Multiply the coefficients, add the exponents.

Exponent Law 2: A Power of a Power — Multiply the Exponents

      (x²)³ = x² · x² · x² = x⁶

Three groups of two x's is six x's. So:

      (xᵃ)ᵇ = xᵃ·ᵇ

Careful — this is where students mix up Laws 1 and 2! x² · x³ = x⁵ (add), but (x²)³ = x⁶ (multiply). When in doubt, expand it out and count the x's.

Exponent Law 3: Dividing Same Bases — Subtract the Exponents

      x⁵ ÷ x² = (x · x · x · x · x)/(x · x) = x³

Two x's on the bottom cancel two x's on top, leaving three. So:

      xᵃ ÷ xᵇ = xᵃ⁻ᵇ   (as long as x ≠ 0 — dividing by zero is never allowed)

Exponent Law 4: The Zero Exponent

Watch the pattern as the exponent drops by 1 — each value divides by 2:

      2³ = 8
      2² = 4      (8 ÷ 2)
      2¹ = 2      (4 ÷ 2)
      2⁰ = 1      (2 ÷ 2)  ← the pattern demands it!

So any (nonzero) number to the zero power is 1: x⁰ = 1. Keep the pattern going one more step and you meet negative exponents:

      2⁻¹ = 1/2,  2⁻² = 1/4,  and in general  x⁻ⁿ = 1/xⁿ

A negative exponent means "flip it into the denominator." It is not a negative number — 2⁻² is a positive 1/4!

Your First Quadratic: x² = 49

An equation with x² in it is called a quadratic equation. Here comes the big surprise:

      x² = 49

What numbers, squared, give 49? Well, 7 × 7 = 49... but ALSO (−7) × (−7) = 49, because a negative times a negative is positive! So this equation has TWO solutions:

      x = 7  or  x = −7,  which we write as  x = ±7

Check both: 7² = 49 ✅ and (−7)² = 49 ✅. Most x² = (positive number) equations come with two answers. Never forget the negative one!

Worked Example 1: 3x² = 48

      3x² ÷ 3 = 48 ÷ 3          divide both sides by 3 (Golden Rule)
      x² = 16                   48 ÷ 3 = 16
      x = ±4                    both 4² and (−4)² equal 16

Check: 3 × 4² = 3 × 16 = 48 ✅ and 3 × (−4)² = 48 ✅

Worked Example 2: x³ = −8

Cubes are different from squares: a cube keeps the sign of its base. (−2)³ = (−2)(−2)(−2) = −8, so:

      x = −2                    the only solution

Check: (−2)³ = −8 ✅. One solution only — because no positive number cubes to a negative.

Worked Example 3: 3ᵏ = 81

The variable is UP in the exponent this time! Ask: how many 3's multiply to make 81?

      3¹ = 3,  3² = 9,  3³ = 27,  3⁴ = 81   ← there it is
      k = 4

Check: 3⁴ = 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 = 81 ✅

✏️ YOUR TURN

(a) Simplify (x³)².  (b) Solve 2x² = 32.

Answers: (a) x⁶ (power of a power: multiply the exponents, 3 × 2 = 6). (b) Divide by 2: x² = 16, so x = ±4. Check: 2 × 16 = 32 ✅

Lesson 8: Function Notation f(x) — Machines You Can Run Backwards

📌 KEY IDEA
f(x) names a machine: put a number in, get a number out. f(4) means "the output when the input is 4." Solving f(x) = 16 means asking "which input gives output 16?"

Meet the Machine

A function is a rule that takes an input and produces exactly one output. We name functions with letters like f, g, and h, and we write the rule like this:

      f(x) = 3x − 2

Read it out loud: "f of x equals 3x minus 2." It means: to get the output, multiply the input by 3 and subtract 2. HUGE warning: f(x) does NOT mean f times x! The parentheses here are not multiplication — they hold the input.

Running the Machine Forward: f(4)

To find f(4), replace every x in the rule with 4:

      f(x) = 3x − 2             the rule
      f(4) = 3 × 4 − 2          replace x with 4
      f(4) = 12 − 2 = 10        multiply first, then subtract

Input 4, output 10. Done!

Running the Machine Backwards: f(x) = 16

Now the famous backwards problem. The output is 16 — what was the input? Translating f(x) = 16 with our rule gives an equation you already know how to solve:

      3x − 2 = 16               the rule's output equals 16
      3x = 18                   add 2 to both sides
      x = 6                     divide both sides by 3

Check: f(6) = 3 × 6 − 2 = 16 ✅. The input was 6. Every "solve for x" you have ever done is secretly running a machine backwards!

A Squaring Machine: g(x) = x² − 5

      g(3) = 3² − 5 = 9 − 5 = 4
      g(−3) = (−3)² − 5 = 9 − 5 = 4

Two different inputs, same output! That is legal for a function (each input still gets exactly ONE output) — and it happens because squaring erases the minus sign. It is also why g(x) = 4 has two answers: x² − 5 = 4 gives x² = 9, so x = ±3.

When Two Machines Agree

Here is a preview of Lesson 10. Two functions:

      f(x) = 2x + 1
      g(x) = x + 7

For which input do both machines give the SAME output? Set the rules equal:

      2x + 1 = x + 7            the outputs must match
      x + 1 = 7                 subtract x from both sides
      x = 6                     subtract 1 from both sides

Check: f(6) = 13 and g(6) = 13 ✅. At input 6, both machines say 13. You just solved your first system without knowing it!

Lesson 9: Inequalities — and the Famous Flip Rule

📌 KEY IDEA
Solve an inequality exactly like an equation — with ONE extra law: multiplying or dividing both sides by a negative number flips the inequality sign.

The Four Signs

Equations claim two sides are equal. Inequalities claim one side is bigger:

      <   less than                  x < 5  means x is smaller than 5
      >   greater than               x > 5  means x is bigger than 5
      ≤   less than or equal to      x ≤ 5  means x can be 5 or smaller
      ≥   greater than or equal to   x ≥ 5  means x can be 5 or bigger

An equation usually has one solution (or two). An inequality has a whole REGION of solutions — infinitely many — so we check our work by test-driving numbers from that region.

Worked Example 1: x + 5 > 12

      x + 5 − 5 > 12 − 5        subtract 5 from both sides (Golden Rule still rules!)
      x > 7                     +5 − 5 cancels; 12 − 5 = 7

Test the region: try x = 8 (should work): 8 + 5 = 13 > 12 ✅. Try x = 7 itself (the boundary — should FAIL, because > is strict): 7 + 5 = 12, and 12 > 12 is false ✅. Perfect: the boundary is not included.

Worked Example 2: 2x − 3 ≤ 9

      2x − 3 + 3 ≤ 9 + 3        add 3 to both sides
      2x ≤ 12                   −3 + 3 cancels; 9 + 3 = 12
      x ≤ 6                     divide both sides by 2

Test the boundary x = 6 (should WORK, because ≤ includes equality): 2 × 6 − 3 = 9 ≤ 9 ✅. Test x = 5: 7 ≤ 9 ✅. Test x = 7: 11 ≤ 9 ❌ — correctly outside the region.

The Flip Rule — Why It Exists

Start with a true inequality and multiply both sides by −1:

      2 < 6                     true
      −2 > −6                   after ×(−1) the sign MUST flip to stay true!

Why? On the number line, −2 sits to the RIGHT of −6, so −2 really is greater. Multiplying by a negative mirrors everything across zero, and the mirror swaps who is bigger. So:

      When you multiply or divide both sides of an inequality by a NEGATIVE number,
      flip the sign: < becomes >, > becomes <, ≤ becomes ≥, ≥ becomes ≤.
      (Adding and subtracting never flip anything, no matter the sign.)

Worked Example 3: −3x < 12

      −3x ÷ (−3) > 12 ÷ (−3)    divide by −3 — FLIP the sign!
      x > −4                    −3 ÷ −3 = 1; 12 ÷ −3 = −4

Test: x = 0 is in the region (0 > −4): −3 × 0 = 0 < 12 ✅. x = −5 is outside (−5 > −4 is false): −3 × (−5) = 15 < 12? False ✅. The flip was correct!

Worked Example 4: 5 − 2x ≥ 11

      5 − 2x − 5 ≥ 11 − 5       subtract 5 from both sides (no flip — it's subtraction)
      −2x ≥ 6                   5 − 5 cancels; 11 − 5 = 6
      x ≤ −3                    divide by −2 — FLIP ≥ to ≤

Test: boundary x = −3: 5 − 2(−3) = 5 + 6 = 11 ≥ 11 ✅. x = −4: 5 + 8 = 13 ≥ 11 ✅. x = 0: 5 ≥ 11 ❌ correctly outside.

A Memory Trick

Think of the negative sign as a mirror. Multiplying by a negative holds a mirror up to the number line — and in a mirror, left and right swap. So the sign swaps too. No mirror (addition, subtraction, positive multiplication), no swap.

Answer: Subtract 3: −4x < 12. Divide by −4 and FLIP: x > −3. Check x = 0: −4 × 0 + 3 = 3 < 15 ✅

Lesson 10: Systems — Two Equations, Two Mysteries

📌 KEY IDEA
A system is two equations about the same two variables. The solution is the ONE pair (x, y) that makes both equations true at the same time.

What Is a System?

Sometimes one mystery number isn't enough — a problem hides two:

      x + y = 9
      y = x + 3

There are lots of pairs that make the first equation true: (1, 8), (2, 7), (4, 5)... and lots that make the second true: (1, 4), (2, 5), (3, 6)... But only ONE pair makes BOTH true at once. Finding it takes one of two classic moves.

Method 1: Substitution

If one equation tells you what y IS, plug that expression into the other equation. The two-variable problem collapses into one variable — and you already own one-variable equations.

Worked Example 1: y = x + 3 and x + y = 9

      x + (x + 3) = 9           substitute: replace y in the second equation with (x + 3)
      2x + 3 = 9                combine like terms: x + x = 2x
      2x = 6                    subtract 3 from both sides
      x = 3                     divide both sides by 2

Now run back for y:

      y = x + 3 = 3 + 3 = 6     plug x = 3 into the first equation

Solution: (x, y) = (3, 6). Check BOTH originals: 3 + 6 = 9 ✅ and 6 = 3 + 3 ✅. Always check both — a pair that fails either one is not a solution!

Method 2: Elimination

If the equations line up nicely, you can add them together so that one variable gets eliminated — cancelled out completely.

Worked Example 2: x + y = 10 and x − y = 2

      x + y = 10
      x − y = 2
      ─────────
      2x = 12                   add the two equations: +y and −y cancel!
      x = 6                     divide both sides by 2

Wait — is adding equations legal? Yes! It is the Golden Rule in disguise: the second equation says x − y and 2 are the same thing, so adding "x − y" to the left and "2" to the right is adding the same amount to both sides.

      y = 10 − x = 10 − 6 = 4   plug x = 6 into the first equation

Solution: (6, 4). Check: 6 + 4 = 10 ✅ and 6 − 4 = 2 ✅

Worked Example 3: 2x + y = 12 and x − y = 3

      2x + y = 12
       x − y = 3
      ─────────
      3x = 15                   add: +y and −y cancel again
      x = 5                     divide both sides by 3
      y = x − 3 = 5 − 3 = 2     rearrange the second equation (add y, subtract 3, to both sides)

Solution: (5, 2). Check: 2 × 5 + 2 = 12 ✅ and 5 − 2 = 3 ✅

A System in Disguise (Word Problem)

Two numbers add to 20, and their difference is 4. What are they?

      x + y = 20                translate "add to 20"
      x − y = 4                 translate "difference is 4"
      ─────────
      2x = 24                   eliminate by adding
      x = 12                    divide by 2
      y = 20 − 12 = 8           plug back in

The numbers are 12 and 8. Check: 12 + 8 = 20 ✅ and 12 − 8 = 4 ✅

Why This Works (and Where It's Going)

Every equation like x + y = 9 can be drawn as a straight line on a graph, and the solution of the system is exactly where the two lines cross. In high school you'll graph these; today you solved them with pure algebra. Same answers, no ruler needed.

Answer: Substitute: x + (x + 2) = 8 → 2x + 2 = 8 → 2x = 6 → x = 3. Then y = 3 + 2 = 5. Check: 3 + 5 = 8 ✅ and 5 = 3 + 2 ✅

Lesson 11: Watch Out! Common Mistakes (Honors Edition)

📌 SURVIVAL RULES
Same thing to both sides. Reverse order. Respect every sign. Flip on negative multiply/divide. Always check.

These six traps catch students every single year — including strong students going fast. Learn them now and they will never catch you.

Mistake 1: The Sign Slip

      x − (−5) = 10   →   trap answer: x = 15   ❌

Subtracting a negative is ADDING: x − (−5) is x + 5, so x = 5. Every minus sign in front of parentheses is a tiny alarm bell. Slow down for exactly one second and rewrite it.

Mistake 2: Forgetting to Distribute the Minus

      −2(x − 4) = −2x − 8   ❌
      −2(x − 4) = −2x + 8   ✅

The −2 must shake hands with EVERY term inside, and −2 × (−4) = +8. Cover the inside terms one at a time with your finger as you multiply — it physically forces you to get them all.

Mistake 3: Flipping Wrong in Inequalities

Two errors live here: forgetting to flip (−3x < 12 → x < −4 ❌) and flipping when you shouldn't (x − 3 < 12 needs NO flip — subtraction never flips anything). Ask before every move: "Am I multiplying or dividing by a NEGATIVE right now?" Only then does the mirror come out.

Mistake 4: Dividing by x (the Solution Thief)

      x² = 5x
      x = 5   ❌ — you just threw away half the answer!

Dividing both sides by x looks legal, but x might be 0, and dividing by zero is forbidden. In fact x = 0 IS a solution here (0² = 5 × 0 ✅), and dividing erased it! The safe road: move everything to one side and factor.

      x² − 5x = 0             subtract 5x from both sides
      x(x − 5) = 0            factor out the common x
      x = 0  or  x − 5 = 0    a product is zero only when a factor is zero
      x = 0  or  x = 5        two solutions, both safe

Check: 0² = 0 = 5 × 0 ✅ and 5² = 25 = 5 × 5 ✅. Rule of honor: never divide both sides by the variable. Factor instead.

Mistake 5: Thinking f(x) Means f Times x

f(4) is not f × 4! It means "the output of machine f when the input is 4." If f(x) = 3x − 2, then f(4) = 10 — a plain number, no f left in it. If your answer still has an f in it, the machine never got run.

Mistake 6: Mixing Up the Exponent Laws

      x² · x³ = x⁵   (add the exponents — you're collecting x's)
      (x²)³ = x⁶     (multiply the exponents — groups of groups)

Same three letters, different answers. When you feel the wobble, expand: x² · x³ is (x·x)(x·x·x) — count five x's. (x²)³ is x²·x²·x² — count six. Ten seconds of counting beats a wrong answer.

📌 EVERYTHING, ONE LAST TIME
Same thing to both sides. Unwrap in reverse order. Respect signs and mirrors. Always check.

64. x = −2. (−2)³ = −8 — a cube keeps its sign, so only one answer. ✅
65. x = ±3/4. (3/4)² = 9/16 and (−3/4)² = 9/16. ✅
66. x = ±4. Divide by 2: x² = 16. Both 4² and (−4)² equal 16. Check: 2 × 16 = 32 ✅
67. x⁷. Multiplying same bases: add the exponents, 3 + 4 = 7.
68. x⁶. Power of a power: multiply the exponents, 3 × 2 = 6.

76. x > 7. Add 4 to both sides (no flip — adding never flips). Test x = 8: 8 − 4 = 4 > 3 ✅
77. x ≤ 6. Subtract 5: 2x ≤ 12. Divide by 2 (positive — no flip). Test boundary x = 6: 12 + 5 = 17 ≤ 17 ✅
78. x < −5. Divide by −3 and FLIP: 15 ÷ (−3) = −5. Test x = −6: −3 × (−6) = 18 > 15 ✅
79. x ≤ −8. Multiply by −1 and FLIP (−x means −1 × x). Test x = −9: −(−9) = 9 ≥ 8 ✅
80. x > −3. Subtract 9: −2x < 6. Divide by −2 and FLIP: 6 ÷ (−2) = −3. Test x = 0: 9 < 15 ✅
81. x ≥ 9. Add 2: (1/3)x ≥ 3. Multiply by 3 (positive — no flip). Test x = 9: 3 − 2 = 1 ≥ 1 ✅
82. x > 6. Subtract 2x: 2x − 7 > 5. Add 7: 2x > 12. Divide by 2. Test x = 7: 21 > 19 ✅
83. (x, y) = (5, 6). Substitute: x + (x + 1) = 11 → 2x = 10 → x = 5. Then y = 5 + 1 = 6. Check: 5 + 6 = 11 ✅ and 6 = 5 + 1 ✅
84. (x, y) = (4, 8). Substitute: x + 2x = 12 → 3x = 12 → x = 4. Then y = 2 × 4 = 8. Check: 4 + 8 = 12 ✅ and 8 = 2 × 4 ✅
85. (x, y) = (9, 5). Add the equations: 2x = 18 → x = 9. Then y = 14 − 9 = 5. Check: 9 + 5 = 14 ✅ and 9 − 5 = 4 ✅
86. (x, y) = (6, 3). Add the equations: 3x = 18 → x = 6. Then 6 − y = 3 gives y = 3. Check: 2 × 6 + 3 = 15 ✅ and 6 − 3 = 3 ✅
87. (x, y) = (3, 7). The two y's are equal, so set the rules equal: x + 4 = 3x − 2 → 6 = 2x → x = 3. Then y = 3 + 4 = 7. Check: 7 = 3 + 4 ✅ and 7 = 3 × 3 − 2 ✅
88. The numbers are 19 and 11. System: x + y = 30 and x − y = 8. Add: 2x = 38 → x = 19, then y = 30 − 19 = 11. Check: 19 + 11 = 30 ✅ and 19 − 11 = 8 ✅
89. Input x = 6. Output 26 means 5x − 4 = 26. Add 4 (undo the −4 layer): 5x = 30. Divide by 5 (undo the ×5 layer): x = 6. Check: f(6) = 30 − 4 = 26 ✅
90. Sample answer: 2x + 3 = 17. How to build it: start with the answer 7, dress it in two layers — multiply by 2 to get 14, then add 3 to get 17 — and write the dressed-up form on the left: 2x + 3 = 17. Unwrapping it (subtract 3, divide by 2) must give 7 back, and it does. Any two layers work, so your equation may look different — verify yours by solving it!
91. Sample answer: 3x + 2 = 2x − 2. Verify: left side: 3 × (−4) + 2 = −10; right side: 2 × (−4) − 2 = −10. Equal! ✅ To build one yourself, put some x's and a number on the left, different x's and a number on the right, chosen so that −4 balances them — then always verify by plugging in.
92. Sample answer: x + 3 = x + 5. Why no x works: subtracting x from both sides leaves 3 = 5, which is false — the variable is gone, so nothing can ever fix it. Any equation where the x's cancel and the leftover sentence is false has no solution.
93. Sample answer: 2(x + 1) = 2x + 2. Why every x works: distributing gives 2x + 2 = 2x + 2, and subtracting 2x leaves 2 = 2, which is true no matter what x is — both sides are the same expression in two costumes (an identity).
94. x = 6 both ways. Method A: divide both sides by 6 → x − 2 = 4 → x = 6. Method B: distribute → 6x − 12 = 24 → add 12 → 6x = 36 → divide by 6 → x = 6. Both are legal because each step is the Golden Rule: dividing first undoes the outer layer, while distributing unwraps the parentheses first — two reverse orders that both end at x.
95. x = 0 or x = 5. Subtract 5x: x² − 5x = 0. Factor: x(x − 5) = 0. A product is zero only when a factor is zero: x = 0 or x − 5 = 0. Check: 0² = 5 × 0 ✅ and 5² = 25 = 5 × 5 ✅ (Dividing by x would have stolen the x = 0 solution!)
96. x = ±3. Add 9: x² = 9. Both 3² and (−3)² equal 9. ✅
97. x = ±6. Subtract 4: x² = 36. Both 6² and (−6)² equal 36. Check: 36 + 4 = 40 ✅
98. Equation: s² = 64, so s = ±8. The real answer is s = 8 meters. We reject s = −8 because a side length is a physical distance — it cannot be negative. The algebra offers two answers; the real world keeps one.
99. Multiplying by −1 mirrors every number across zero on the number line, and the mirror swaps which side is bigger. Demonstration: 3 < 5 is true. Multiply both by −1: you get −3 and −5. On the number line −3 sits to the RIGHT of −5, so −3 is greater: −3 > −5. The only way to keep the statement true was to flip the sign. No flip, and you would be claiming −3 < −5, which is false.
100. (a) Equation: 2w + 2(w + 4) = 36 (two widths plus two lengths make the perimeter, and the length is w + 4). (b) Distribute: 2w + 2w + 8 = 36. Combine like terms: 4w + 8 = 36. Subtract 8: 4w = 28. Divide by 4: w = 7. (c) Width = 7 cm, length = 7 + 4 = 11 cm. Check: 2 × 7 + 2 × 11 = 14 + 22 = 36 ✅
