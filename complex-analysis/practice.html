i² = −1   (the one rule!)
a + bi lives at (a, b):  real part a, imaginary part b
add/subtract pile by pile — never mix the piles
×i = quarter turn;  the cycle goes 1 → i → −1 → −i → 1 ...
multiply: four baby multiplications; i² flips a sign
|a + bi| = √(a² + b²)      conjugate of a + bi is a − bi
(a + bi)(a − bi) = a² + b²

Grab a pencil and paper. Start with the easy ones — they use the exact same patterns from the lessons. A few problems are open-ended: they have more than one right answer. Don't peek at the answer key until you've tried!

Hint for every problem: first ask yourself, "Is this a SLIDE (adding), a SPIN (multiplying by i), or a spin-and-stretch (multiplying anything else)?"

Problems 87–88 — Word problems!

  • 87.
    The spinning robot. A robot's position on the complex map starts
    at 2 + i. Every minute, its position is multiplied by i.
    (a) Where is it after 1 minute? (b) After 2 minutes?
    (c) After 4 minutes? (d) After 9 minutes? (Ride the cycle —
    don't compute all nine!)
  • 88.
    The mirror patrol. Let z = 3 + 4i. (a) How far is z from zero?
    (b) How far is its conjugate from zero? (c) Compute z plus its
    own conjugate. (d) Explain why ANY number plus its conjugate is
    always real.

🔴 CHALLENGE (Problems 91–100)

  • 91.
    The spiral finale — powers of 1 + i. (a) Compute (1 + i)².
    (b) Compute (1 + i)³ (multiply your (a) answer by 1 + i).
    (c) Compute (1 + i)⁴ (square your (a) answer).
    (d) List the lengths of 1 + i and your three answers: √2, then
    what? What happens to the length each step? And the angle:
    45°, then what? What happens to the angle each step?
    (e) Use the pattern to predict (1 + i)⁸ — then CHECK it by
    squaring your (c) answer.
  • 92.
    Design a spin story — open-ended! Pick ANY starting point on the
    complex map. Multiply by i three times, writing down where you
    land each time, and tell the story (a robot, a dancer, a
    rocket...). What would a fourth multiplication do?
  • 93.
    The impossible equation, solved twice. (a) Solve x² = −9 — there
    are TWO answers! Check them both. (b) Solve x² = −25.
    (c) How many solutions does x² = −1 have? Name them.
  • 94.
    The mystery multiplier. The number 2 + i is multiplied by a
    mystery number w and lands at 1 + 3i. (a) Show w is NOT i:
    check where i sends 2 + i first. (b) Show that w = 1 + i works —
    multiply it out. (c) Bonus: where does that same w send 3 − i?
    (d) Super bonus: what mystery number sends 1 + i to 2i?
  • 95.
    The length law — open-ended discovery! Compute |1 + i| and
    |2 − i|, and multiply them. Then multiply the NUMBERS:
    (1 + i)(2 − i) — and find the length of the answer. What do you
    notice? Now invent your OWN pair of numbers and test whether
    |z · w| = |z| · |w| holds again.
  • 96.
    Conjugate detective — open-ended discovery! Let z = 1 + 2i and
    w = 3 − i. (a) Compute the conjugate of (z + w). Then compute
    conj(z) + conj(w). Same or different? (b) Compute the conjugate
    of (z · w). Then compute conj(z) · conj(w). Same or different?
    (c) In one or two sentences, state the two mirror laws you just
    discovered.
  • 97.
    The pure-spin division. (a) Compute (1 + i) ÷ (1 − i).
    (b) The answer is PURELY imaginary — why? Hint: |1 + i| and
    |1 − i| are equal, so the division has no stretch in it, only
    spin. How big is the spin from direction −45° to direction +45°?
    (c) Check your answer: multiply it by (1 − i) — you should get
    back 1 + i.
  • 98.
    The four-spoke wheel. (a) Compute 1 + i + i² + i³.
    (b) Compute i⁴ + i⁵ + i⁶ + i⁷. (Factor out i⁴ first — what's
    left in the parentheses?) (c) Every block of four consecutive
    powers sums to the same thing. Use that: what is
    1 + i + i² + ... + i⁹⁹ (one hundred terms)? (d) What if the sum
    stops at i¹⁰¹ instead (one hundred two terms)?
  • 99.
    Every quadratic has roots! (a) Check that x = 1 + 2i solves
    x² − 2x + 5 = 0: compute x², then −2x, then add all three
    pieces. (b) Do it again for x = 1 − 2i. (c) Why did the
    imaginary parts cancel out to zero — and what does that tell you
    about how complex roots of real equations arrive?
  • 100.
    The grand finale — design it! Invent one problem whose answer
    is a SUM (a slide on the map) and one whose answer is a QUARTER
    TURN (a multiplication by i). Then solve both to make sure they
    work! Bonus: invent a problem whose answer is a length of 5.
    (Use problems 25, 41, and 65 as models.)
