⚡ Multiplying Anything — Four Baby Multiplications

📌 Key idea of this section

Every part × every part — then i² = −1
flips one sign and changes one pile into the other.

The rectangle with a trapdoor

How do you multiply (1 + 2i) × (3 + i)? The same way you'd multiply (1 + 2) × (3 + 1): every part times every part — four baby multiplications. Picture splitting a rectangle into four rooms.

(1 + 2i)(3 + i) = 1·3 + 1·i + 2i·3 + 2i·i
                = 3 + i + 6i + 2i²

Three of the rooms behave normally. But the last room has a trapdoor: it's 2i², and i² = −1, so that room drops out of the imaginary pile and lands in the REAL pile — with its sign flipped:

3 + i + 6i + 2i² = 3 + 7i − 2 = 1 + 7i

That's the entire method: four baby multiplications, combine the two i-rooms, and let the i²-room flip into the real pile.

Two famous cases

(1 + i)(1 + i) = 1 + i + i + i² = 1 + 2i − 1 = 2i

Squaring 1 + i gives PURE 2i — it landed exactly on the imaginary axis! (Remember this one. Lesson 6 explains the magic.)

(1 + i)(1 − i) = 1 − i + i − i² = 1 + 1 = 2

Two numbers that aren't real at all multiplied into a plain, honest 2. The imaginary rooms cancelled! (Remember this one too. Lesson 7 turns it into a division machine.)

Stretching without spinning

Multiplying by a plain REAL number has no i²-room at all:

3 · (2 + i) = 6 + 3i

The arrow from 0 to (2, 1) just got 3 times longer: same direction, triple the length. Real numbers STRETCH; i SPINS. Every multiplication is some recipe of spinning and stretching — and that's the secret waiting in Lesson 6.
