📌 Everything, one last time

i² = −1 — one invented rule opens a whole new number world
a + bi lives at (a, b) — right for real, up for imaginary
add pile by pile; multiply four rooms with one trapdoor
×i = quarter turn; lengths multiply, angles ADD
twins (conjugates) multiply to a² + b² — and make division work

The recap list

  • ·
    No real number squares to −1 — so we invented i, with the single rule i² = −1.
  • ·
    Complex numbers a + bi fill a whole MAP: real axis across, imaginary axis up.
  • ·
    Adding slides arrows tip to tail — real piles with real, imaginary with imaginary.
  • ·
    Multiplying by i is a quarter turn; the cycle 1, i, −1, −i repeats every four.
  • ·
    Any multiplication: four baby multiplications; the i²-room flips into the real pile.
  • ·
    Every number is a length (the modulus, by Pythagoras) and a direction (an angle). Multiply: lengths multiply, angles add. Squaring doubles the angle.
  • ·
    The conjugate mirrors across the real axis; twins multiply to a² + b², which makes division possible.

The magic sentence

Adding slides.
Multiplying spins and stretches.
And i is the quarter turn that started it all.

Say it out loud three times. Seriously! That's the whole lesson in three lines.

The treasure chest

One last thing to grow into. In the 1700s, Leonhard Euler pushed these ideas further than anyone and found this:

e^(iπ) + 1 = 0

Five superstars of math — 0, 1, π, e, and our new friend i — squeezed into seven symbols. You don't know e yet (you will, and it's wonderful), but you already understand the heart of it: this equation is about spinning, π is about half a turn, and a half turn from 1 lands exactly at −1. Even the most famous equation in mathematics is, deep down, the quarter-turn number doing its dance. Keep going in math, and complex analysis will be waiting for you — with the proof and everything.
