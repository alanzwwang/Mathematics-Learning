        i × i = −1

Those three lines are complex analysis in action. The first line breaks the oldest rule in arithmetic — "nothing squares to a negative" — by inventing a new number that does exactly that. The second looks innocent, until you notice something spooky: multiplying spun the number a quarter turn. The third says a number built from 3 and 4 sits 5 steps from home — a clue that these new numbers have a length and a direction, like arrows. Right now those lines might look mysterious. That's okay! By the end of this lesson, you'll understand all three — and you'll know exactly why each one works.

Why do people care? Because complex numbers are the math of SPINNING and STRETCHING — and the world is full of things that spin, wave, and wiggle. The electricity in your walls, the signals inside your phone, the lift under an airplane wing, the quantum rules inside every atom — all of it is computed with these numbers. They were invented about 500 years ago by Italian mathematicians who kept hitting equations with no answer, and the grown-up subject that grew from them — complex analysis — is often called the most beautiful math ever discovered. The best news: the big ideas are built from things you already know — adding coordinates, small fractions, and one 2,500-year-old triangle trick.

Lesson 1: The Number That Shouldn't Exist
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        Squaring a real number NEVER gives a negative.
        So x² = −1 has no real answer...
        until we invent one: i, with the single rule i² = −1.

The wall of squaring

Try to square your way to a negative number. Go ahead — try!

        3² = 9           (−3)² = 9
        (1/2)² = 1/4     0² = 0

Positive times positive is positive. Negative times negative is positive. Zero times zero is zero. There is simply no way — no real number can square to −1. For most of human history, that was the end of the story: x² = −1 was called impossible.

Invented numbers, a proud tradition

But here's a secret: mathematicians have invented their way out of "impossible" many times before.

  · "What is 3 − 5?" Once upon a time: impossible! Then NEGATIVE numbers were invented — and suddenly owing money made sense. People called them absurd.
  · "What is 1 ÷ 4?" Impossible, if only whole numbers exist! Then FRACTIONS were invented — and sharing one pizza among four friends became a number: 1/4.
  · "What is x, if x² = −1?" Impossible... until the 1500s, when Italian mathematicians, racing to solve harder equations, dared to write down a new number.

Each invention felt fake at first — and each became as normal as breakfast. The pattern: when an equation has no answer, don't give up. Invent the answer.

The one rule

The new number is called i (short for "imaginary unit" — a terrible name, given as an insult, that stuck). It obeys exactly ONE special rule:

        i² = −1

Everything else about i is ordinary arithmetic. It adds, it multiplies, it follows all the usual rules. It just also happens to be the answer to the impossible equation.

A complex number is any number with both kinds of parts:

        3 + 2i   ←  real part: 3,   imaginary part: 2

If the imaginary part is 0, you're looking at an ordinary real number wearing a disguise: 7 = 7 + 0i. So the real numbers live INSIDE the complex numbers — the same way the whole numbers live inside the fractions. Nothing you knew is gone; the number world just got bigger.

✏️ Your Turn

(a) What is the imaginary part of 5 − 4i? (Careful — signs come along for the ride!) (b) Is 7 a complex number?

Answers: (a) −4 — the minus sign is part of the imaginary part. (b) Yes: 7 = 7 + 0i. Every real number is a complex number with imaginary part 0.

Lesson 2: The Complex Map — Right for Real, Up for Imaginary
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        a + bi lives at the point (a, b):
        a steps right, then b steps up.

One line is too small

Where does i go on the number line? Trick question: NOWHERE. Every spot on the line is already taken by a real number, and i isn't one. That's not a problem — it's a hint. If i won't fit on the line, give it a whole MAP.

Here's how. A complex number has two parts, and you already know something else with two parts: coordinates! So let 3 + 2i live at the point (3, 2) — go right 3 for the real part, up 2 for the imaginary part:

                imaginary
                    ▲
                2 ─ ┤         • 3 + 2i
                    │
                1 ─ • i
                    │
      ──────•───────┼─────────•──────► real
            −2      │         3
                    │
               −1 ─ ┤
                    │
               −2 ─ • −2i

Look around the map:

  · The flat horizontal line is the REAL axis — every ordinary number lives there: 4 sits at (4, 0), −2 sits at (−2, 0).
  · The tall vertical line is the IMAGINARY axis — the pure imaginary numbers live there: i at (0, 1), −2i at (0, −2).
  · Everything off both lines — like 3 + 2i — is a full-blown complex number, with a foot in both worlds.
  · Zero sits at the crossing, the center of everything.

Arrows from home

One more way to see a complex number — and it's the way that matters most later. Draw an ARROW from 0 out to the point. Then 3 + 2i isn't just a dot; it's a journey: 3 east, 2 north. Every complex number is an arrow with a length and a direction. Keep that picture. Lesson 6 blows it wide open.

✏️ Your Turn

(a) Where does −1 + 2i live? (b) What number lives at (4, 0)?

Answers: (a) At the point (−1, 2) — left 1, up 2. (b) Just plain 4 — a real number on the floor line.

Lesson 3: Adding and Subtracting — Pile by Pile
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        Add the real parts. Add the imaginary parts.
        (a + bi) + (c + di) = (a + c) + (b + d)i

Apples and oranges

Think of 3 + 2i as two piles: 3 in the real pile, 2 in the imaginary pile. You can never merge the piles into one — 3 + 2i is already as simple as it gets, the same way "3 apples and 2 oranges" isn't 5 of anything.

But two complex numbers CAN be combined — pile by pile:

        (2 + 3i) + (1 + 4i) = 3 + 7i

Real pile: 2 + 1 = 3. Imaginary pile: 3 + 4 = 7. That's the whole rule!

Fractions work too — pile by pile, exactly like the Fractions lesson:

        (1/2 + i) + (3/2 + 2i) = 2 + 3i     (because 1/2 + 3/2 = 2)

Sliding arrows

Watch the same addition on the map, with arrows. Start at 0, walk the first arrow (right 2, up 3), then walk the second arrow from there (right 1, up 4). Where do you end up? Right 3, up 7 — the answer! Adding complex numbers is joining arrows tip to tail: a SLIDE across the map. Nothing spins, nothing stretches — you just end up somewhere new.

Subtracting is the same game, with signs to watch:

        (5 + 2i) − (3 + 7i) = 2 − 5i

Real: 5 − 3 = 2. Imaginary: 2 − 7 = −5. Yes, imaginary parts can go negative — the pile can owe!

And every number has an opposite that cancels it back to zero: the opposite of 2 − 3i is −2 + 3i, because (2 − 3i) + (−2 + 3i) = 0. The opposite arrow is the same length, turned all the way around.

✏️ Your Turn

(a) (4 + i) + (1 + 3i) = ? (b) (2 + 3/2 i) − (1 + 1/2 i) = ?

Answers: (a) 5 + 4i. (b) 1 + i — the real parts give 1, and 3/2 − 1/2 = 1.

Lesson 4: Multiplying by i — The Quarter Turn
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        Multiplying by i turns every number
        a quarter turn counterclockwise.

The four-step spin cycle

Start at 1 and multiply by i, over and over. Use the one rule, i² = −1, whenever it shows up:

        1 → i → i² = −1 → i³ = −i → i⁴ = 1 → i⁵ = i → ...

(Slow motion: i³ = i² × i = −1 × i = −i. And i⁴ = i² × i² = (−1) × (−1) = 1.)

Four multiplications, and you're back home at 1! Now find those answers on the map:

        1  is at (1, 0)  — EAST
        i  is at (0, 1)  — NORTH
        −1 is at (−1, 0) — WEST
        −i is at (0, −1) — SOUTH

East, north, west, south, east... Each multiplication by i spins the arrow a QUARTER TURN counterclockwise. That's why i⁴ = 1: four quarter turns make one full turn. The impossible number is a spinning machine!

Riding the cycle

The answers repeat every four: 1, i, −1, −i, 1, i, −1, −i, ... So you can simplify ANY power of i by counting how many complete spins to throw away — remainders, just like the Number Theory lesson!

        i¹⁰ = ?   10 = 4 × 2 + 2,  so throw away 2 full turns:
        i¹⁰ = i² = −1

Everything spins, not just 1

The quarter turn works on every number:

        i · 3 = 3i              (3, 0) → (0, 3)      east to north ✅
        i · 2i = −2             (0, 2) → (−2, 0)     north to west ✅
        i · (1 + i) = −1 + i    (1, 1) → (−1, 1)     diagonal, spun ✅

(Sharp eyes: a quarter turn sends the point (x, y) to (−y, x). Check it on all three!)

And the most useful spin fact of all: TWO quarter turns = a half turn = multiplying by −1. Of course — that's just i² = −1 said in spinning language. The one rule was a rotation rule all along.

✏️ Your Turn

(a) i · 5i = ? (b) Use the cycle: i²³ = ? (How many full turns can you throw away?)

Answers: (a) 5i² = −5. (b) 23 = 4 × 5 + 3, so i²³ = i³ = −i.

Lesson 5: Multiplying Anything — Four Baby Multiplications
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        Every part × every part — then i² = −1
        flips one sign and changes one pile into the other.

The rectangle with a trapdoor

How do you multiply (1 + 2i) × (3 + i)? The same way you'd multiply (1 + 2) × (3 + 1): every part times every part — four baby multiplications. Picture splitting a rectangle into four rooms.

        (1 + 2i)(3 + i) = 1·3 + 1·i + 2i·3 + 2i·i
                        = 3 + i + 6i + 2i²

Three of the rooms behave normally. But the last room has a trapdoor: it's 2i², and i² = −1, so that room drops out of the imaginary pile and lands in the REAL pile — with its sign flipped:

        3 + i + 6i + 2i² = 3 + 7i − 2 = 1 + 7i

That's the entire method: four baby multiplications, combine the two i-rooms, and let the i²-room flip into the real pile.

Two famous cases

        (1 + i)(1 + i) = 1 + i + i + i² = 1 + 2i − 1 = 2i

Squaring 1 + i gives PURE 2i — it landed exactly on the imaginary axis! (Remember this one. Lesson 6 explains the magic.)

        (1 + i)(1 − i) = 1 − i + i − i² = 1 + 1 = 2

Two numbers that aren't real at all multiplied into a plain, honest 2. The imaginary rooms cancelled! (Remember this one too. Lesson 7 turns it into a division machine.)

Stretching without spinning

Multiplying by a plain REAL number has no i²-room at all:

        3 · (2 + i) = 6 + 3i

The arrow from 0 to (2, 1) just got 3 times longer: same direction, triple the length. Real numbers STRETCH; i SPINS. Every multiplication is some recipe of spinning and stretching — and that's the secret waiting in Lesson 6.

✏️ Your Turn

(a) (2 + i)(1 + 2i) = ? (The answer lands on an axis!) (b) 2 · (3 − i) = ?

Answers: (a) 2 + 4i + i + 2i² = 5i — pure imaginary, straight up the axis! (b) 6 − 2i.

Lesson 6: Length and Direction — The Spin-and-Stretch Secret
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        Every complex number is a LENGTH and a DIRECTION.
        When you multiply: lengths multiply, angles ADD.

How far from home?

Every arrow has a length — its distance from 0. Mathematicians write it with bars: |3 + 4i| means "the length of 3 + 4i."

And here's where a 2,500-year-old friend appears. The arrow to (3, 4) is the diagonal of a 3-by-4 right triangle, so Pythagoras does the measuring:

        |3 + 4i| = √(3² + 4²) = √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5

(There it is — the third line from the welcome page!) The length of a + bi is √(a² + b²), and it has a fancy name: the modulus. Some lengths come out whole — |6 + 8i| = 10, |4 + 3i| = 5 — and some don't: |1 + i| = √2, which is fine; √2 just means "the number that squares to 2."

Every arrow also has a direction — the angle it makes with the real axis. East is 0°, i points north at 90°, −1 points west at 180°, and 1 + i splits the difference at 45° — halfway between east and north, since its two parts are equal.

The secret

Now the punchline of the whole lesson. Watch lengths and angles when numbers multiply. Take (1 + i) × (1 + i) = 2i, from Lesson 5:

        lengths:  |1 + i| × |1 + i| = √2 × √2 = 2 = |2i| ✅
        angles:   45° + 45° = 90° — and 2i really does point north ✅

It works for i × i = −1 too: lengths 1 × 1 = 1 ✅, angles 90° + 90° = 180° ✅. Multiplying complex numbers MULTIPLIES the lengths and ADDS the angles. Spin and stretch — that's all multiplication ever was!

This is why squaring 1 + i landed so neatly: squaring doubles the angle (45° → 90°) and squares the length (√2 → 2). Cubing would triple the angle. Grown-ups call this De Moivre's idea, and it turns impossible-looking power problems into simple angle arithmetic.

And here's the honest name-drop: when multiplication is this smooth — every product a graceful spin-and-stretch — the whole calculus of these numbers becomes smooth too. THAT deep, smooth, spinning subject is complex analysis. You're standing in its front yard.

✏️ Your Turn

(a) |4 + 3i| = ? (b) z points at 30° and w points at 60°. What direction does z · w point?

Answers: (a) √(16 + 9) = √25 = 5. (b) 30° + 60° = 90° — straight up the imaginary axis.

Lesson 7: The Mirror Trick — Conjugates and Division
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        The conjugate flips the imaginary sign: a + bi ↔ a − bi.
        (a + bi)(a − bi) = a² + b² — always real!

The mirror twin

Every complex number has a mirror twin across the real axis: the conjugate of 2 + 3i is 2 − 3i. Same real part, flipped imaginary part — the arrow reflects from north-of-the-line to south-of-it.

Now multiply the twins and watch the miracle:

        (2 + 3i)(2 − 3i) = 4 − 6i + 6i − 9i²
                         = 4 + 9
                         = 13 = 2² + 3²

The middle rooms cancel (−6i and +6i), and the trapdoor flips the last sign (+9 instead of −9). Always:

        (a + bi)(a − bi) = a² + b²

Notice: a² + b² is exactly the number under the √ in the length formula. So twins multiply to the SQUARED length — a pure, positive, real number. The imaginary parts destroy each other.

Dividing: keep, change... mirror!

What could (1 + 3i) ÷ (1 + i) possibly mean? The same thing it always means: WHAT NUMBER times (1 + i) gives (1 + 3i)?

The trick is a cousin of keep-change-flip: multiply top and bottom by the conjugate of the bottom. The bottom turns real, and real division is easy:

            1 + 3i       (1 + 3i)(1 − i)     4 + 2i
            ──────   =   ───────────────   =  ─────  =  2 + i
            1 + i        (1 + i)(1 − i)        2

Top: (1 + 3i)(1 − i) = 1 − i + 3i − 3i² = 4 + 2i. Bottom: (1 + i)(1 − i) = 1 + 1 = 2.

Check it backwards: does (2 + i)(1 + i) = 1 + 3i? 2 + 2i + i + i² = 1 + 3i ✅. Division works!

So the full toolbox is complete: add, subtract, multiply, divide. The complex numbers are a whole self-contained number world — nothing you compute ever kicks you out of it.

✏️ Your Turn

(a) What is the conjugate of 4 − i? (b) Compute (4 + i)(4 − i).

Answers: (a) 4 + i. (b) 16 + 1 = 17 — twins always multiply to a² + b².

Lesson 8: Watch Out! Common Mistakes
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Keep the big ideas in sight:

        Real and imaginary are separate piles.
        i² = −1 — the minus sign is the whole point.
        Multiplying spins and stretches; it never just "gets bigger."

Students mix up these five ideas every single year. Learn them now, and they won't catch you!

Mistake 1: "i is just a mystery letter, like x"

        i works like any variable?   ❌

x can stand for anything — that's its job. i is ONE specific number with ONE special rule: i² = −1. Treat it like a number that carries a tiny superpower, not like a placeholder. When you see i², something always happens: it becomes −1. ✅

Mistake 2: "i² = 1, because squaring kills the minus"

        i squared... signs cancel, so 1?   ❌

i² = −1 BY DEFINITION — it's the one rule, the reason i exists. The flip is the whole point. (And a sneaky bonus trap: (−i)² = (−1)² · i² = −1 too — BOTH i and −i square to −1!) ✅

Mistake 3: "3 + 2i simplifies to 5"

        Merge the piles: 3 + 2i = 5? or 5i?   ❌

Apples plus oranges isn't 5 of anything. Real and imaginary are separate piles forever — 3 + 2i is already fully simplified. You can only combine real with real and imaginary with imaginary, during an addition. ✅

Mistake 4: "|3 + 4i| = 3 + 4 = 7"

        Length = add the parts?   ❌

Length is the DIAGONAL of the right triangle, and diagonals come from Pythagoras, not from adding: |3 + 4i| = √(9 + 16) = 5. (Adding the parts measures a staircase, not the arrow.) ✅

Mistake 5: "Imaginary means fake"

        These numbers are just pretend?   ❌

The name was literally an insult — the philosopher Descartes coined "imaginary" to mock them, and it stuck. But the electric grid powering your home, the wings of the plane overhead, the Wi-Fi in your room, and the quantum rules inside every atom are all computed with i. Imaginary is a name, not a verdict. ✅

Lesson 9: Review — The Big Picture
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Everything, one last time:

        i² = −1 — one invented rule opens a whole new number world
        a + bi lives at (a, b) — right for real, up for imaginary
        add pile by pile; multiply four rooms with one trapdoor
        ×i = quarter turn; lengths multiply, angles ADD
        twins (conjugates) multiply to a² + b² — and make division work

The recap list

  · No real number squares to −1 — so we invented i, with the single rule i² = −1.
  · Complex numbers a + bi fill a whole MAP: real axis across, imaginary axis up.
  · Adding slides arrows tip to tail — real piles with real, imaginary with imaginary.
  · Multiplying by i is a quarter turn; the cycle 1, i, −1, −i repeats every four.
  · Any multiplication: four baby multiplications; the i²-room flips into the real pile.
  · Every number is a length (the modulus, by Pythagoras) and a direction (an angle). Multiply: lengths multiply, angles add. Squaring doubles the angle.
  · The conjugate mirrors across the real axis; twins multiply to a² + b², which makes division possible.

The magic sentence

        Adding slides.
        Multiplying spins and stretches.
        And i is the quarter turn that started it all.

Say it out loud three times. Seriously! That's the whole lesson in three lines.

The treasure chest

One last thing to grow into. In the 1700s, Leonhard Euler pushed these ideas further than anyone and found this:

        e^(iπ) + 1 = 0

Five superstars of math — 0, 1, π, e, and our new friend i — squeezed into seven symbols. You don't know e yet (you will, and it's wonderful), but you already understand the heart of it: this equation is about spinning, π is about half a turn, and a half turn from 1 lands exactly at −1. Even the most famous equation in mathematics is, deep down, the quarter-turn number doing its dance. Keep going in math, and complex analysis will be waiting for you — with the proof and everything.
