Lesson 1: Undoing Operations — How to Solve an Equation
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        An equation is a balanced scale. To find the mystery number x,
        UNDO whatever was done to it — last move first —
        doing the same thing to BOTH sides.

        3x + 4 = 19   →   3x = 15   →   x = 5

Meet the mystery number

A variable is a letter that stands for a number we don't know yet (or a number that can change). We'll use x most of the time. Writing 3x means "3 times x" — the × sign goes into hiding, because x is too important to be confused with ×.

An equation is a sentence claiming two things are equal:

        3x + 4 = 19

Read it as: "some mystery number, tripled, plus 4, is 19." Solving the equation means finding which value of x makes the sentence true.

The balance rule

Here is the one law that runs everything in this lesson — honestly, in all of algebra:

        Whatever you do to one side, do to the other.

An equation is a balanced scale. Add 5 to the left pan only — it tips. Add 5 to both pans — still balanced. Every legal move keeps the balance, and every step you take exists to get x alone on one side.

Undo in reverse order

What was done to x in 3x + 4? Look through the eyes of the order of operations: x was first multiplied by 3, then 4 was added. To unwrap x, undo the LAST move first — like unwrapping a present: the last tape strip on is the first one off.

Example 1: Solve 3x + 4 = 19.

        3x + 4 = 19
        3x + 4 − 4 = 19 − 4     (undo the +4: subtract 4 from BOTH sides)
        3x = 15                 (the 4 − 4 cancels — that was the point)
        3x ÷ 3 = 15 ÷ 3         (undo the ×3: divide BOTH sides by 3)
        x = 5

The checkback — your free lie detector. Plug 5 back into the ORIGINAL equation: 3(5) + 4 = 15 + 4 = 19 ✅. Ten seconds, total certainty. This is the old arithmetic wisdom — every operation has an undo partner — grown up into a superpower.

Example 2: Solve x/2 − 3 = 1.  (x/2 means x ÷ 2.)

        x/2 − 3 = 1
        x/2 − 3 + 3 = 1 + 3     (undo the −3: add 3 to BOTH sides)
        x/2 = 4
        2 · (x/2) = 2 · 4       (undo the ÷2: multiply BOTH sides by 2)
        x = 8

        Check: 8/2 − 3 = 4 − 3 = 1 ✅

Notice the order: we undid the SUBTRACT before the DIVIDE — the reverse of the order of operations, every time. PEMDAS builds the expression; anti-PEMDAS takes it apart.

Backwards word problems

"I'm thinking of a number. I multiply it by 5, subtract 7, and get 13. What's my number?" Translate the words into an equation — 5x − 7 = 13 — then undo:

        5x − 7 = 13
        5x = 20          (add 7 to both sides — undoes the −7)
        x = 4            (divide both sides by 5 — undoes the ×5)

        Check: 5(4) − 7 = 20 − 7 = 13 ✅

✏️ Your Turn

(a) 2x + 9 = 17   (b) 4x − 3 = 9   (c) x/3 + 5 = 8

Answers: (a) subtract 9: 2x = 8; divide by 2: x = 4. Check: 2(4) + 9 = 17 ✅. (b) add 3: 4x = 12; divide by 4: x = 3. Check: 4(3) − 3 = 9 ✅. (c) subtract 5: x/3 = 3; multiply by 3: x = 9. Check: 9/3 + 5 = 8 ✅.

Lesson 2: Order of Operations with Variables — and f(x)
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        PEMDAS still rules: parentheses, powers, ×÷ left to right, +− left to right.
        To EVALUATE an expression, substitute the value inside parentheses,
        then follow the order, step by step.

        f(x) = 3x + 2   means:   "whatever you feed me, triple it and add 2."

Substitution: swapping the letter for a number

Evaluating an expression means finding its value for a particular x. The golden rule of substitution:

        Replace the variable with (parentheses around the value).

The parentheses are not decoration — they protect you from sign disasters and order mix-ups, as you're about to see.

Example 1: Evaluate 2x² − 3x + 4 when x = 3.

        2(3)² − 3(3) + 4        (substitute: every x becomes (3))
        = 2 · 9 − 3 · 3 + 4     (powers first: (3)² = 9)
        = 18 − 9 + 4            (multiplications next: 2 · 9 = 18 and 3 · 3 = 9)
        = 9 + 4                 (+ and − tie, so left to right: 18 − 9 = 9)
        = 13

Watch what the parentheses prevented: 2 · 9 is NOT "18² then something" — and 3x with x = 3 is 3 · 3 = 9, not 33!

Function notation: naming the rule

A function is a rule that takes an input and produces exactly one output. We write it:

        f(x) = 2x + 3

Read aloud: "f of x equals 2x plus 3." The letter f is the rule's name; the x in parentheses is the input slot. f(4) means "the output when 4 goes in":

        f(4) = 2(4) + 3 = 8 + 3 = 11
        f(0) = 2(0) + 3 = 0 + 3 = 3
        f(−2) = 2(−2) + 3 = −4 + 3 = −1     (substituting a negative — parentheses doing their job)
        f(1/2) = 2(1/2) + 3 = 1 + 3 = 4

A table of inputs and outputs shows the pattern at a glance:

        x        0   1   2   3
        f(x)     3   5   7   9

Every time x grows by 1, f(x) grows by 2 — exactly the 2 sitting in front of the x. That number is the steepness of the rule (its official name is slope), and you just discovered it from a table.

Running a function BACKWARDS

Here's the honors move: don't just find outputs from inputs — find the input from the output. For what x is f(x) = 13?

        2x + 3 = 13           (set the rule equal to the target output)
        2x = 10               (subtract 3 from both sides)
        x = 5                 (divide both sides by 2)

        Check: f(5) = 2(5) + 3 = 13 ✅

A function question is secretly a Lesson 1 equation in disguise.

Feeding a function to a function

If f(x) = 2x + 3 and g(x) = x² − 1, what is f(g(2))? Work from the inside out:

        g(2) = (2)² − 1 = 4 − 1 = 3       (inside first)
        f(3) = 2(3) + 3 = 6 + 3 = 9       (feed that output into f)

So f(g(2)) = 9. This inside-out chaining is called composition, and it runs half of higher math.

✏️ Your Turn

Let h(x) = 3x − 4. Find: (a) h(5)   (b) h(0)   (c) h(2/3)   (d) the x for which h(x) = 8.

Answers: (a) 3(5) − 4 = 15 − 4 = 11. (b) 3(0) − 4 = −4. (c) 3(2/3) − 4 = 2 − 4 = −2. (d) 3x − 4 = 8 → 3x = 12 → x = 4. Check: h(4) = 12 − 4 = 8 ✅.

Lesson 3: The Laws of Exponents
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Key ideas of this section:

        Same base, multiplying: ADD exponents.       2³ × 2² = 2⁵
        Same base, dividing:   SUBTRACT exponents.   2⁵ ÷ 2² = 2³
        Power of a power:      MULTIPLY exponents.   (2²)³ = 2⁶
        Zero power: 2⁰ = 1.    Negative power: 2⁻³ = 1/2³ = 1/8

You know powers: 2⁵ = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 32, and the exponent counts the copies. Now we turn that one idea into five laws — and every law will be PROVEN from the copy-counting picture, never just declared.

Law 1: multiplying same-base powers — ADD the exponents

        2³ × 2² = (2 × 2 × 2) × (2 × 2) = 2⁵

Three copies meet two copies: five copies total. No multiplying of 2s required — the exponents do the adding:

        2³ × 2² = 2³⁺² = 2⁵ = 32         (check: 8 × 4 = 32 ✅)
        x³ · x⁴ = x³⁺⁴ = x⁷              (any base works, so variables work too)

Law 2: dividing same-base powers — SUBTRACT the exponents

        2⁵ ÷ 2² = 32 ÷ 4 = 8 = 2³

Why: five 2s on top, two 2s on the bottom — two pairs cancel, three survive:

        2⁵/2² = (2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2)/(2 × 2) = 2 × 2 × 2 = 2³
        x⁵/x² = x⁵⁻² = x³

Law 3: power of a power — MULTIPLY the exponents

        (2²)³ = 4³ = 64 = 2⁶

Why: (2²)³ means three copies of 2², and each 2² is two 2s — that's 3 × 2 = 6 copies:

        (2²)³ = (2 × 2) × (2 × 2) × (2 × 2) = 2⁶
        (x²)³ = x⁶

Law 4: the zero exponent — anything to the zero is 1

This one looks like a trick, so let's force it out into the open. Law 2 says:

        2³/2³ = 2³⁻³ = 2⁰

But 2³/2³ is a number divided by itself — that's 1. Both statements describe the same thing, so:

        2⁰ = 1

Any nonzero base to the zero power is 1. It has to be — the subtraction law demands it. (0⁰ is a special case mathematicians argue about; you can safely leave that fight for later.)

Law 5: negative exponents — flip to a fraction

Push Law 2 one step further, past zero:

        2³/2⁵ = 2³⁻⁵ = 2⁻²

But expand the copies: the three 2s on top cancel three of the five on the bottom, leaving two 2s downstairs: 2³/2⁵ = 1/2² = 1/4. So:

        2⁻² = 1/2² = 1/4
        2⁻ⁿ = 1/2ⁿ     (a negative exponent means "one over the positive power")
        3⁻² = 1/3² = 1/9

Now the exponent line makes sense in both directions: ...2⁻² = 1/4, 2⁻¹ = 1/2, 2⁰ = 1, 2¹ = 2, 2² = 4, 2³ = 8... each step right doubles, each step left halves. The laws didn't come from nowhere — they're one pattern, extended smoothly.

Powers of products

One more useful move. What's (2x)³? Expand:

        (2x)³ = (2x)(2x)(2x) = 2 · 2 · 2 · x · x · x = 8x³

The power lands on BOTH factors. Similarly (3x²)² = 9x⁴: the 3 squares to 9, and x² squares to x⁴ by Law 3.

Worked example: simplify (2x³)(3x²).

        (2x³)(3x²) = 2 · 3 · x³ · x²      (multiplication commutes — gather the parts)
        = 6 · x³⁺²                        (multiply the numbers, ADD the exponents — Law 1)
        = 6x⁵

Square roots: the square's undo partner

Every operation has an undo partner, and squaring's partner is the square root:

        √25 = 5    because    5² = 25
        √9 = 3     because    3² = 9

Some roots are not whole numbers: √2 is about 1.41, because 1.41² ≈ 2. Numbers like √2 can't be written as any fraction — they're called irrational, "no ratio." You can trap them between neighbors: 1² = 1 and 2² = 4, so √2 sits between 1 and 2.

✏️ Your Turn

(a) x⁶ ÷ x²   (b) (2³)²   (c) 5⁰   (d) 2⁻³   (e) (3x²)²

Answers: (a) x⁶⁻² = x⁴ (dividing — subtract exponents). (b) 2⁶ = 64 (power of a power — multiply exponents; check: 8² = 64 ✅). (c) 1 (any nonzero number to the zero power is 1). (d) 1/2³ = 1/8. (e) 9x⁴ (3² = 9, and (x²)² = x⁴ by Law 3).

Lesson 4: Distribute, Combine, Factor — The Properties at Work
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Key ideas of this section:

        Distributive: a(x + b) = ax + ab        3(x + 4) = 3x + 12
        Like terms: same letter, same power — combine their numbers.
        Factoring = distributing in reverse:    6x + 8 = 2(3x + 4)

The distributive law, with variables

You already know 7 × 13 = 7 × 10 + 7 × 3. The same law works when a variable moves in:

        3(x + 4) = 3x + 12

Why it's true: 3(x + 4) means three copies of (x + 4), which is (x + 4) + (x + 4) + (x + 4) = 3x + 12. Picture a 3-by-(x + 4) rectangle of dots sliced into a 3-by-x slab and a 3-by-4 slab. Multiplication spreads over addition — over EACH term inside.

Distributing with minus signs — glue the sign to the term

        5(x − 2) = 5x − 10
        −2(x + 3) = −2x − 6     (the −2 multiplies BOTH terms: −2 · x and −2 · 3)
        −(x − 5) = −x + 5       (a bare − out front means −1 times: −1 · x = −x, −1 · (−5) = +5)

That last line is the most-missed move in algebra class: minus a quantity flips BOTH signs inside.

Like terms: the fruit-salad rule

You can't simplify 2x + 3 — x's and plain numbers are different species (2 apples + 3 oranges stays 2 apples + 3 oranges). But same-species terms merge:

        4x + 3 + 2x + 1 = (4x + 2x) + (3 + 1) = 6x + 4

The middle step is the commutative law letting us reorder, then the distributive law in reverse: 4x + 2x = (4 + 2)x = 6x. Like terms = same variable, same power. (x and x² are NOT like terms — different powers, different species.)

With minus signs, the glue trick saves lives:

        7x − 2 − 3x − 5 = 7x + (−2) + (−3x) + (−5) = 4x − 7

Two roads, one answer

Evaluate 3(x + 4) at x = 5 two ways:

        Road 1 (inside first):      3(5 + 4) = 3 · 9 = 27
        Road 2 (distribute first):  3x + 12 = 3(5) + 12 = 15 + 12 = 27

Same answer — because the distributive law is a LAW, not a trick. Use whichever road is easier; smart solvers pick per problem.

Factoring: distributing in reverse

Undo's revenge: sometimes you want to UNDO a distribution — pull out what the terms share. It's called factoring, and the thing you pull out is the GCF (greatest common factor):

        6x + 8 = 2(3x + 4)        (2 is the biggest number dividing both 6 and 8)
        12x + 18 = 6(2x + 3)      (6 is the GCF of 12 and 18)

Check by distributing back: 6(2x + 3) = 12x + 18 ✅. Factoring becomes your main tool for quadratics in Lesson 9.

The double distribution — quadratics peek in

What is (x + 2)(x + 3)? Distribute TWICE: each term in the first pair multiplies each term in the second.

        (x + 2)(x + 3) = x · x + x · 3 + 2 · x + 2 · 3
        = x² + 3x + 2x + 6
        = x² + 5x + 6           (combine like terms)

A 4-box area picture proves it: split an (x + 2)-by-(x + 3) rectangle into x², 3x, 2x, and 6. The result is a quadratic — an expression with x² as its highest power. You'll meet them again in Lesson 9, where we run this machine backwards.

✏️ Your Turn

(a) Distribute: 4(x − 3).  (b) Combine like terms: 5x + 2 − x + 6.  (c) Factor: 8x + 12.  (d) Expand: (x + 1)(x + 4).

Answers: (a) 4x − 12. (b) (5x − x) + (2 + 6) = 4x + 8. (c) 4(2x + 3) — check: 4 · 2x + 4 · 3 = 8x + 12 ✅. (d) x² + 4x + x + 4 = x² + 5x + 4.

Lesson 5: Negative Numbers — The Rules, With Proofs
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Key ideas of this section:

        negative × negative = POSITIVE        (−3)(−4) = 12
        Division follows the same sign rules as multiplication.
        |x| = distance from zero:  |−5| = 5.
        −x means "the opposite of x" — and the opposite of a negative is positive.

Multiplying negatives: the pattern that forces the rule

You already know adding and subtracting below zero. Multiplying needs a reason, so let's build one. Follow the pattern down:

        (−2) × 3 = −6
        (−2) × 2 = −4
        (−2) × 1 = −2
        (−2) × 0 = 0
        (−2) × (−1) = ???

Each step down, the answer rises by 2. The next value MUST be 2 — the pattern breaks otherwise. So:

        (−2)(−1) = 2,   and in general   (−a)(−b) = ab

Same story with debts: removing 2 debts of 3 dollars each, written (−2) × (−3), makes you 6 dollars richer. And a second proof by opposites: −(−3) means "the opposite of −3," which is 3. Sign rules for multiplication: same signs → positive; different signs → negative.

Division inherits the rules

Division is multiplication's undo partner, so it follows the same sign logic:

        −12 ÷ 3 = −4        (because 3 × (−4) = −12 — checkback!)
        −12 ÷ (−4) = 3      (because (−4) × 3 = −12 ✅)
        12 ÷ (−4) = −3      (because (−4) × (−3) = 12 ✅)

Negatives meet fractions

        −1/2 + 3/4 = −2/4 + 3/4 = 1/4         (common denominator, then add)
        (−2/3)(3/4) = −6/12 = −1/2            (different signs → negative; then simplify)
        (−1/2) ÷ (1/4) = (−1/2) × 4 = −2      (÷ means × the flip — Lesson 6 has the details)

Absolute value: distance, not sign

The absolute value of a number is its distance from zero on the number line — and distance is never negative:

        |−5| = 5     |3| = 3     |3 − 8| = |−5| = 5

Think of | | as a "drop the sign" machine — but always do the inside work FIRST.

Equations with negative coefficients

        Example: −3x = 12
        −3x ÷ (−3) = 12 ÷ (−3)      (undo ×(−3) by dividing by −3 — the partner rule!)
        x = −4
        Check: −3(−4) = 12 ✅

        Example: −x + 4 = 9
        −x = 5                      (subtract 4 from both sides)
        x = −5                      (multiply both sides by −1 — remember, −x is (−1)x)
        Check: −(−5) + 4 = 5 + 4 = 9 ✅

        Example: x − (−2) = 7
        x + 2 = 7                   (subtracting a negative adds)
        x = 5
        Check: 5 − (−2) = 7 ✅

✏️ Your Turn

(a) (−4)(−6)   (b) −18 ÷ 3   (c) −2/3 + 1/6   (d) −2x + 1 = 7   (e) |−7| − |3|

Answers: (a) 24 (same signs → positive). (b) −6 (different signs → negative). (c) −4/6 + 1/6 = −3/6 = −1/2. (d) −2x = 6 → x = −3; check: −2(−3) + 1 = 7 ✅. (e) 7 − 3 = 4.

Lesson 6: Fractions Are Division — Exact Answers and Clearing
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Key ideas of this section:

        A fraction is division, frozen:  17 ÷ 5 = 17/5.  No remainder needed!
        To ÷ a fraction, × its flip (reciprocal):  (2/3) ÷ (4/5) = (2/3)(5/4) = 5/6.
        Clear fractions from an equation by multiplying EVERY term
        by a common denominator.

The upgrade: remainders become exact

The old arithmetic left leftovers: 17 ÷ 5 = 3 R 2. Algebra's answer is exact: 17/5 (which is 3 2/5). The remainder didn't vanish — it became the fraction 2/5, the leftover divided by the group size. From now on, "divide" means "give the exact fraction."

The four operations, fraction edition

        2/3 + 1/4 = 8/12 + 3/12 = 11/12       (common denominator 12, then add tops)
        5/6 − 1/4 = 10/12 − 3/12 = 7/12       (same story)
        (3/4)(2/5) = 6/20 = 3/10              (top × top, bottom × bottom, simplify)
        (2/3) ÷ (4/5) = (2/3)(5/4) = 10/12 = 5/6

That last move deserves a definition: the reciprocal of a fraction is its flip — the reciprocal of 4/5 is 5/4. Dividing by a fraction is multiplying by its reciprocal. Why it works: dividing by 4/5 means "divide by 4, then multiply by 5" — because splitting into fifths gives 5 times as many pieces as splitting into wholes: (2/3) ÷ 4 × 5 = (2/3) × (5/4).

Fractions with variables

        x/3 + x/6 = 2x/6 + x/6 = 3x/6 = x/2

Same common-denominator game — x/3 is 2x/6 in disguise. (Check with x = 6: 6/3 + 6/6 = 2 + 1 = 3, and x/2 = 3 ✅.)

Solving (a/b)x = c: the reciprocal strike

        (3/4)x = 9
        (4/3) · (3/4)x = 9 · (4/3)     (multiply both sides by the reciprocal — (4/3)(3/4) = 1)
        x = 12
        Check: (3/4)(12) = 9 ✅

Clearing fractions: the one-move wonder

Equations with several fractions have a magic first step — multiply EVERY term by a common denominator and watch the fractions vanish:

        x/2 + x/3 = 5
        6 · (x/2) + 6 · (x/3) = 6 · 5      (multiply EVERY term by 6 — both sides stay balanced)
        3x + 2x = 30                       (6/2 = 3, and 6/3 = 2 — fractions cleared!)
        5x = 30
        x = 6
        Check: 6/2 + 6/3 = 3 + 2 = 5 ✅

Warning: the multiplier lands on EVERY term, both sides. Miss one and the balance breaks.

Ratios: unknowns in proportion

A recipe mixes juice and water in the ratio 2:3, making 40 cups total. How much of each? The trick: call the parts 2x and 3x — automatically in a 2:3 ratio.

        2x + 3x = 40
        5x = 40
        x = 8
        juice = 2x = 16 cups,   water = 3x = 24 cups
        Check: 16 + 24 = 40 ✅  and  16:24 = 2:3 (divide both by 8) ✅

✏️ Your Turn

(a) 3/4 + 1/6   (b) (2/5)x = 6   (c) x/3 + x/4 = 7   (d) Split 28 in the ratio 1:3.

Answers: (a) 9/12 + 2/12 = 11/12. (b) x = 6 · (5/2) = 15; check: (2/5)(15) = 6 ✅. (c) multiply every term by 12: 4x + 3x = 84 → 7x = 84 → x = 12; check: 12/3 + 12/4 = 4 + 3 = 7 ✅. (d) x + 3x = 28 → x = 7, so 7 and 21; check: 7 + 21 = 28 ✅.

Lesson 7: Variables on Both Sides — and Two Mysteries at Once
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Key ideas of this section:

        Variables on both sides? Collect the x's on ONE side first:
        subtract the smaller x-term from BOTH sides.
        Two mysteries need two equations. Solve by substitution
        (swap one variable for its twin) or elimination (add the equations).

Gathering the x's

When x appears on both sides, one extra move comes first: subtract the smaller x-term from BOTH sides so all the x's gather on one side. Then it's a Lesson 1 problem.

Example 1: 5x − 3 = 2x + 9

        5x − 3 − 2x = 2x + 9 − 2x      (subtract 2x from BOTH sides — the x's gather left)
        3x − 3 = 9
        3x = 12                        (add 3 to both sides)
        x = 4                          (divide both sides by 3)
        Check: left = 5(4) − 3 = 17;   right = 2(4) + 9 = 17 ✅  (check BOTH sides!)

Example 2: 3(x − 2) = x + 4   (distribute FIRST when parentheses block the door)

        3x − 6 = x + 4                 (distribute the 3 over both terms)
        2x − 6 = 4                     (subtract x from both sides)
        2x = 10                        (add 6 to both sides)
        x = 5
        Check: left = 3(5 − 2) = 9;   right = 5 + 4 = 9 ✅

Two weird but wonderful cases

        2x + 4 = 2x + 4   →  subtract 2x: 4 = 4.  ALWAYS true — every x works (infinitely many solutions).
        2x + 4 = 2x + 7   →  subtract 2x: 4 = 7.  NEVER true — no x works (no solution).

If the x's vanish, the leftover sentence decides: true → all numbers; false → none.

Systems: two equations, two mysteries

One equation with two unknowns, like x + y = 10, has many answers (1 and 9, 2 and 8, ...). But add a second condition — say x − y = 4 — and usually exactly ONE pair survives both. Two equations used together are called a system.

Method 1: substitution. If y = x + 2 and x + y = 8, swap the y in the second equation for its equal, x + 2:

        x + (x + 2) = 8          (substitute: y IS x + 2)
        2x + 2 = 8
        2x = 6
        x = 3,   so  y = 3 + 2 = 5
        Check: 3 + 5 = 8 ✅  and  5 = 3 + 2 ✅

Method 2: elimination. Equals added to equals are equal — so adding two whole equations is legal:

         x + y = 10
         x − y = 4
        ─────────────
        2x      = 14           (the +y and −y eliminated each other — that was the plan)
        x = 7,   so  y = 10 − 7 = 3
        Check: 7 + 3 = 10 ✅  and  7 − 3 = 4 ✅

Word problems: where systems earn their pay

"A burger costs 3 dollars more than a drink. Together they cost 9 dollars." Let d = drink, b = burger:

        b = d + 3           (the burger is 3 more than the drink)
        b + d = 9           (together they cost 9)
        (d + 3) + d = 9     (substitute the first equation into the second)
        2d + 3 = 9
        2d = 6
        d = 3,   b = 6
        Check: 6 is 3 more than 3 ✅, and 6 + 3 = 9 ✅

✏️ Your Turn

(a) 7x − 4 = 3x + 8   (b) 2(x + 1) = x + 7   (c) x + y = 12 and x − y = 2   (d) y = 2x and x + y = 9

Answers: (a) 4x = 12 → x = 3 (check: 21 − 4 = 17 and 9 + 8 = 17 ✅). (b) 2x + 2 = x + 7 → x = 5 (check: 2(6) = 12 and 5 + 7 = 12 ✅). (c) add the equations: 2x = 14 → x = 7, y = 5 (check: 7 + 5 = 12 and 7 − 5 = 2 ✅). (d) x + 2x = 9 → 3x = 9 → x = 3, y = 6 (check: 6 = 2 · 3 ✅ and 3 + 6 = 9 ✅).

Lesson 8: Inequalities — Same Moves, One Dark Twist
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Key ideas of this section:

        Solve inequalities with the SAME undo moves as equations.
        Multiply or divide by a NEGATIVE? FLIP the sign:
        −2x > 6   →   x < −3
        The answer is a whole region of the number line, not one number.

From = to >

An inequality says one side is bigger: > (greater than), < (less than), ≥ (greater or equal), ≤ (less or equal). The good news: every undo move from Lesson 1 still works.

Example 1: x + 3 > 7

        x + 3 > 7
        x > 4             (subtract 3 from both sides — same balance logic)

The solution isn't one number — it's every number bigger than 4: 4.5, 10, a million. Picture it on the number line: an open circle at 4 (4 itself is NOT allowed) shaded to the right. Check two values: x = 5 gives 8 > 7 ✅; x = 4 gives 7 > 7 — false, correctly excluded.

Example 2: 2x − 5 ≤ 9

        2x − 5 ≤ 9
        2x ≤ 14           (add 5 to both sides)
        x ≤ 7             (divide by 2 — POSITIVE 2, so no flip)

Closed circle at 7 (7 IS allowed — the "or equal" part), shaded left.

The dark twist: negative multipliers flip the sign

Here's the one new rule, and it's famous: multiplying or dividing both sides by a NEGATIVE flips the inequality. Why? Multiplying by −1 is a mirror flip of the whole number line — every point lands on its opposite, so whatever was on the right ends up on the left:

        2 < 3   but   −2 > −3       (mirror image!)

Example 3: −2x > 6

        −2x > 6
        x < −3            (divide by −2 — and FLIP > to <)

        Check with x = −4 (inside the claimed region): −2(−4) = 8 > 6 ✅
        Check with x = −2 (outside it): −2(−2) = 4 > 6? No — correctly excluded ✅

Always check an inequality with a test value from your answer region — it's the checkback for this new world.

Dodging the flip (a second method — take both roads!)

Example 4: 5 − x ≥ 2

        Road 1 (flip):      −x ≥ −3  (subtract 5),  then  x ≤ 3  (multiply by −1, FLIP)
        Road 2 (no flip):   5 ≥ 2 + x  (add x to BOTH sides),  then  3 ≥ x  (subtract 2)

        Both say: x ≤ 3.   Check x = 0: 5 ≥ 2 ✅.   Check x = 4: 1 ≥ 2? No ✅.

Clever rearranging can dodge the flip entirely — but you must know the flip anyway, for the day there's no dodge.

✏️ Your Turn

Answers: (a) x < 6 (test x = 5: 1 < 2 ✅). (b) 3x ≥ 9 → x ≥ 3 (test x = 4: 13 ≥ 10 ✅). (c) multiply by −2 and flip: x < −6 (test x = −8: −(−8)/2 = 4 > 3 ✅). (d) divide by −3 and flip: x > −4 (test x = 0: 0 < 12 ✅).

Lesson 9: Quadratics Peek In — Two Answers Are Better Than One
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Key ideas of this section:

        x² = 49 has TWO solutions: x = 7 AND x = −7, because (−7)² = 49 too.
        If (something)(something) = 0, one of the somethings must be 0.
        x² + 5x + 6 = 0 factors into (x + 2)(x + 3) = 0  →  x = −2 or −3.

The two-answer surprise

Solve x² = 49. Obviously x = 7... but also x = −7, because (−7)(−7) = 49 (Lesson 5's sign rule!). So:

        x² = 49   →   x = ±7          (± means "plus or minus" — both answers)

        x² = 9/4   →   x = ±3/2       (fractions play too: (3/2)² = 9/4 ✅)

The √ symbol means only the positive root — the equation x² = 49 is where BOTH roots live.

The zero product property

Here's a law so simple it's easy to miss: if a product is zero, at least one factor is zero.

        If A · B = 0, then A = 0 or B = 0.

(No two nonzero numbers multiply to 0 — there's no trick exception.) Watch it work:

        (x − 2)(x − 5) = 0
        x − 2 = 0   or   x − 5 = 0
        x = 2   or   x = 5
        Check x = 2: (0)(−3) = 0 ✅    Check x = 5: (3)(0) = 0 ✅

Factoring a quadratic — Lesson 4's machine in reverse

Lesson 4 built (x + 2)(x + 3) = x² + 5x + 6. Now run it backwards: given x² + 5x + 6 = 0, find the factor pairs. We need two numbers that multiply to 6 and add to 5. Hunt the factor pairs of 6: (1, 6)? Sum 7, no. (2, 3)? Sum 5, yes!

        x² + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3) = 0
        x + 2 = 0   or   x + 3 = 0
        x = −2   or   x = −3
        Check x = −2: 4 − 10 + 6 = 0 ✅    Check x = −3: 9 − 15 + 6 = 0 ✅

The recipe: find two numbers with product = the constant and sum = the x-coefficient; those complete the factor pairs.

The U-shape

What does a quadratic LOOK like? Table f(x) = x²:

        x        −2  −1   0   1   2
        f(x)      4   1   0   1   4

Both sides climb symmetrically — the graph is a U called a parabola. Satellite dishes and fountain arcs are parabolas. (When you take Algebra, you'll meet the quadratic formula, which solves ANY quadratic — even unfactorable ones. Consider this lesson a trailer for that movie.)

Lesson 10: Watch Out! Common Mistakes
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Keep the big ideas in sight:

        Same moves to both sides — EVERY term. The exponent laws.
        Minus signs glue and flip. Inequalities flip for negative multipliers.
        x² = k has two roots.

Six mistakes claim the most points on algebra tests every year. Each one below has a face you'll recognize — learn them now, and they'll never catch you.

Mistake 1: Operating on only part of a side

        x/2 + 3 = 7   →   multiply by 2:  x + 3 = 14?   ❌

The 2 must land on EVERY term of the left side: x + 6 = 14, so x = 8. ✅ (Check: 8/2 + 3 = 7 ✅.) Whatever you do to a side, you do to the WHOLE side.

Mistake 2: Inventing exponent laws

        2³ × 2² = 4⁵? ❌ (bases multiplied!)    2³ × 2² = 2⁶? ❌ (exponents multiplied!)

Same-base products keep the base and ADD the exponents: 2⁵ = 32 ✅ (check: 8 × 4 = 32). And the twin trap: (x²)³ = x⁵? ❌ — a power of a power MULTIPLIES exponents: x⁶ ✅.

Mistake 3: The minus-sign slip in distribution

        −2(x − 3) = −2x − 6?   ❌

The −2 multiplies BOTH terms: −2 · x = −2x and −2 · (−3) = +6, giving −2x + 6 ✅. A bare minus out front obeys the same rule: −(x − 5) = −x + 5, not −x − 5. (Quick check with x = 8: −(8 − 5) = −3, and −8 + 5 = −3 ✅.)

Mistake 4: Combining unlike terms

        2x + 3 = 5x?   ❌

Different species don't merge — 2x + 3 is finished. Only like terms combine: 2x + 3x = 5x ✅. And x + x² is NOT 2x² — different powers, different species.

Mistake 5: Forgetting the inequality flip

        −3x < 12   →   x < −4?   ❌

Dividing by −3 flips the sign: x > −4 ✅. Test a value: x = 0 gives 0 < 12 ✅, and 0 is indeed greater than −4. The flip is not a nuisance — it's the number line looking in a mirror.

Mistake 6: Losing the negative root

        x² = 25   →   x = 5?   Half right! ❌

(−5)² = 25 as well: x = ±5 ✅. Solving x² = k produces two roots — the √ symbol alone only hands you the positive one.

Lesson 11: Review — The Big Picture
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Everything, one last time:

        Equations: undo the last move first — same operation on BOTH sides
        Evaluate: substitute with (parentheses), then PEMDAS; f(x) is a named rule
        Exponents: × add, ÷ subtract, power-of-power multiply, ⁰ is 1, ⁻ⁿ flips to a fraction
        Properties: distribute over every term; combine only like terms; factor = undistribute
        Negatives: same signs → +, different signs → −; |x| is distance from 0
        Fractions: ÷ a fraction = × its flip; clear equations with a common denominator
        Systems: substitute or eliminate; inequalities: flip for negative multipliers
        Quadratics: x² = k gives ±; factor by product-sum hunting

The recap list

  · Solve by undoing in reverse order: 3x + 4 = 19 → 3x = 15 → x = 5. Then check by substitution — always.
  · f(x) = 2x + 3 turns "evaluate" into a verb: f(4) = 11, and f(x) = 13 backwards gives x = 5.
  · Exponent laws are copy-counting: 2³ × 2² = 2⁵, (x²)³ = x⁶, 2⁰ = 1, 2⁻³ = 1/8.
  · Distribute to every term (−2(x − 3) = −2x + 6), combine only like terms, and factor by pulling out the GCF: 12x + 18 = 6(2x + 3).
  · Sign rules have REASONS: (−2)(−3) = 6 because the pattern and the debts both say so.
  · Clear fractions early: x/2 + x/3 = 5 → 3x + 2x = 30 → x = 6.
  · Variables on both sides: gather, then undo. Two mysteries: substitute or eliminate.
  · Inequalities: same moves, but a negative multiplier flips the sign — verify with a test value.
  · Quadratics answer twice: x² = 49 → ±7, and factoring turns x² + 5x + 6 = 0 into (x + 2)(x + 3) = 0.

The magic sentence

        Balance every move, undo in reverse,
        exponents are copies, and minus can flip —
        check your answer, and the math must confess.

Say it out loud three times. Seriously!

Why this matters

This lesson was called arithmetic, but you just did algebra — real algebra, the honors kind: equations, functions, exponent laws, systems, inequalities, and a first taste of quadratics. Notice that no new numbers were needed: fractions, negatives, and powers were enough. The new ingredient was the mystery number x and the moves for cornering it. From here, every math course you take — Geometry, Algebra II, Precalculus — is built from these exact moves stretched over bigger canvases. You didn't just learn a lesson; you learned the language.
