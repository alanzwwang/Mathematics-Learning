Grab a pencil and paper. Start with the easy ones — they use the exact same patterns from the lessons. A few problems are open-ended: they have more than one right answer.

Hint for every problem: translate! Turn the log into a power sentence — "BASE to the POWER equals TARGET" — and the answer usually appears.

Problems 85–87 — Build with the rule! Real logs are decimals — here are two real ones to work with.

  • 85.
    Scientists know log₂(3) ≈ 1.6. Use the multiplication rule on 24 = 8 × 3
    to find log₂(24).
  • 86.
    And log(2) ≈ 0.3. Use it twice to find log(4), and three times to find log(8).
  • 87.
    Fill in the blank with a whole number: log₂(40) = ___ + log₂(5).
    (Hint: 40 = 8 × 5.)

Problems 88–90 — Logs in the wild! (Lesson 6)

  • 88.
    A bacteria colony doubles every hour. After how many hours is it 64 times
    as big?
  • 89.
    How many times stronger is a magnitude 5 earthquake than a magnitude 3?
  • 90.
    A ball bounces to half its previous height each bounce. After how many
    bounces is it at 1/16 of its starting height?

🔴 CHALLENGE (Problems 91–100)

  • 91.
    Down the ladder! Find log₂(½), log₂(⅛), and log(1/100).
    (Hint: each step down divides by the base — and the log drops by 1.)
  • 92.
    Nested logs! Work from the inside out:
    a) log₂(log₂(16)) b) log₃(log₂(8))
  • 93.
    The power rule, two ways: find log₂(4³) by first computing 4³, then check
    with the rule log(Aⁿ) = n × log(A). Do the same for log(100²).
  • 94.
    Open-ended! The number 64 gives whole-number logs in MANY different bases.
    Find three of them (three different bases where log of 64 is a whole number),
    and prove each one works.
  • 95.
    Pass the mark! Start with 1 and keep doubling: 2, 4, 8, ... How many
    doublings does it take to pass 100?
  • 96.
    A different Petri dish starts with 3 blobs and doubles every hour. After how
    many WHOLE hours is it past 50 blobs? (List the blobs hour by hour: 6, 12, ...)
  • 97.
    Earthquake deluxe! a) How many times stronger is a magnitude 6 quake than a
    magnitude 4? b) And magnitude 6 versus magnitude 2? Write part b as a power
    of 10.
  • 98.
    Sandwich plus rule! a) Between which two whole numbers does log₂(12) sit?
    b) Fill in the blank with a whole number: log₂(12) = 2 + log₂(___).
    c) Explain in one sentence why parts a and b agree with each other.
  • 99.
    Open-ended! Write three different log equations — with three different
    bases — that ALL equal 3. (One of them should use the invisible base!)
  • 100.
    The grand finale — design it! Invent your own word problem whose answer is
    log₂(32) = 5 (a doubling story), AND another whose answer is log(100,000) = 5
    (a zeros story). Then solve them to make sure they work!

That's the math that ran science for 350 years and still measures the planet today. The next time you hear a magnitude on the news or watch something double and double again, smile: you think like a mathematician now. Great work!
