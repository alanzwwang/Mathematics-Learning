Lesson 1: The Backwards Question — What a Logarithm Really Is
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        A logarithm asks one question: WHAT POWER?
        log₂(8) = 3   because   2³ = 8

The forwards question (you already know)

You've been answering power questions for years: what is 2³? Three 2s multiplied: 2 × 2 × 2 = 8. What is 3²? That's 3 × 3 = 9. What is 5³? That's 5 × 5 × 5 = 125. The question "what is 2³?" starts with the power and asks for the result. Call it the forwards question.

The backwards question

Now flip it around. Suppose I tell you the result — 8 — and the number being multiplied — 2 — and ask: how many times do you multiply 2 to get 8?

        2 × 2 × 2 = 8     ←  three 2s!

The answer is 3. That question — "how many times do I multiply?" or "what power?" — IS a logarithm. We write it like this:

        log₂(8) = 3

Read it out loud as: "log, base 2, of 8 equals 3." The little 2 is the number being multiplied (it's called the base). The 8 is the target. And the answer, 3, is the power — the number of times you multiply the base by itself to hit the target.

        log₂(8) = 3   means exactly the same thing as   2³ = 8

Same fact, two languages. Log language asks "what power?"; power language answers it.

A few more:

        log₂(16) = 4   because   2⁴ = 16   (four 2s: 2×2×2×2 = 16)
        log₃(27) = 3   because   3³ = 27
        log₅(25) = 2   because   5² = 25

Notice the answers are small — 2, 3, 4 — even when the target is big. A logarithm counts multiplications, and multiplications stack up fast. That's the whole flavor of this subject: big numbers, small answers.

Lesson 2: The Two Languages — Translating Logs and Powers
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        Every log sentence is a power sentence in disguise.
        log₂(8) = 3   ⇄   2³ = 8.   Learn to translate both ways!

In Lesson 1 you met the big idea. Now let's make it automatic. Every log sentence has three parts, and each part has a name and a job:

        log₂(8) = 3
             ↑   ↑   ↑
          base target power

  · The BASE is the number being multiplied (the little one, sitting low).
  · The TARGET is the number you're trying to build.
  · The POWER is the answer: how many times to multiply the base.

To translate from log language to power language, use this chant:

        "BASE to the POWER equals TARGET."

        log₂(8) = 3     →     "2 to the 3 equals 8"     →     2³ = 8 ✅
        log₅(25) = 2    →     "5 to the 2 equals 25"    →     5² = 25 ✅

To translate back — from a power sentence to a log sentence — ask where each piece goes. The base stays the base. The power becomes the answer. The target goes inside the parentheses:

        3⁴ = 81     →     log₃(81) = 4 ✅
        2⁶ = 64     →     log₂(64) = 6 ✅

Why bother translating? Because some questions are easy in one language and hard in the other. "What is 2⁵?" — easy in power language (32). "What power of 2 gives 32?" — easy in log language (log₂(32) = 5). A good mathematician switches languages without thinking, like a bilingual kid switching between English and Spanish.

One more piece of vocabulary: the power is also called the EXPONENT. So "what power?" and "what exponent?" are the same question. In fact, some people define it that way: a logarithm IS an exponent!

✏️ Your Turn

Translate into log language: c) 2⁵ = 32. d) 10² = 100.

Answers: a) 3³ = 27. b) 7² = 49. c) log₂(32) = 5. d) log₁₀(100) = 2 — and Lesson 3 shows why we usually write that one WITHOUT the little 10!

Lesson 3: Base 10 — The Log That Counts Zeros
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        When no base is written, the base is 10.
        log(1,000) = 3  —  the answer is just the number of zeros!

The invisible base

Look at this log: log(100). Something's missing — the little base! When a mathematician writes "log" with no base, the base is secretly 10. It's like a nickname so popular that nobody uses the full name. (Why 10? Because our whole number system is built on 10s: ten ones make a ten, ten tens make a hundred...)

So log(100) really means log₁₀(100). And here is why base 10 is the friendliest base of all — its powers are just a 1 followed by zeros:

        10¹ = 10           log(10) = 1
        10² = 100          log(100) = 2
        10³ = 1,000        log(1,000) = 3
        10⁴ = 10,000       log(10,000) = 4

See the pattern? In base 10, the log just counts the zeros. A million has 6 zeros, so log(1,000,000) = 6. No multiplying required!

The sandwich trick

But what about log(400)? Four hundred isn't a clean power of 10. Here's the beautiful part — you can still say a lot:

        100  <  400  <  1,000

Since log(100) = 2 and log(1,000) = 3, the answer must be sandwiched between 2 and 3. (A calculator says about 2.6 — and sure enough, 400 sits between 100 and 1,000, closer to 100.) You don't always need the exact answer. "Between 2 and 3" is often plenty — and it proves you understand what a log IS.

        log₂(10) is between 3 and 4   (because 8 < 10 < 16)
        log₃(50) is between 3 and 4   (because 27 < 50 < 81)

✏️ Your Turn

a) log(100,000) = ?   b) log(1) = ?  (How many zeros does 1 have?)
c) Between which two whole numbers does log(7,500) sit?

Answers: a) 5 — count the zeros! b) 0 — a plain 1 has no zeros at all (and 10⁰ = 1, which Lesson 5 explains). c) Between 3 and 4, because 1,000 < 7,500 < 10,000.

Lesson 4: The Superpower — Multiplication Becomes Addition
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        log(A × B) = log(A) + log(B)     multiplication → addition
        log(A ÷ B) = log(A) − log(B)     division → subtraction

Watch this

Multiply two powers of 10: 100 × 1,000 = 100,000. Now look at their logs:

        log(100) = 2          log(1,000) = 3          log(100,000) = 5

The logs ADDED: 2 + 3 = 5! The multiplication of big numbers turned into the addition of small ones. It's not a coincidence — it works every time, in every base. Try base 2:

        8 × 4 = 32
        log₂(8) = 3     log₂(4) = 2     log₂(32) = 5        and 3 + 2 = 5 ✅

Why it works (the one-sentence reason)

When you multiply powers of the same base, you ADD the exponents: 2³ × 2² = 2⁵ (three 2s times two 2s is five 2s). A logarithm IS the exponent — so of course logs add when numbers multiply!

        log(A × B) = log(A) + log(B)

Division does the opposite — subtracting exponents: 2⁵ ÷ 2² = 2³. So:

        log₂(32 ÷ 4) = log₂(8) = 3          and 5 − 2 = 3 ✅
        log(A ÷ B) = log(A) − log(B)

A word from history

This rule is why logarithms were invented! In 1614, a Scottish mathematician named John Napier published the first log tables. Multiplying giant numbers by hand (astronomers did it daily) took forever, and one slip ruined everything. Napier's trick: look up each number's log in a book, ADD the logs (easy!), then look the answer up backwards in the book. Multiplication became addition. For over 350 years, every scientist and engineer used log tables or a slide rule — a clever ruler with log spacing that adds by sliding. Slide rules designed bridges and airplanes, and even flew to the Moon with the Apollo astronauts. When cheap electronic calculators arrived in the 1970s, the slide rule retired. But logs themselves never did — Lesson 6 shows where they still work today.

The power rule (a bonus gift)

What happens when a number is raised to a power inside a log? Watch: log₂(4³). First, 4³ = 64, and log₂(64) = 6. But also: 4³ = 4 × 4 × 4, so its log is log₂(4) + log₂(4) + log₂(4) = 2 + 2 + 2 = 6 — the exponent just multiplies out front:

        log(Aⁿ) = n × log(A)

Three copies of a number → three copies of its log. The challenge problems will make you use this!

✏️ Your Turn

a) Use the rule to find log₂(8 × 16) — then check by multiplying first.
b) Find log₂(64) − log₂(16), then check with the division rule.

Answers: a) log₂(8) + log₂(16) = 3 + 4 = 7. Check: 8 × 16 = 128 and 2⁷ = 128 ✅. b) 6 − 4 = 2. Check: 64 ÷ 16 = 4 and log₂(4) = 2 ✅.

Lesson 5: The Landmarks — 0, 1, and the Questions You Can't Ask
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Key ideas of this section:

        log of 1 is ALWAYS 0 (in every base).        log₂(1) = 0
        log of the base is ALWAYS 1.                 log₅(5) = 1
        log of 0 (or a negative) does not exist.

Landmark 1: log(1) = 0

What power of 2 gives you 1? Multiply zero 2s! An "empty multiplication" leaves just 1 — that's why mathematicians say 2⁰ = 1. So:

        log₂(1) = 0        log₁₀(1) = 0        log₇(1) = 0

Every base, same answer. Remember Lesson 3's zero-counting: the number 1 has no zeros — and its log is 0. Two routes, same landmark.

Landmark 2: log of the base = 1

What power of 5 gives 5? Just one: 5¹ = 5. So log₅(5) = 1. Always:

        log₂(2) = 1        log₁₀(10) = 1        log₃(3) = 1

The forbidden questions

Can you find log₂(0)? That asks: "what power of 2 equals 0?" Try some: 2¹ = 2, 2² = 4, 2³ = 8... multiplying 2s only makes things bigger — never 0. And 2⁰ = 1, not 0. There is NO answer, so log(0) does not exist. (Calculators say "Error." Now you know why!)

Negative targets are just as impossible: powers of 2 are always positive, so log₂(−8) can't be found either. You can only take logs of numbers bigger than 0.

The bonus ladder: going below zero

Here's a peek at next year's math. The powers of 2 form a ladder — and each step down divides by 2:

        8   = 2³        log₂(8) = 3
        4   = 2²        log₂(4) = 2
        2   = 2¹        log₂(2) = 1
        1   = 2⁰        log₂(1) = 0
        ½   = 2⁻¹       log₂(½) = −1
        ¼   = 2⁻²       log₂(¼) = −2

The ladder doesn't stop at 1 — it keeps going into fractions, and the logs keep counting: past 0 into the negatives. So log₂(½) = −1. Don't worry if this feels weird — it's a challenge-problem bonus. The ladder is all you need: each step down, divide by 2 and subtract 1 from the log.

✏️ Your Turn

a) log₉(1)      b) log₉(9)      c) log(1,000) − log(1)      d) (ladder bonus) log₂(⅛)

Answers: a) 0 — always! b) 1 — always! c) 3 − 0 = 3. d) −3: three steps down from 1 (½, ¼, ⅛), each dividing by 2.

Lesson 6: Logs in the Wild — Doubling and the Earthquake Scale
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Key idea of this section:

        "How many times did it double?" IS a base-2 log.
        The earthquake scale runs on base 10: +1 magnitude = ×10 the shaking.

The doubling detective

A Petri dish of bacteria doubles every hour: one blob, then 2 blobs, 4, 8, 16... After how many hours is there 64 times as much?

        "64 times as much, doubling each hour"
            = "how many 2s make 64?"
            = log₂(64) = 6 hours

Any question about doubling — money doubling, a rumor reaching twice as many people each day, folded paper doubling in thickness — is secretly log base 2. And a question about halving (a bouncing ball reaching half its height each bounce) is the same log walked DOWN the ladder: 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16 — four bounces, because 16 = 2⁴.

The earthquake scale

Scientists measure earthquakes on a magnitude scale where each whole step up means 10 times the shaking. A magnitude 5 quake shakes 10 times harder than a magnitude 4. Magnitude 6 versus magnitude 4? Two steps: 10 × 10 = 100 times the shaking.

        steps apart = log(how many times stronger)

A quake 1,000 times stronger than another is 3 steps up, because log(1,000) = 3. The scale squishes enormous differences into friendly numbers — and that is exactly what logs are FOR. (Sound volume in decibels and acidity in pH work the same way: every step multiplies by 10, so the scale is a log in disguise.)

✏️ Your Turn

a) A rumor doubles in reach every day. How many days until it reaches 32 times as many people?
b) How many times stronger is a magnitude 7 quake than a magnitude 5?

Answers: a) log₂(32) = 5 days. b) Two steps: 10 × 10 = 100 times stronger.

Lesson 7: Watch Out! Common Mistakes
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Keep the question in sight:

        A logarithm asks "WHAT POWER?" — nothing else.

Mistake 1: Dividing instead of finding the power

Asked for log₂(8), the most common wrong answer on Earth is 4 — because "8 divided by 2 is 4!" But logs don't divide. They ask "what power?" Since 2³ = 8, the answer is 3. When you catch yourself dividing, stop and chant: WHAT POWER?

Mistake 2: Scrambling the three parts

log₂(8) = 3 means 2³ = 8 — NOT 8³ = 2, and NOT 3² = 8. The base sits low, the power is the answer, and the target waits inside the parentheses. Chant the translation: "BASE to the POWER equals TARGET."

Mistake 3: Thinking log(1) = 1

It feels like 1 should map to 1, but log(1) = 0 — always, in every base. (How many zeros does 1 have? None!) It's log of the BASE that equals 1: log₅(5) = 1.

Mistake 4: Adding logs when the numbers add

The rule turns multiplication into addition — not addition into addition! log(2 + 3) is log(5), which has nothing to do with log(2) + log(3). The rule only works one way: numbers multiply → logs add. If the numbers add, the rule stays in its toolbox.

Mistake 5: Expecting every log to be a whole number

log₂(10) isn't 5 — it isn't any whole number. It sits between 3 and 4 (since 8 < 10 < 16). Whole-number answers only happen at exact powers: 8, 16, 32... In between, logs are decimals. Being able to sandwich them (Lesson 3) is what proves you understand!

Mistake 6: Forgetting the invisible base 10

log(1,000) with nothing sitting low means base 10 — answer 3. Don't let the missing base fool you into thinking the question is broken. It's the most common log of all, working incognito.

Lesson 8: Review — The Big Picture
─────────────────────────────────────────────

📌 Everything, one last time:

        A logarithm asks "WHAT POWER?"      log₂(8) = 3  because  2³ = 8
        No base written → base 10 → count the zeros.    log(1,000) = 3
        Multiply → logs ADD.   Divide → logs SUBTRACT.
        log(1) = 0 always.   log of the base = 1 always.
        "How many doublings?" = log base 2.   Earthquake steps = log base 10.

The recap list

  · The three parts: BASE (the number being multiplied), TARGET (what you're building), POWER (the answer — how many times).
  · Translation chant: "BASE to the POWER equals TARGET."   log₅(125) = 3  ⇄  5³ = 125
  · Base 10 counts zeros: log(100,000) = 5. In-between targets get sandwiched: log(400) is between 2 and 3.
  · The superpower: log(A × B) = log(A) + log(B), because multiplying powers adds exponents. Napier built the world's calculator on this in 1614.
  · Landmarks: log(1) = 0, log(base) = 1, and log(0) doesn't exist — no power lands on 0.
  · The ladder keeps going: 8, 4, 2, 1, ½, ¼ — and the logs count 3, 2, 1, 0, −1, −2.

The magic sentence

        A logarithm is a question: WHAT POWER?
        Base ten? Count the zeros.
        Multiplying turns to adding —
        and log of 1 is 0.

Say it out loud three times. Seriously!

Why this matters

You now speak two languages — powers and logs — and can switch between them on command. You can estimate logs nobody memorized, split products into sums the way astronomers did for 350 years, and read the earthquake scale like a scientist. And there's a door open ahead: in algebra class, logs are the tool that solves equations where the unknown sits up in the exponent — like "when will my savings double?" You'll be ready.
