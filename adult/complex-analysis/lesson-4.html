Power Series and Analytic Functions

Key idea of this section

∑ aₙ (z − a)ⁿ converges absolutely on D(a, R) and diverges for |z − a| > R,
where 1/R = limsup |aₙ|1/n   (Cauchy–Hadamard).
Inside the disc of convergence the sum is holomorphic,
may be differentiated term by term, and aₙ = f⁽ⁿ⁾(a)/n!.

The radius of convergence

Theorem 4.1 (Cauchy–Hadamard). Given complex coefficients aₙ, let 1/R = limsup |aₙ|1/n, with the conventions 1/0 = ∞ and 1/∞ = 0. Then ∑ aₙ zⁿ converges absolutely for |z| < R, uniformly on every closed disc |z| ≤ ρ < R, and diverges for |z| > R.

Proof. Let ρ < r < R. By the definition of limsup, |aₙ|1/n < 1/r for all large n, so |aₙ zⁿ| ≤ (ρ/r)ⁿ for |z| ≤ ρ and n large. The Weierstrass M-test against the convergent geometric series ∑ (ρ/r)ⁿ gives absolute and uniform convergence on |z| ≤ ρ. If |z| > R, then |aₙ|1/n > 1/|z| for infinitely many n, so |aₙ zⁿ| > 1 infinitely often, the terms do not tend to 0, and the series diverges.

On the circle |z| = R anything can happen: ∑ zⁿ diverges at every point of |z| = 1, ∑ zⁿ/n² converges at every point, and ∑ zⁿ/n converges at every point except z = 1 (by Dirichlet's test). When the ratio |aₙ₊₁/aₙ| has a limit L, that limit equals 1/R, but the root form is the general one; for ∑ zn² only the root test applies, and R = 1.

Example. For ∑ (2ⁿ + 3ⁿ) zⁿ, |aₙ|1/n = 3 (1 + (2/3)ⁿ)1/n → 3, so R = 1/3.

Term-by-term differentiation

Theorem 4.2. If f(z) = ∑ aₙ zⁿ has radius R > 0, then f is holomorphic on D(0, R) and f′(z) = ∑ n aₙ zn−1, a series with the same radius R.

Proof. Since n1/n → 1, limsup |n aₙ|1/n = limsup |aₙ|1/n, so g(z) = ∑ n aₙ zn−1 has radius R. Fix z₀ with |z₀| < ρ < R and let 0 < |h| < ρ − |z₀|. For each N write f = SN + EN, the partial sum up to degree N and the tail. Then

(f(z₀ + h) − f(z₀))/h − g(z₀) = [(SN(z₀ + h) − SN(z₀))/h − SN′(z₀)] + [SN′(z₀) − g(z₀)] + (EN(z₀ + h) − EN(z₀))/h.

Using aⁿ − bⁿ = (a − b)(an−1 + an−2 b + … + bn−1), the last term is at most ∑ over n > N of |aₙ| n ρn−1, the tail of a convergent series; given ε > 0 choose N so large that it and the middle term are each below ε. For this fixed N the first bracket tends to 0 as h → 0, because SN is a polynomial. Hence the whole expression is below 3ε for small |h|.

Iterating, f has derivatives of all orders on D(0, R), and evaluating the k-th derivative at 0 gives ak = f⁽ᵏ⁾(0)/k!. Consequently the power series representing a function about a point is unique. A function on an open set U is called analytic if around every point of U it is given by a convergent power series. Theorem 4.2 shows that analytic functions are holomorphic; the deep converse, proved in Lesson 7, is that holomorphic functions are analytic.

The elementary transcendental functions

The series ez = ∑ zⁿ/n!, cos z = ∑ (−1)ⁿ z2n/(2n)! and sin z = ∑ (−1)ⁿ z2n+1/(2n+1)! have infinite radius and define entire functions. Term-by-term differentiation gives (ez)′ = ez; the Cauchy product of two absolutely convergent series together with the binomial theorem gives ez+w = ez ew, and so ∑ zⁿ/n! agrees with the definition ex (cos y + i sin y) of Lesson 2. The identities

cos z = (eiz + e−iz)/2,   sin z = (eiz − e−iz)/(2i)

hold for all complex z, and show that the complex sine is unbounded: sin(iy) = i sinh y. This is not an accident; by Liouville's theorem (Lesson 7) every bounded entire function is constant.

Why real calculus cannot see radii of convergence

The function 1/(1 + x²) is real analytic on all of ℝ, yet its Maclaurin series 1 − x² + x⁴ − … converges only for |x| < 1. Real analysis offers no explanation. Complex analysis does: 1/(1 + z²) has poles at ±i, at distance 1 from the origin, and we shall prove that the Taylor series of a holomorphic function at a converges on the largest disc about a on which the function is holomorphic. The radius of convergence is therefore the distance to the nearest singularity in the complex plane.

Exercise

  • (a)
    Find the radius of convergence of ∑ n³ zⁿ/5ⁿ.
  • (b)
    Find the radius of convergence of ∑ zn!, whose coefficient aₖ is 1 if k is a factorial and 0 otherwise.
  • (c)
    Assuming the distance-to-the-nearest-singularity principle, find the radius of convergence of the Taylor series of 1/(z² − 2z + 5) about 0.
Show solution

Answers: (a) |aₙ|1/n = n3/n/5 → 1/5, so R = 5. (b) The nonzero coefficients all equal 1, so limsup |aₖ|1/k = 1 and R = 1. (c) The poles are the roots 1 ± 2i of z² − 2z + 5, at distance √5 from 0, so R = √5.
