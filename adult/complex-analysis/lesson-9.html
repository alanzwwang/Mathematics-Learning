The Residue Theorem

Lesson 9 of 14

Overview1234567891011121314ProblemsAssessment

Key idea of this section

If f is holomorphic on U except at isolated singularities a₁, …, aₖ,
and γ is a closed path in U \ {a₁, …, aₖ} with n(γ, z) = 0 for all z ∉ U, then
      ∮γ f(z) dz = 2πi ∑ n(γ, aⱼ) Res(f, aⱼ).

The theorem

Theorem 9.1 (Cauchy's residue theorem, 1831). Under the hypotheses of the key idea, the integral of f over γ equals 2πi times the sum of the residues, each weighted by the winding number.

Proof. Let Pⱼ(z) be the principal part of f at aⱼ, a series in negative powers of z − aⱼ converging on ℂ \ {aⱼ}. Then g = f − P₁ − … − Pₖ has only removable singularities, so after removing them it is holomorphic on U, and ∮γ g dz = 0 by the homology form of Cauchy's theorem. Integrating each Pⱼ term by term, every power (z − aⱼ)−n with n ≥ 2 has a primitive and contributes nothing, while c₋₁(z − aⱼ)⁻¹ contributes 2πi n(γ, aⱼ) Res(f, aⱼ).

For a positively oriented simple closed curve, such as a circle or the boundary of a rectangle, the winding numbers are 1 for points inside and 0 for points outside, and the formula reduces to 2πi times the sum of the residues inside.

Computing residues

Residues are local, and in practice one rarely computes a full Laurent series. The standard rules are:

  • 1.
    If a is a simple pole, Res(f, a) = lim (z − a) f(z) as z → a.
  • 2.
    If f = g/h with g, h holomorphic at a, g(a) ≠ 0, h(a) = 0 and h′(a) ≠ 0, then Res(f, a) = g(a)/h′(a).
  • 3.
    If a is a pole of order at most m, then Res(f, a) = (1/(m − 1)!) lim dm−1/dzm−1 [(z − a)ᵐ f(z)] as z → a.
  • 4.
    If f has a known Laurent expansion, read off c₋₁; this is the only option at an essential singularity.

Examples. At z = i, the function ez/(z² + 1) has the simple pole with residue ei/(2i) by rule 2. The function 1/(z² + 1)² has a double pole at i, and by rule 3 its residue is the derivative of (z + i)⁻² at i, namely −2(2i)⁻³ = −2/(−8i) = 1/(4i) = −i/4. The function π cot(πz) has a simple pole at every integer n with residue π cos(πn)/(π cos(πn)) = 1, and π/sin(πz) has residue (−1)ⁿ at n; these two facts are the engine of the summation method in Lesson 10. The function e1/z has residue 1 at its essential singularity 0, read off from its Laurent series.

Worked integrals

Example. Compute ∮ over |z| = 3 of ez/(z(z − 2)) dz. Both poles lie inside; Res at 0 is e⁰/(0 − 2) = −1/2 and Res at 2 is e²/2, so the integral is 2πi(e² − 1)/2 = πi(e² − 1).

Example. Compute ∮ over |z| = 2 of z/(z⁴ − 1) dz. The four simple poles ±1, ±i all lie inside. By rule 2 the residue at a pole a is a/(4a³) = 1/(4a²), and the sum over a = 1, −1, i, −i is (1 + 1 − 1 − 1)/4 = 0. So the integral is 0.

The residue at infinity

If f is holomorphic for |z| > R, define Res(f, ∞) = −(1/2πi) ∮ over |z| = ρ of f dz for any ρ > R, equivalently minus the coefficient of 1/z in the Laurent expansion valid near ∞. The minus sign makes the following statement true: if f is holomorphic on ℂ except at finitely many points, the sum of all its residues, including the one at ∞, is zero. This often shortens computations dramatically. In the last example, z/(z⁴ − 1) = z⁻³ + z⁻⁷ + … near ∞ has no 1/z term, so the residue at ∞ is 0 and the integral over any circle enclosing all four poles vanishes without computing a single finite residue. Similarly ∮ over |z| = 2 of z¹⁰/(z¹¹ − 1) dz = 2πi, because z¹⁰/(z¹¹ − 1) = 1/z + z⁻¹² + … near ∞.

Exercise

  • (a)
    Find Res(z/(z² − 3z + 2), 2).
  • (b)
    Find Res(e2z/(z − 1)³, 1).
  • (c)
    Compute ∮ over |z − 2i| = 2 of dz/(z² + 9).
Show solution

Answers: (a) z² − 3z + 2 = (z − 1)(z − 2), so the residue is 2/(2 − 1) = 2. (b) By rule 3 with m = 3, the residue is (1/2) · (d²/dz²) e2z at 1 = (1/2) · 4e² = 2e². (c) The poles are ±3i; only 3i lies inside, since |3i − 2i| = 1 < 2 while |−3i − 2i| = 5. The residue there is 1/(6i), so the integral is 2πi/(6i) = π/3.
