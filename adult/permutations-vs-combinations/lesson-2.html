The Cycle Decomposition

Lesson 2 of 13

Overview12345678910111213ProblemsAssessment

Key idea of this section

Every σ ∈ Sₙ is a product of disjoint cycles, unique up to order.
The cycle type of σ is a partition λ of n; the order of σ is lcm(λ₁, λ₂, …).

Cycles

Definition 2.1. Let a₁, …, aℓ be distinct elements of [n], with ℓ ≥ 1. The cycle (a₁ a₂ ⋯ aℓ) is the permutation sending a₁ ↦ a₂ ↦ ⋯ ↦ aℓ ↦ a₁ and fixing every element not among the aᵢ. Its length is ℓ. A cycle of length 2 is a transposition. Two cycles are disjoint if their underlying sets of moved points are disjoint.

The same cycle has ℓ names: (a₁ a₂ ⋯ aℓ) = (a₂ ⋯ aℓ a₁), and so on by rotation. A 1-cycle (a) is the identity; we write it only when we wish to record fixed points explicitly. Disjoint cycles commute, because each acts as the identity on the points moved by the other.

The decomposition theorem

Theorem 2.2 (Cycle decomposition). Every permutation σ ∈ Sₙ can be written as a product of disjoint cycles. The decomposition is unique up to the order of the factors and the inclusion or omission of 1-cycles.

Proof. Define a relation on [n] by i ∼ j if j = σᵐ(i) for some integer m. This is an equivalence relation: reflexivity uses m = 0, symmetry uses −m, and transitivity adds exponents. Its classes are the orbits of the cyclic subgroup ⟨σ⟩ on [n]. Fix an orbit O and a point i ∈ O. The sequence i, σ(i), σ²(i), … takes values in the finite set O, so some value repeats; let ℓ ≥ 1 be the least positive integer with σℓ(i) = σm(i) for some 0 ≤ m < ℓ. Applying σ⁻ᵐ gives σℓ−m(i) = i, and minimality of ℓ forces m = 0. Thus i, σ(i), …, σℓ−1(i) are distinct and σℓ(i) = i. Every element σᵐ(i) of O equals σr(i) where r is the residue of m modulo ℓ, so O = {i, σ(i), …, σℓ−1(i)}, and σ acts on O as the cycle (i σ(i) ⋯ σℓ−1(i)). Since [n] is the disjoint union of the orbits, σ equals the product of these cycles, one for each orbit. For uniqueness, observe that if σ = γ₁⋯γr is any product of disjoint cycles (including 1-cycles for the fixed points), then the support of each γⱼ is invariant under σ and σ acts on it transitively, so the supports are exactly the orbits of ⟨σ⟩, and on each orbit the cycle is determined by σ itself. ∎

Example 2.3. Let σ = 4 7 1 3 2 8 5 6 in one-line notation. Following 1: 1 ↦ 4 ↦ 3 ↦ 1. Following the least unused point 2: 2 ↦ 7 ↦ 5 ↦ 2. Then 6 ↦ 8 ↦ 6. Hence σ = (1 4 3)(2 7 5)(6 8).

Cycle type and order

Definition 2.4. The cycle type of σ ∈ Sₙ is the multiset of its cycle lengths (including 1-cycles), written as a partition λ = (λ₁ ≥ λ₂ ≥ ⋯ ≥ λr) of n, or in multiplicity notation 1m₁ 2m₂ ⋯ nmₙ, where mᵢ is the number of cycles of length i. The number of cycles is κ(σ) = r = m₁ + ⋯ + mₙ.

Proposition 2.5. The order of σ is the least common multiple of its cycle lengths.

Proof. Write σ = γ₁⋯γr with disjoint cycles of lengths λ₁, …, λr. Because the γⱼ commute, σᵐ = γ₁ᵐ⋯γrᵐ, and because they have disjoint supports, σᵐ is the identity exactly when each γⱼᵐ is. An ℓ-cycle has order ℓ (the proof of Theorem 2.2 shows γm fixes a point of its support only when ℓ divides m). Hence σᵐ = 1 if and only if every λⱼ divides m, that is, if and only if lcm(λ₁, …, λr) divides m. ∎

The permutation of Example 2.3 has cycle type (3, 3, 2) and order lcm(3, 3, 2) = 6. The maximal order of an element of Sₙ is Landau's function g(n) = max lcm(λ) over partitions λ of n; its first values are g(5) = 6, g(6) = 6, g(7) = 12, g(8) = 15, g(9) = 20, g(10) = 30. Landau proved in 1903 that ln g(n) ∼ √(n ln n), so the maximal order grows faster than any polynomial but much more slowly than n!.

Powers and inverses

Proposition 2.6. Let γ be an ℓ-cycle and k an integer, and put d = gcd(ℓ, k). Then γᵏ is a product of d disjoint cycles, each of length ℓ/d.

Proof. Identify the support of γ with ℤ/ℓℤ so that γ acts as x ↦ x + 1. Then γᵏ acts as x ↦ x + k, whose orbits are the cosets of the subgroup generated by k in ℤ/ℓℤ. That subgroup is dℤ/ℓℤ, of order ℓ/d, and it has d cosets. ∎

For example, (1 2 3 4 5 6)⁴ has gcd(6, 4) = 2 cycles of length 3: it sends 1 ↦ 5 ↦ 3 ↦ 1 and 2 ↦ 6 ↦ 4 ↦ 2, so it equals (1 5 3)(2 6 4). The inverse of a cycle is the reversed cycle, (a₁ a₂ ⋯ aℓ)⁻¹ = (aℓ ⋯ a₂ a₁), and the inverse of a product of disjoint cycles is obtained by reversing each cycle. In particular σ and σ⁻¹ always have the same cycle type.

Exercise

  • (a)
    Write σ = 2 3 1 5 4 in cycle notation and find its order.
  • (b)
    Compute (1 2 3 4 5 6)⁴ by Proposition 2.6 and check it directly.
  • (c)
    Find Landau's value g(7) and a permutation in S₇ attaining it.
Show solution

Answers: (a) 1 ↦ 2 ↦ 3 ↦ 1 and 4 ↦ 5 ↦ 4, so σ = (1 2 3)(4 5), of order lcm(3, 2) = 6. (b) d = 2 cycles of length 3: (1 5 3)(2 6 4), since the fourth power adds 4 modulo 6. (c) g(7) = 12, attained by cycle type (4, 3), for example (1 2 3 4)(5 6 7); the other partitions of 7 give lcm at most 10 (cycle type (5, 2)).
