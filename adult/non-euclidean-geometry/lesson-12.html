The Gauss–Bonnet Theorem

Lesson 12 of 13

Overview12345678910111213ProblemsAssessment

Key idea of this section

Local:   ∫∫R K dA + ∫_∂R kg ds + Σ (π − interior angles) = 2π
Triangle: ∫∫Δ K dA = α + β + γ − π
Global:  ∫∫M K dA = 2π χ(M)

Geodesic curvature

For a unit-speed curve c(s) on an oriented surface, the geodesic curvature is kg = ⟨c″, N × c′⟩, the component of acceleration in the surface perpendicular to the curve. Geodesics are exactly the curves with kg = 0. A key lemma is Liouville's formula: in orthogonal coordinates with E = 1, if ψ(s) is the angle from ∂_r to c′(s), then

kg = dψ/ds + (√G)r dθ/ds.

Theorem 12.1 (local Gauss–Bonnet for a geodesic triangle). If Δ is a geodesic triangle with angles α, β, γ lying in a normal coordinate neighbourhood, then ∫∫Δ K dA = α + β + γ − π.

Proof. Use geodesic polar coordinates centred at the vertex A, with the side AB along θ = 0 and the side AC along θ = α. Write the side BC as r = ρ(θ), 0 ≤ θ ≤ α. With dA = √G dr dθ and K√G = −(√G)rr,

∫∫Δ K dA = ∫₀α ∫₀ρ(θ) −(√G)rr dr dθ = ∫₀α [ 1 − (√G)r(ρ(θ), θ) ] dθ,

using (√G)r → 1 as r → 0. Along the geodesic BC Liouville's formula gives dψ = −(√G)r dθ, so the integral equals α + ψ(C) − ψ(B). At B the angle between the outward radial direction and BC is ψ(B) = π − β; at C the angle between ∂_r and the direction of travel equals the interior angle, ψ(C) = γ. Hence ∫∫ K dA = α + γ − (π − β). ∎

For K ≡ 1 this is Girard's theorem, for K ≡ −1 it is Theorem 9.1, and for K ≡ 0 it is Euclid I.32. The general local theorem, for a simply connected region R with piecewise smooth boundary and exterior angles εᵢ at the corners, reads ∫∫R K dA + ∫_∂R kg ds + Σ εᵢ = 2π; its proof combines Green's theorem with Hopf's Umlaufsatz (the tangent of a simple closed curve turns by exactly 2π).

The global theorem

Theorem 12.2 (Gauss–Bonnet). For a compact oriented Riemannian surface M without boundary, ∫∫M K dA = 2πχ(M) = 2π(2 − 2g).

Proof. Triangulate M by small geodesic triangles, with V vertices, E edges and F faces. Summing Theorem 12.1 over the faces, ∫∫M K dA = (sum of all angles) − πF. The angles around each vertex add to 2π, so the sum of all angles is 2πV. Each face has three edges and each edge lies on two faces, so 3F = 2E and πF = 2πE − 2πF. Therefore ∫∫M K dA = 2πV − 2πE + 2πF = 2πχ(M). ∎

The left-hand side is defined by the metric, the right-hand side by topology alone. If M has boundary, ∫∫M K dA + ∫_∂M kg ds = 2πχ(M).

Consequences

  • 1.
    A metric on S² must have positive curvature somewhere; a metric on the torus with K ≥ 0 is flat; a surface of genus g ≥ 2 must have negative curvature somewhere.
  • 2.
    A closed surface of constant curvature K = −1 and genus g has area 4π(g − 1); constant curvature +1 forces the sphere (area 4π) or ℝP² (area 2π).
  • 3.
    Two geodesics on a surface with K ≤ 0 cannot bound a simply connected digon (it would have ∫∫ K ≤ 0 but angle term positive), so on a simply connected complete surface with K ≤ 0 geodesics are unique between points (a two-dimensional case of the Cartan–Hadamard theorem).
  • 4.
    Holonomy: parallel transport of a vector around the boundary of a simply connected region R rotates it by ∫∫R K dA. Around the latitude circle at latitude φ on the unit sphere the enclosed cap has area 2π(1 − sin φ), so a transported vector rotates by 2π(1 − sin φ), i.e. by −2π sin φ modulo 2π. This is the Foucault pendulum, whose plane of swing turns through 2π sin φ per sidereal day; at Paris (φ ≈ 48.85°) that is about 271° per day, roughly 11.3° per hour.
  • 5.
    Poincaré–Hopf: the indices of a vector field with isolated zeros sum to χ(M); in particular every vector field on S² vanishes somewhere (the hairy ball theorem).

Uniformisation and the trichotomy

Theorem 12.3 (Uniformisation; Poincaré and Koebe, 1907). Every simply connected Riemann surface is biholomorphic to the Riemann sphere, ℂ, or the disk D. Consequently every closed orientable surface carries a metric of constant curvature +1, 0 or −1 in each conformal class, and the sign is that of χ(M).

Thus the three geometries of this course are not three curiosities but the three possible model geometries for surfaces: the sphere for g = 0, the Euclidean plane for g = 1, and the hyperbolic plane for every g ≥ 2 — "most" surfaces are hyperbolic. Thurston's geometrisation programme, completed by Perelman (2003), extends this picture to three dimensions with eight model geometries, of which hyperbolic geometry H³ is again the richest.

Exercise

  • (a)
    A torus of revolution has K > 0 on its outer half and K < 0 on its inner half. What is ∫∫ K dA over the whole torus?
  • (b)
    A geodesic quadrilateral on a surface has angles 100°, 95°, 85° and 90°. What is ∫∫ K dA over it?
  • (c)
    Find the total curvature of a hyperbolic surface of genus 3 and its area if K ≡ −1/4.
Show solution

Answers: (a) 0, since χ(torus) = 0. (b) Splitting along a diagonal into two geodesic triangles, ∫∫ K dA = (sum of angles) − 2π = 370° − 360° = 10° = π/18 ≈ 0.1745. (c) ∫∫ K dA = 2π(2 − 6) = −8π. With K = −1/4, area = −8π/(−1/4) = 32π.
