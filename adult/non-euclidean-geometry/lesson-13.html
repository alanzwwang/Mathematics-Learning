Riemann's lecture

On 10 June 1854, Bernhard Riemann gave his Habilitation lecture "Über die Hypothesen, welche der Geometrie zu Grunde liegen" (published in 1868). He proposed that a space is an n-dimensional manifold whose infinitesimal distance is given by a positive definite quadratic form ds² = Σ gij(x) dxⁱ dxʲ, that curvature should be measured in each two-dimensional direction, and that the geometry of physical space is an empirical question to be settled by physics — anticipating the separation, decisive for relativity, between mathematical geometry and physical space.

Riemannian manifolds

A Riemannian manifold (M, g) is a smooth manifold with a smoothly varying inner product on each tangent space. There is a unique torsion-free connection ∇ compatible with g (the Levi-Civita connection), with Christoffel symbols

Γᵏᵢⱼ = ½ gᵏˡ ( ∂ᵢ gjl + ∂ⱼ gil − ∂ₗ gij ).

Geodesics are the curves with ∇_ẋ ẋ = 0, i.e. ẍᵏ + Γᵏᵢⱼ ẋⁱ ẋʲ = 0; they are the critical points of the energy functional and locally minimise length. For a conformal metric e2φδij one finds Γᵏᵢⱼ = δᵢₖ ∂ⱼφ + δⱼₖ ∂ᵢφ − δᵢⱼ ∂ₖφ; with φ = −ln y this reproduces the Christoffel symbols of Lesson 5. The Riemann curvature tensor R(X, Y)Z = ∇_X∇_Y Z − ∇_Y∇_X Z − ∇[X,Y]Z measures the failure of second covariant derivatives to commute, and the sectional curvature of a 2-plane spanned by X, Y is

sec(X, Y) = ⟨R(X, Y)Y, X⟩ / (|X|²|Y|² − ⟨X, Y⟩²),

which on a surface is the Gaussian curvature. Contractions give the Ricci tensor Rij and the scalar curvature R; on a surface R = 2K.

Space forms

Theorem 13.1 (Killing–Hopf). A complete, simply connected Riemannian n-manifold of constant sectional curvature κ is isometric to the round sphere Sⁿ of radius 1/√κ (κ > 0), to Euclidean space ℝⁿ (κ = 0), or to hyperbolic space Hⁿ scaled to curvature κ (κ < 0). Every complete manifold of constant curvature is a quotient of one of these by a discrete group of isometries acting freely.

Hyperbolic n-space has the same models as H²: the hyperboloid in ℝⁿ˒¹ with isometry group O⁺(n,1), the upper half-space with metric (dx₁² + ⋯ + dxₙ²)/xₙ², and the Poincaré and Klein balls. For n = 3 the orientation-preserving isometries of the upper half-space are the Möbius maps of the Riemann sphere, PSL(2,ℂ), acting on its boundary; discrete subgroups are Kleinian groups. Mostow rigidity (1968) asserts that for n ≥ 3 a closed hyperbolic manifold is determined up to isometry by its fundamental group — in sharp contrast with the 6g − 6 dimensional Teichmüller spaces of surfaces.

Geodesic deviation

Let γs(t) be a family of unit-speed geodesics and J = ∂γ/∂s the variation field (a Jacobi field), orthogonal to the geodesics. On a surface of constant curvature K, its length satisfies the Jacobi equation J″ + K J = 0, so with J(0) = 0, J′(0) = 1:

J(t) = sin(√K t)/√K   (K > 0),      J(t) = t   (K = 0),      J(t) = sinh(√−K t)/√−K   (K < 0).

Nearby geodesics refocus on the sphere (conjugate points at distance π/√K), separate linearly in the plane, and diverge exponentially in the hyperbolic plane. Exponential divergence is the source of the chaotic (Anosov, mixing, ergodic) behaviour of the geodesic flow on compact hyperbolic surfaces, proved by Hopf (1939) and Anosov (1967) — the mathematical prototype of deterministic chaos.

General relativity

In Einstein's general theory of relativity (1915), spacetime is a four-dimensional Lorentzian manifold with metric of signature (−, +, +, +); freely falling bodies follow timelike geodesics and light follows null geodesics. Gravitation is not a force but curvature, and tidal acceleration is literally the geodesic deviation equation D²J/dt² = −R(J, u)u. The metric is determined by the matter content through the Einstein field equations

Rμν − ½ R gμν + Λ gμν = (8πG/c⁴) Tμν.

Outside a spherical mass M the solution is the Schwarzschild metric:

ds² = −(1 − rs/r) c²dt² + dr²/(1 − rs/r) + r²(dθ² + sin²θ dφ²),     rs = 2GM/c².

Its predictions include the deflection of light passing a mass at impact parameter b by the angle 4GM/(c²b), which for a ray grazing the Sun is about 1.75 arcseconds (confirmed, within the large uncertainties of the time, by the eclipse expeditions of 1919), the anomalous perihelion precession of Mercury of about 43 arcseconds per century, and the gravitational time dilation that must be corrected for in satellite navigation systems (about 38 microseconds per day for GPS clocks, the net of +45 μs from weaker gravity and −7 μs from orbital speed).

The geometry of the universe

The Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker metrics, describing a homogeneous isotropic universe, have the form ds² = −c²dt² + a(t)² dΣ², where dΣ² is a metric of constant curvature k = +1, 0 or −1 on three-dimensional space: spherical, Euclidean or hyperbolic. The three geometries of this course are thus the three candidate geometries for space at cosmological scales. Measurements of the cosmic microwave background (Planck collaboration, 2018), combined with baryon acoustic oscillation data, give a spatial curvature density parameter ΩK = 0.001 ± 0.002, consistent with flat space; a hyperbolic or spherical universe with a radius of curvature much larger than the observable universe cannot be excluded. The question Gauss is said to have tested by triangulating three mountain peaks, and which Lobachevsky proposed to test with stellar parallaxes, is still an empirical one.

Exercise

  • (a)
    Two geodesics on the unit sphere leave a point at angle 0.01 rad. How far apart are they (approximately) after travelling π/2? After π?
  • (b)
    Repeat (a) in the hyperbolic plane for a travelled distance of 10.
  • (c)
    Compute the Schwarzschild radius of the Sun (M = 1.989 × 10³⁰ kg, G = 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻², c = 2.998 × 10⁸ m/s).
Show solution

Answers: (a) Separation ≈ 0.01 · sin t: after π/2 it is 0.01, after π it is 0 (the antipodal point is conjugate). (b) 0.01 · sinh 10 ≈ 0.01 · 11013.2 ≈ 110.1 — by then the linear approximation is meaningless; the point is that the separation grows like et. (c) rs = 2GM/c² = 2 · 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ · 1.989 × 10³⁰ / (2.998 × 10⁸)² ≈ 2.655 × 10²⁰ / 8.988 × 10¹⁶ ≈ 2954 m, about 2.95 km.
