Key idea of this section

[n]q = 1 + q + ⋯ + qⁿ⁻¹,   [n k]q = [n]q! / ([k]q! [n − k]q!)
[n k]q = ∑ over 0-1 words with k ones of qinv(w) = #{k-dimensional subspaces of Fqⁿ}
∏i=0n−1 (1 + qⁱx) = ∑ₖ qk(k−1)/2 [n k]q xᵏ

Definition and the two q-Pascal rules

For an indeterminate q, put [n]q = 1 + q + ⋯ + qⁿ⁻¹ = (1 − qⁿ)/(1 − q), [n]q! = [1]q [2]q ⋯ [n]q, and for 0 ≤ k ≤ n define the Gaussian (or q-binomial) coefficient [n k]q = [n]q!/([k]q! [n − k]q!), with [n k]q = 0 if k < 0 or k > n. At q = 1 it reduces to C(n, k). We usually suppress the subscript q.

Proposition 11.1. For 1 ≤ k ≤ n,

[n k] = [n − 1  k − 1] + qᵏ [n − 1  k]
[n k] = qⁿ⁻ᵏ [n − 1  k − 1] + [n − 1  k]

Proof. Factor out [n − 1]!/([k]! [n − k]!) from the right-hand side of the first rule; what remains is [k] + qᵏ[n − k] = (1 + ⋯ + qᵏ⁻¹) + (qᵏ + ⋯ + qⁿ⁻¹) = [n]. The second rule is the same computation with [n − k] + qⁿ⁻ᵏ[k] = [n]. ∎

By induction on n, [n k] is a polynomial in q of degree k(n − k) with nonnegative integer coefficients, palindromic since [n k] = [n n − k] and the definition is invariant under q ↦ q⁻¹ up to the factor qk(n−k).

[4 2] = 1 + q + 2q² + q³ + q⁴             [5 2] = 1 + q + 2q² + 2q³ + 2q⁴ + q⁵ + q⁶

Combinatorial meaning: inversions and partitions in a box

For a word w ∈ {0, 1}ⁿ, an inversion is a pair i < j with wᵢ = 1 and wⱼ = 0. Theorem 11.2. ∑ qinv(w), over words with k ones and n − k zeros, equals [n k]. Proof. Let W(n, k) be the sum. If the last letter is 1 it is involved in no inversion, contributing W(n − 1, k − 1); if it is 0, each of the k ones forms an inversion with it, contributing qᵏ W(n − 1, k). This is the first q-Pascal rule, and the initial values agree. ∎

Reading a word as a lattice path (1 = step east, 0 = step north), inv(w) is the area of the region between the path and the bottom-right corner of the k × (n − k) box, so [n k] is also the generating function of integer partitions whose Young diagram fits in a k × (n − k) box, counted by size. The coefficients of [5 2] above, 1, 1, 2, 2, 2, 1, 1, count partitions of 0, …, 6 with at most 2 parts, each at most 3.

Subspaces of vector spaces over finite fields

Theorem 11.3. If q is a prime power, the number of k-dimensional subspaces of Fqⁿ is [n k]q.

Proof. An ordered k-tuple of linearly independent vectors can be chosen in (qⁿ − 1)(qⁿ − q)⋯(qⁿ − qᵏ⁻¹) ways, since the ith vector must avoid the span of the previous ones, which has qⁱ⁻¹ elements. Each k-dimensional subspace has (qᵏ − 1)(qᵏ − q)⋯(qᵏ − qᵏ⁻¹) ordered bases. Dividing and cancelling the powers of q gives ∏i=0k−1 (qⁿ⁻ⁱ − 1)/(qᵏ⁻ⁱ − 1) = [n k]q. ∎

[4 2]₂ = (15 · 14)/(3 · 2) = 35        [4 2]₃ = (80 · 78)/(8 · 6) = 130
subspaces of F₂⁴ of all dimensions: 1 + 15 + 35 + 15 + 1 = 67

This is the basis of the slogan that combinatorics of sets is linear algebra over the field with one element: subsets correspond to subspaces, and C(n, k) is the q → 1 limit of [n k]q. The q-Pascal rules count subspaces according to whether they lie in a fixed hyperplane.

The q-binomial theorem

Theorem 11.4 (Cauchy; Rothe). ∏i=0n−1 (1 + qⁱ x) = ∑k=0n qk(k−1)/2 [n k] xᵏ.

Proof. Induction on n; the case n = 0 is trivial. Multiplying the identity for n by (1 + qⁿx), the coefficient of xᵏ becomes qk(k−1)/2[n k] + qⁿ q(k−1)(k−2)/2[n k − 1]. Since (k − 1)(k − 2)/2 + n = k(k − 1)/2 + (n + 1 − k), this equals qk(k−1)/2([n k] + qn+1−k[n k − 1]) = qk(k−1)/2[n + 1 k] by the second q-Pascal rule. ∎

Further results (cited): the q-Vandermonde identity [m + n k] = ∑ⱼ q(m−j)(k−j) [m j][n k − j]; Gauss's evaluation ∑ₖ (−1)ᵏ [n k] = 0 for odd n and (1 − q)(1 − q³)⋯(1 − qⁿ⁻¹) for even n; and the unimodality of the coefficient sequence of [n k], proved by Sylvester (1878) by invariant-theoretic methods (in modern terms, the representation theory of sl₂) and combinatorially by O'Hara (1990). For n = 4, Gauss's sum is 1 − [4 1] + [4 2] − [4 3] + 1 = 1 − q − q³ + q⁴ = (1 − q)(1 − q³).
