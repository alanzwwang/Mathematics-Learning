Classification of Plane Isometries

Lesson 3 of 12

Overview123456789101112ProblemsAssessment

Key idea of this section

Every isometry of the plane is one of: identity, translation, rotation,
reflection, glide reflection — and is a product of at most three reflections.

Reflections generate the group

Theorem 3.1. Every isometry of ℝ² is the composition of at most three reflections.

Proof. Let T be an isometry. If T = id, it is the product of zero reflections. Otherwise pick a point P with T(P) ≠ P and let σ₁ be the reflection in the perpendicular bisector of P and T(P), so σ₁T fixes P. Now we have an isometry fixing a point. If it is not the identity, pick Q with σ₁T(Q) ≠ Q; since σ₁T(P) = P, the points Q and σ₁T(Q) are equidistant from P, so the perpendicular bisector of Q and σ₁T(Q) passes through P, and the reflection σ₂ in it fixes P; then σ₂σ₁T fixes P and Q, hence fixes every point of line PQ. If it is not yet the identity, it is a non-identity isometry fixing a line pointwise, and by Corollary 2.3 it must be the reflection σ₃ in that line (an isometry fixing PQ pointwise and some third non-collinear point is the identity; otherwise it agrees with the reflection on three non-collinear points). In all cases T is the product of at most three reflections. ∎

Products of two reflections

Let σℓ denote the reflection in a line ℓ. If ℓ and m are parallel at distance d, then σm σℓ is the translation by 2d in the direction perpendicular to the lines, from ℓ towards m. If ℓ and m meet at O with directed angle θ from ℓ to m, then σm σℓ is the rotation about O through 2θ. This follows from Sβ Sα = R2(β−α). Conversely, every translation and every rotation can be factored in infinitely many ways as a product of two reflections: for a rotation about O through φ one may take any line ℓ through O and let m be the line through O at angle φ/2 from ℓ. This freedom is the key computational tool.

Composition of rotations. Let ρA be rotation about A through α and ρB rotation about B through β, with A ≠ B. Let n = line AB, choose ℓ through A with ρA = σn σℓ and m through B with ρB = σm σn. Then ρB ρA = σm σn σn σℓ = σm σℓ. If α + β is not a multiple of 2π, the lines ℓ and m meet at a point C and ρB ρA is the rotation about C through α + β; the triangle ABC has angles α/2 at A and β/2 at B. If α + β ≡ 0 (mod 2π), then ℓ ∥ m and the composite is a translation. In particular the composite of two half-turns about A and B is the translation by 2(B − A).

The classification

Theorem 3.2. Every isometry of ℝ² is exactly one of the following.

  • 1.
    The identity (fixes every point).
  • 2.
    A non-trivial translation x ↦ x + v, v ≠ 0 (no fixed point, direct).
  • 3.
    A non-trivial rotation about a point (exactly one fixed point, direct).
  • 4.
    A reflection in a line (fixes exactly a line, opposite).
  • 5.
    A glide reflection: a reflection in a line ℓ followed by a non-zero translation along ℓ (no fixed point, opposite).

Proof. Write T(x) = Ax + b. Suppose first det A = 1, so A = Rθ. If θ ≡ 0, T is a translation (or the identity). If θ ≢ 0, then I − A is invertible (its determinant is 2 − 2cos θ > 0), so T(x) = x has a unique solution c, and T(x) = A(x − c) + c is the rotation about c through θ. Now suppose det A = −1. Then A = Sα is the reflection in a line L₀ through 0 with unit direction u, and has eigenvalues 1 (eigenvector u) and −1 (eigenvector u⊥). Decompose b = su + tu⊥. Then T(x) = Sα x + tu⊥ + su. The map x ↦ Sα x + tu⊥ is the reflection in the line ℓ = L₀ + (t/2)u⊥, because it fixes that line pointwise and is opposite. Hence T is the reflection in ℓ followed by the translation su along ℓ; T is a reflection if s = 0 and a glide reflection if s ≠ 0. The five types are distinguished by orientation and fixed-point sets, so they are mutually exclusive. ∎

The proof gives an effective test. For an opposite isometry T(x) = Ax + b, with u a unit vector fixed by A, the glide component is s = ⟨b, u⟩; T is a reflection if and only if ⟨b, u⟩ = 0, equivalently if and only if T² = id, since T² is the translation by 2su.

Fixed points and conjugacy classes

Conjugation by g ∈ E(2) maps a rotation about c through θ to the rotation about g(c) through ±θ (with the sign − if g is opposite), a translation by v to the translation by Av, a reflection in ℓ to the reflection in g(ℓ), and a glide reflection with axis ℓ and glide length s to one with axis g(ℓ) and the same glide length. Thus the conjugacy classes of E(2) are: the identity; all translations of a given length; rotations through ±θ for fixed θ ∈ (0, π]; all reflections; and glide reflections with a given glide length. In SE(2) rotations through θ and −θ are not conjugate.

Complex form

Identify ℝ² with ℂ. Direct isometries are z ↦ eiθz + b and opposite ones are z ↦ eiθz̄ + b. The rotation about c through θ is z ↦ c + eiθ(z − c). For an opposite isometry, T²(z) = eiθ(e−iθz + b̄) + b = z + eiθb̄ + b, so T is a reflection iff eiθb̄ + b = 0, and otherwise a glide reflection with translation part (eiθb̄ + b)/2.

Exercise

  • (a)
    Let σ₁ be the reflection in the x-axis and σ₂ the reflection in the line y = x. Identify σ₂σ₁.
  • (b)
    Let ρ₀ be the rotation through 90° about (0, 0) and ρ₁ the rotation through 90° about (1, 0). Identify ρ₁ρ₀ and find its fixed point.
  • (c)
    Classify T(x, y) = (−x + 4, y + 3) and T(x, y) = (y + 2, x + 3).
Show solution

Answers: (a) σ₁(x, y) = (x, −y) and σ₂(x, y) = (y, x), so σ₂σ₁(x, y) = (−y, x): the rotation about the origin through +90°, twice the 45° angle from the x-axis to y = x. (b) ρ₀(x, y) = (−y, x) and ρ₁(x, y) = (1 − y, x − 1), so ρ₁ρ₀(x, y) = (1 − x, −y − 1). This is a half-turn (angles add to 180°); solving 1 − x = x and −y − 1 = y gives the centre (1/2, −1/2). (c) The first has A = diag(−1, 1), the reflection in the y-axis, with fixed direction u = (0, 1) and ⟨b, u⟩ = 3 ≠ 0: a glide reflection with axis x = 2 and glide vector (0, 3). The second has A the reflection in y = x, u = (1, 1)/√2, ⟨b, u⟩ = 5/√2 ≠ 0: a glide reflection; T²(x, y) = (x + 5, y + 5), so the glide vector is (5/2, 5/2), and the axis is y = x + 1/2.
