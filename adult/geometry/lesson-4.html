Symmetry Groups, Lattices and the Crystallographic Restriction

Lesson 4 of 12

Key idea of this section

Finite symmetry groups of the plane: cyclic Cₙ or dihedral Dₙ (Leonardo's theorem).
A lattice can only have rotational symmetry of order 1, 2, 3, 4 or 6, since 2cos θ ∈ ℤ.
There are 7 frieze groups and 17 wallpaper groups.

The symmetry group of a figure

For a subset X ⊆ ℝ², its symmetry group is Sym(X) = {g ∈ E(2) : g(X) = X}. The regular n-gon has the dihedral group Dₙ of order 2n, consisting of n rotations about the centre through multiples of 2π/n and n reflections. A pinwheel with n identical bent arms has only the cyclic group Cₙ of rotations. The question of which subgroups of E(2) can occur leads to a beautiful classification.

Leonardo's theorem

Theorem 4.1 (Leonardo da Vinci, Weyl). Every finite subgroup G of E(2) is conjugate to either the cyclic group Cₙ generated by R2π/n, or the dihedral group Dₙ generated by R2π/n and the reflection S₀ in the x-axis.

Proof. First, G has a fixed point. Let x be any point and let c = (1/|G|) ∑g ∈ G g(x) be the centroid of its orbit. Since isometries are affine, they preserve centroids of finite sets (an affine map commutes with weighted averages whose weights sum to 1), so h(c) = (1/|G|) ∑ hg(x) = c for every h ∈ G, because g ↦ hg permutes G. Conjugating by the translation that moves c to 0, we may assume G ⊆ O(2). Let H = G ∩ SO(2), a finite group of rotations. If H is trivial, H = C₁. Otherwise let θ ∈ (0, 2π) be the least positive angle with Rθ ∈ H. For any Rφ ∈ H write φ = kθ + ψ with 0 ≤ ψ < θ; then Rψ = Rφ Rθ−k ∈ H, so ψ = 0 by minimality. Hence H is generated by Rθ, and since Rθ has finite order, θ = 2π/n for n = |H|, so H = Cₙ. If G = H we are done. Otherwise G contains a reflection σ, H has index 2 in G (it is the kernel of det restricted to G), and G = H ∪ σH. Conjugating by a rotation we can make σ the reflection in the x-axis, so G = Dₙ. ∎

The orbit-centroid argument is the simplest instance of a general principle: a compact group acting by affine maps on a convex set has a fixed point, obtained by averaging with respect to Haar measure.

Discrete groups and lattices

A subgroup G of E(2) is discrete if every orbit is a discrete set; equivalently, its translation subgroup TG = G ∩ ℝ² is discrete and its point group (the image of G in O(2)) is finite. A discrete subgroup of ℝ² is 0, an infinite cyclic group ℤv, or a lattice ℤv ⊕ ℤw with v, w linearly independent. A frieze group is a discrete group whose translation subgroup is infinite cyclic; a wallpaper group, or plane crystallographic group, is one whose translation subgroup is a lattice.

Key observation. If g = (b, A) ∈ G and τv ∈ TG, then g τv g⁻¹ = τAv ∈ TG. So the point group preserves the lattice Λ = TG.

Theorem 4.2 (crystallographic restriction). If a rotation Rθ maps a lattice Λ ⊂ ℝ² onto itself, then Rθ has order 1, 2, 3, 4 or 6.

Proof. In a basis v, w of Λ, the linear map Rθ has a matrix M with integer entries, since Rθ v and Rθ w lie in Λ = ℤv ⊕ ℤw. The trace is invariant under change of basis, so 2cos θ = tr Rθ = tr M ∈ ℤ. Since |2cos θ| ≤ 2, we get 2cos θ ∈ {−2, −1, 0, 1, 2}, i.e. θ ∈ {π, 2π/3, π/2, π/3, 0} up to sign, giving orders 2, 3, 4, 6, 1. ∎

In particular no periodic tiling of the plane has five-fold rotational symmetry: 2cos(2π/5) = (√5 − 1)/2 is irrational. The Penrose tilings (1974) have local five-fold symmetry but are aperiodic, and Dan Shechtman's discovery of icosahedral quasicrystals in 1982, recognised by the 2011 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, showed that nature also exploits order without periodicity. The same trace argument in dimension n shows that a finite-order integer matrix has eigenvalues that are roots of unity whose minimal polynomials have degree at most n; for n = 3 the allowed orders are again 1, 2, 3, 4, 6.

Frieze and wallpaper groups

Combining a point group with a compatible lattice, and taking into account glide reflections whose glide part is not a lattice vector, one obtains the classifications. Up to conjugacy by affine maps there are exactly 7 frieze groups and exactly 17 wallpaper groups. The wallpaper classification is due to Evgraf Fedorov (1891) and was rediscovered by George Pólya and Paul Niggli (1924). In crystallographic notation the 17 groups are p1, p2, pm, pg, cm, pmm, pmg, pgg, cmm, p4, p4m, p4g, p3, p3m1, p31m, p6, p6m. Their point groups are the ten groups C₁, C₂, C₃, C₄, C₆, D₁, D₂, D₃, D₄, D₆, exactly the cyclic and dihedral groups allowed by Theorem 4.2. In three dimensions there are 32 crystallographic point groups and 230 space groups (Fedorov and Schoenflies, 1891); if mirror-image pairs are identified the number is 219. Bieberbach's theorems (1910–1912) show in every dimension that the translations in a crystallographic group form a lattice of finite index and that there are only finitely many such groups up to affine conjugacy.

Orbifold notation

John Conway's orbifold notation encodes a wallpaper group by the quotient surface ℝ²/G. Each symbol has a cost: a rotation point of order n (written n) costs (n − 1)/n, a mirror ∗ costs 1, a corner of order n on a mirror costs (n − 1)/(2n), a glide × costs 1, and a handle costs 2. The magic theorem states that the plane groups are exactly the signatures of total cost 2; for example 632 (= p6) costs 5/6 + 2/3 + 1/2 = 2, and ∗442 (= p4m) costs 1 + 3/8 + 3/8 + 1/4 = 2. This is an Euler characteristic computation in disguise: the cost equals 2 − χorb.

Exercise

  • (a)
    Find the order of the symmetry group of a regular hexagon and the number of its elements of order 2.
  • (b)
    Show that the matrix of a rotation through 90° in the lattice basis v = (1, 0), w = (0, 1) has trace 0, and find the matrix of the rotation through 60° in the basis v = (1, 0), w = (1/2, √3/2) of the hexagonal lattice.
  • (c)
    Verify the magic theorem for the signatures 333 and 2222.
Show solution

Answers: (a) D₆ has order 12. Its elements of order 2 are the six reflections and the half-turn, so 7 in total. (b) R90° sends v ↦ w and w ↦ −v, matrix [[0, −1], [1, 0]] with trace 0 = 2cos 90°. R60° sends v ↦ w and w ↦ (−1/2, √3/2) = w − v, so its matrix (columns are images) is [[0, −1], [1, 1]], with trace 1 = 2cos 60°. (c) 333 costs 3 · 2/3 = 2 and 2222 costs 4 · 1/2 = 2; these are p3 and p2.
