Brownian Motion and Random Fractals

Lesson 12 of 13

Key idea of this section

Almost surely: the graph of linear Brownian motion has dimH = dimB = 3/2,
the path of d-dimensional Brownian motion (d ≥ 2) has dimH = 2,
and the zero set of linear Brownian motion has dimH = 1/2.

Brownian motion

A standard linear Brownian motion is a stochastic process (B(t))t≥0 with B(0) = 0, independent increments, B(t) − B(s) normally distributed with mean 0 and variance t − s for s < t, and continuous sample paths (Wiener, 1923). A d-dimensional Brownian motion has d independent linear Brownian motions as coordinates.

Brownian motion is statistically self-similar: for every c > 0, the process c−1/2 B(ct) is again a standard Brownian motion. A random set is not a fixed point of a deterministic IFS, but its law is invariant under rescaling, and the dimension is read off from the scaling exponent: time scales by c, space by c1/2.

Regularity

Theorem 12.1. Almost surely, B is α-Hölder continuous on [0, 1] for every α < 1/2, and for no α > 1/2. Moreover, almost surely B is nowhere differentiable (Paley, Wiener and Zygmund, 1933).

The Hölder statement follows from the Kolmogorov–Chentsov criterion, since E|B(t) − B(s)|2m = Cm |t − s|m for every m; Lévy's modulus of continuity sharpens it to lim suph→0 sup|t−s|≤h |B(t) − B(s)|/√(2h log(1/h)) = 1 almost surely.

Lemma 12.2. If f : [0, 1] → ℝ satisfies |f(t) − f(s)| ≤ c|t − s|α, then the graph Γf has dimB̄ Γf ≤ 2 − α.

Proof. Let 0 < δ < 1 and divide [0, 1] into ⌈1/δ⌉ columns of width δ. Over each column f oscillates by at most cδα, so the part of the graph above that column is covered by at most cδα−1 + 2 squares of side δ. Hence Nδ(Γf) ≤ (1/δ + 1)(cδα−1 + 2) ≤ C δα−2, and the claim follows. ∎

The graph of Brownian motion

Theorem 12.3 (Taylor, 1953). Almost surely, the graph Γ = {(t, B(t)) : 0 ≤ t ≤ 1} has dimH Γ = dimB Γ = 3/2.

Proof. Upper bound: by Theorem 12.1 and Lemma 12.2, dimB̄ Γ ≤ 2 − α for every α < 1/2, so dimH Γ ≤ dimB̄ Γ ≤ 3/2.

Lower bound by the energy method: let μ be the image of Lebesgue measure on [0, 1] under t ↦ (t, B(t)). For 1 < s < 3/2,

E Is(μ) = ∫₀¹∫₀¹ E[ (|t − u|² + |B(t) − B(u)|²)−s/2 ] dt du

With h = |t − u|, B(t) − B(u) has the law of √h Z with Z standard normal, so the integrand is E[(h² + hZ²)−s/2]. Split according to |Z| ≤ √h or not. On the first event the integrand is at most h−s, and the event has probability at most √h; on the second it is at most h−s/2|Z|−s, and E[|Z|−s; |Z| > √h] ≤ C(1 + h(1−s)/2). Hence, for small h,

E[(h² + hZ²)−s/2] ≤ h1/2 − s + C h−s/2 + C h1/2 − s ≤ C′ h1/2 − s

because 1/2 − s < −s/2 when s > 1. The function h1/2−s is integrable over [0, 1]² exactly when s − 1/2 < 1, i.e. s < 3/2. So E Is(μ) < ∞, hence Is(μ) < ∞ almost surely, and dimH Γ ≥ s by Theorem 7.4. Letting s ↑ 3/2 along a sequence gives dimH Γ ≥ 3/2 almost surely. ∎

Brownian paths in higher dimensions

Theorem 12.4 (Lévy; Taylor 1953). For d ≥ 2, almost surely dimH B[0, 1] = 2.

Proof. Upper bound: B is α-Hölder for α < 1/2, so by Theorem 6.3(v), dimH B[0, 1] ≤ (1/α) dimH [0, 1] = 1/α for every α < 1/2. Lower bound: let μ be the occupation measure, the image of Lebesgue measure under B. For s < 2 ≤ d,

E Is(μ) = ∫₀¹∫₀¹ E|B(t) − B(u)|−s dt du = E|Z|−s ∫₀¹∫₀¹ |t − u|−s/2 dt du

where Z is a standard normal vector in ℝᵈ. Here E|Z|−s < ∞ because s < d, and the time integral is finite because s/2 < 1. ∎

So the planar Brownian path has Hausdorff dimension 2 — the dimension of the plane — yet Lévy proved it has Lebesgue measure zero almost surely. The exact gauge function for which the Hausdorff measure is positive and finite is r² log(1/r) log log log(1/r) in the plane (Ray 1963, Taylor 1964) and r² log log(1/r) in dimensions d ≥ 3 (Ciesielski and Taylor, 1962). The outer boundary (frontier) of the planar path has dimension 4/3, as Mandelbrot conjectured from simulations and Lawler, Schramm and Werner proved in 2001 using the Schramm–Loewner evolution; this work contributed to Werner's Fields Medal in 2006.

Zero sets and level sets

The zero set Z = {t ∈ [0, 1] : B(t) = 0} is almost surely a closed, perfect, Lebesgue-null set of Hausdorff dimension 1/2: a random Cantor set. The upper bound comes from counting the dyadic intervals of length 2−k meeting Z, whose expected number is of order 2k/2; the lower bound uses the local time at zero as a mass distribution.

Fractional Brownian motion and Weierstrass functions

Fractional Brownian motion BH with Hurst index H ∈ (0, 1) is the centred Gaussian process with covariance E[BH(t)BH(s)] = (|t|2H + |s|2H − |t − s|2H)/2; H = 1/2 is ordinary Brownian motion. It is H-self-similar and has stationary increments, which are positively correlated for H > 1/2 and negatively correlated for H < 1/2. The same proofs give, almost surely,

dimH graph(BH) = 2 − H,     dimH BH[0, 1] = min{d, 1/H} in ℝᵈ

Mandelbrot and van Ness (1968) introduced fractional Brownian motion as a model for long-range dependence in hydrology (the Hurst phenomenon in Nile flood data) and finance.

The deterministic analogue is the Weierstrass function

W(t) = ∑k≥0 aᵏ cos(2π bᵏ t),   0 < a < 1,  ab > 1

which is continuous and nowhere differentiable (Weierstrass 1872 under stronger hypotheses; Hardy 1916 in this generality). Its graph has box dimension 2 + log a / log b (Kaplan, Mallet-Paret and Yorke, 1984). That the Hausdorff dimension takes the same value was a long-standing problem: it was proved for many parameters by Barański, Bárány and Romanowska (2014) and for every integer b ≥ 2 by Shen (2018).

Exercise

  • (a)
    What are the dimensions of the graph of fractional Brownian motion with H = 0.3, and of its path in ℝ² and ℝ⁴?
  • (b)
    Compute the box dimension of the graph of W with a = 1/2 and b = 4.
  • (c)
    Show that Lemma 12.2 is not sharp in general by considering f(t) = √t.
  • (d)
    Use the energy method to show that the path of 3-dimensional Brownian motion has dimension at least s for every s < 2, and explain why the argument cannot give more than 2.
Show solution

Answers: (a) Graph: 2 − 0.3 = 1.7. Path in ℝ²: min{2, 1/0.3} = 2. Path in ℝ⁴: min{4, 10/3} = 10/3 ≈ 3.333. (b) 2 + log(1/2)/log 4 = 2 − 1/2 = 3/2. (c) √t is 1/2-Hölder on [0, 1], so Lemma 12.2 gives dimB̄ ≤ 3/2, but its graph is a smooth monotone curve of finite length, of dimension 1. (d) E Is(μ) = E|Z|−s ∫∫|t − u|−s/2 dt du with Z standard normal in ℝ³; E|Z|−s < ∞ for s < 3 and the time integral is finite for s < 2. For s ≥ 2 the time integral diverges, reflecting the true dimension 2 of the path.
