Multifractal Measures

Lesson 13 of 13

Overview12345678910111213ProblemsAssessment

Key idea of this section

For a measure μ, the local dimension α(x) = limr→0 log μ(B(x, r)) / log r varies with x.
The multifractal spectrum f(α) = dim_H{ x : α(x) = α }
is the Legendre transform of the moment scaling exponent τ(q):  f(α) = infq ( qα − τ(q) ).

So far each fractal set has carried a single dimension. Measures supported on fractals — the self-similar measures of the chaos game, harmonic measure on a fractal boundary, the energy dissipation in a turbulent fluid — are typically far less homogeneous: they concentrate with different intensities at different points. Multifractal analysis decomposes the support into level sets of the local intensity and measures each level set's dimension.

Local dimension

For a finite Borel measure μ on ℝⁿ and x in its support, the lower and upper local dimensions are the lim inf and lim sup of log μ(B(x, r))/log r as r → 0; if they agree, the common value is the local dimension dimloc μ(x). A measure is exact-dimensional if the local dimension exists and equals a constant d for μ-almost every x; then d = dimH μ, the infimum of dimH E over sets E of full measure. The mass distribution principle (Lesson 7) is the statement that sets where the lower local dimension is everywhere at least s have dimension at least s; its converse (the "Billingsley lemma") gives the matching upper bound.

The binomial measure

Fix 0 < p < 1/2 and let μp be the self-similar measure for the IFS x ↦ x/2, x ↦ x/2 + 1/2 with probabilities (p, 1 − p). Equivalently, μp gives the dyadic interval I(w) of the binary word w = w₁…wk the mass

μp(I(w)) = pN₀(w) (1 − p)N₁(w)

where N₀ and N₁ count the zeros and ones of w. If x has binary digits with asymptotic frequency η of zeros, then

α(x) = lim (−1/k) log₂ μp(Ik(x)) = −η log₂ p − (1 − η) log₂(1 − p)

and α(x) ranges over [αmin, αmax] = [−log₂(1 − p), −log₂ p]. By the Besicovitch–Eggleston theorem (Theorem 7.5), the set of points with zero-frequency η has dimension H(η) = −η log₂ η − (1 − η) log₂(1 − η). Hence the multifractal spectrum is given parametrically by

α(η) = −η log₂ p − (1 − η) log₂(1 − p),     f(α(η)) = H(η),     0 ≤ η ≤ 1

The maximum f = 1 occurs at η = 1/2 (typical points for Lebesgue measure), and f(α) = α at η = p (typical points for μp itself, which therefore has dimension H(p)).

Moment scaling and the Legendre transform

Define the partition function at scale 2−k by summing over the dyadic intervals of length 2−k:

Sk(q) = ∑|w|=k μp(I(w))q = (pq + (1 − p)q)k

The moment scaling exponent τ(q) is defined by Sk(q) ≈ (2−k)τ(q), so

τ(q) = −log₂( pq + (1 − p)q )

τ is real-analytic, increasing and strictly concave, with τ(0) = −1 (the negative of the dimension of the support) and τ(1) = 0 (total mass 1). Its Legendre transform recovers the spectrum.

Theorem 13.1 (multifractal formalism for self-similar measures; Cawley–Mauldin 1992, Arbeiter–Patzschke 1996). For a self-similar measure satisfying the open set condition, for every α ∈ [αmin, αmax],

f(α) = infq∈ℝ ( qα − τ(q) )

and the infimum is attained at the q with τ′(q) = α.

Heuristic derivation. Group the 2ᵏ intervals according to their local exponent: about 2k f(α) intervals have mass about 2−kα. Then Sk(q) ≈ ∑α 2k f(α) 2−kqα ≈ 2−k infα (qα − f(α)) by Laplace's method, so τ(q) = infα (qα − f(α)); since f is concave, Legendre duality inverts this to f(α) = infq (qα − τ(q)). ∎

Verification for the binomial measure. Differentiate τ:

τ′(q) = −( pq log₂ p + (1 − p)q log₂(1 − p) ) / ( pq + (1 − p)q )

which is α(η) with η = pq/(pq + (1 − p)q). A direct computation shows qτ′(q) − τ(q) = H(η) for this η, confirming Theorem 13.1. At q = 0, η = 1/2 and f = 1; at q = 1, η = p and f = α = H(p); as q → +∞ the larger weight 1 − p dominates, η → 0 and α → αmin = −log₂(1 − p); as q → −∞, η → 1 and α → αmax = −log₂ p, with f → 0 at both ends.

Generalised dimensions

The Rényi (or generalised) dimensions are Dq = τ(q)/(q − 1) for q ≠ 1, with D1 = τ′(1) by continuity. D0 is the box dimension of the support, D1 is the information dimension (the dimension of the measure), and D2 is the correlation dimension, estimated in practice by the Grassberger–Procaccia algorithm (1983) from the fraction of pairs of points at distance less than r. For the binomial measure with p = 1/3:

αmin = log₂(3/2) ≈ 0.5850,   αmax = log₂ 3 ≈ 1.5850
D0 = 1,   D1 = H(1/3) ≈ 0.9183,   D2 = −log₂(5/9) = log₂(9/5) ≈ 0.8480
the peak f = 1 occurs at α = −(1/2) log₂(p(1 − p)) = (1/2) log₂(9/2) ≈ 1.0850

Applications and further theory

Multifractal ideas entered physics through turbulence: Frisch and Parisi (1985) explained the anomalous scaling of velocity structure functions by a spectrum of singularities, and Halsey, Jensen, Kadanoff, Procaccia and Shraiman (1986) introduced the f(α) formalism for strange attractors and diffusion-limited aggregation. In complex analysis, Makarov (1985) proved that harmonic measure on the boundary of any simply connected planar domain has dimension exactly 1, however fractal the boundary — a striking rigidity theorem — while its multifractal spectrum carries deep information about the domain (the universal integral means spectrum, with conjectures of Brennan and Kraetzer still open). In probability, the multifractal analysis of Brownian occupation measures, of Gaussian multiplicative chaos and of Liouville quantum gravity is an active research field.

Exercise

  • (a)
    For p = 1/4, find αmin and αmax.
  • (b)
    For p = 1/4, compute D2 and D1.
  • (c)
    Show that f(α) = 0 at αmin and αmax, and f = 1 at α = −(1/2) log₂(p(1 − p)).
  • (d)
    Explain why τ(0) = −1 for every probability measure on [0, 1] whose support is all of [0, 1].
Show solution

Answers: (a) αmin = −log₂(3/4) = log₂(4/3) ≈ 0.4150 and αmax = −log₂(1/4) = 2. (b) D2 = −log₂(1/16 + 9/16) = −log₂(5/8) = log₂ 1.6 ≈ 0.6781. D1 = H(1/4) = (1/4)·2 + (3/4) log₂(4/3) ≈ 0.5 + 0.3113 = 0.8113. (c) At η = 0 and η = 1 the entropy H(η) is 0, and these give α = −log₂(1 − p) and α = −log₂ p; at η = 1/2, H = 1 and α = −(1/2)(log₂ p + log₂(1 − p)). (d) Sk(0) counts the dyadic intervals of length 2−k with positive mass, which is 2ᵏ when the support is [0, 1]; so 2ᵏ = (2−k)τ(0) gives τ(0) = −1.
